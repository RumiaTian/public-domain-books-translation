## I / 一
===Original===

Earliest Impressions

On the theory that our genuine impulses may be connected with our childish experiences, that one’s bent may be tracked back to that “No-Man’s Land” where character is formless but nevertheless settling into definite lines of future development, I begin this record with some impressions of my childhood.

All of these are directly connected with my father, although of course I recall many experiences apart from him. I was one of the younger members of a large family and an eager participant in the village life, but because my father was so distinctly the dominant influence and because it is quite impossible to set forth all of one’s early impressions, it has seemed simpler to string these first memories on that single cord. Moreover, it was this cord which not only held fast my supreme affections, but also first drew me into the moral concerns of life, and later afforded a clue there to which I somewhat wistfully clung in the intricacy of its mazes.

===Chinese===

最早的印象

基于这样一个想法——我们真正的冲动或许同童年的经历相连，一个人的志向或许可以追溯到那片“无人之境”，在那里性格尚未成形、却已在朝未来发展的明确路线沉淀——我便从童年的几点印象写起这份记录。

这些印象全都同我的父亲直接相关，虽然我自然也记得许多与他无关的经历。我是一个大家庭里年纪较小的孩子，是乡村生活的热心参与者；可是因为父亲毫无疑问是家中压倒一切的影响，也因为要把一个人全部的早期印象尽数写出是绝无可能的，把这些最初的记忆串在同一根绳上，似乎更简单些。何况，正是这根绳，不仅系着我至高的情感，而且头一个把我引进了人生的道德事务，后来在那座迷宫的重重曲折里，又给了我一条我不无怅惘地紧抓不放的线索。

It must have been from a very early period that I recall “horrid nights” when I tossed about in my bed because I had told a lie. I was held in the grip of a miserable dread of death, a double fear, first, that I myself should die in my sins and go straight to that fiery Hell which was never mentioned at home, but which I had heard all about from other children, and, second, that my father﻿—representing the entire adult world which I had basely deceived﻿—should himself die before I had time to tell him. My only method of obtaining relief was to go downstairs to my father’s room and make full confession. The high resolve to do this would push me out of bed and carry me down the stairs without a touch of fear. But at the foot of the stairs I would be faced by the awful necessity of passing the front door﻿—which my father, because of his Quaker tendencies, did not lock﻿—and of crossing the wide and black expanse of the living room in order to reach his door. I would invariably cling to the newel post while I contemplated the perils of the situation, complicated by the fact that the literal first step meant putting my bare foot upon a piece of oilcloth in front of the door, only a few inches wide, but lying straight in my path. I would finally reach my father’s bedside perfectly breathless and having panted out the history of my sin, invariable received the same assurance that if he “had a little girl who told lies,” he was very glad that she “felt too bad to go to sleep afterward.” No absolution was asked for or received, but apparently the sense that the knowledge of my wickedness was shared, or an obscure understanding of the affection which underlay the grave statement, was sufficient, for I always went back to bed as bold as a lion, and slept, if not the sleep of the just, at least that of the comforted.

想是从很早的年纪起，我就记得那些“可怕的夜晚”——因为说了谎，我在床上翻来覆去。我被一种对死亡的悲惨恐惧攥着：是双重的怕——一怕我自己带着罪孽死去，直坠那家里从不提起、我却从别的孩子嘴里听全了的烈火地狱；二怕我的父亲——他代表着我卑鄙地欺骗了的整个成人世界——会在我来得及向他坦白之前就死去。我唯一的解救之法，就是下楼到父亲的房间里彻底忏悔。这样做的宏愿会把我推出被窝，领我走下楼梯，一点不害怕。可一到楼梯脚下，我就得面对可怕的必经之途：先过前门——父亲因为贵格会的脾气，从不锁门——再横穿那间又宽又黑的堂屋，才够得着他的房门。我总要抱住楼梯柱盘算这一路的凶险，而事情更麻烦的是，字面意义上的第一步，意味着把赤脚踩上门前一条只有几英寸宽的油布，而它正横在我的去路上。等我终于气喘吁吁地摸到父亲床边，喘着气倒出我的罪状，得到的保证永远是一个：倘若他“有一个说谎的小姑娘”，他很高兴她“事后难过得睡不着觉”。赦免没有讨，也没有给；可是显然，知道我的坏有人分担了，或者对那严肃话语底下藏着的爱有一种朦昽的领会，也就够了，因为我总是雄赳赳气昂昂地回到床上，睡上一觉——纵然算不得义人的睡眠，至少是被安慰者的睡眠。

I recall an incident which must have occurred before I was seven years old, for the mill in which my father transacted his business that day was closed in 1867. The mill stood in the neighboring town adjacent to its poorest quarter. Before then I had always seen the little city of ten thousand people with the admiring eyes of a country child, and it had never occurred to me that all its streets were not as bewilderingly attractive as the one which contained the glittering toyshop and the confectioner. On that day I had my first sight of the poverty which implies squalor, and felt the curious distinction between the ruddy poverty of the country and that which even a small city presents in its shabbiest streets. I remember launching at my father the pertinent inquiry why people lived in such horrid little houses so close together, and that after receiving his explanation I declared with much firmness when I grew up I should, of course, have a large house, but it would not be built among the other large houses, but right in the midst of horrid little houses like those.

Homestead at Cedarville.

我记得一件事，必定发生在我七岁之前，因为父亲那天办事的那座磨坊1867年就关了。磨坊坐落在邻镇，紧挨着镇上最穷的街区。在那以前，我一直用一个乡下孩子仰慕的眼睛看这座万人小城，从没想过它并非条条街道都同开着闪亮的玩具店和糖果店的那条一样迷人。那一天，我头一回见到了意味着肮脏穷困的那种贫困，觉出了乡下红润的贫穷同一座哪怕很小的城市在最破败的街上呈现的贫穷之间那种古怪的分别。记得我朝父亲发出直截了当的疑问：人们干吗住在这样挤作一堆的可怕小房子里？听完他的解释，我斩钉截铁地宣布：等我长大，我自然也要有一所大房子，可它不盖在别的大房子中间，就盖在这类可怕小房子正当中。

塞达维尔的家宅。

That curious sense of responsibility for carrying on the world’s affairs which little children often exhibit because “the old man clogs our earliest years,” I remember in myself in a very absurd manifestation. I dreamed night after night that everyone in the world was dead excepting myself, and that upon me rested the responsibility of making a wagon wheel. The village street remained as usual, the village blacksmith shop was “all there,” even a glowing fire upon the forge and the anvil in its customary place near the door, but no human being was within sight. They had all gone around the edge of the hill to the village cemetery, and I alone remained alive in the deserted world. I always stood in the same spot in the blacksmith shop, darkly pondering as to how to begin, and never once did I know how, although I fully realized that the affairs of the world could not be resumed until at least one wheel should be made and something started. Every victim of nightmare is, I imagine, overwhelmed by an excessive sense of responsibility and the consciousness of a fearful handicap in the effort to perform what is required; but perhaps never were the odds more heavily against “a warder of the world” than in these reiterated dreams of mine, doubtless compounded in equal parts of a childish version of Robinson Crusoe and of the end-of-the-world predictions of the Second Adventists, a few of whom were found in the village. The next morning would often find me, a delicate little girl of six, with the further disability of a curved spine, standing in the doorway of the village blacksmith shop, anxiously watching the burly, red-shirted figure at work. I would store my mind with such details of the process of making wheels as I could observe, and sometimes I plucked up courage to ask for more. “Do you always have to sizzle the iron in water?” I would ask, thinking how horrid it would be to do. “Sure!” the good-natured blacksmith would reply, “that makes the iron hard.” I would sigh heavily and walk away, bearing my responsibility as best I could, and this of course I confided to no one, for there is something too mysterious in the burden of “the winds that come from the fields of sleep” to be communicated, although it is at the same time too heavy a burden to be borne alone.

小孩子常常显出一种替世界的运转操心负责的古怪心肠——因为“老人堵住了我们最早的岁月”——我记得在我自己身上，这心肠有过一个非常荒唐的表现。我一夜接一夜地梦见世上人人都死了，只剩我一个，造一只马车轮的责任全压在我身上。村街照旧，村里的铁匠铺样样俱全，炉子上火苗正旺，铁砧照惯例摆在门边，可一个人影也看不见。他们全都绕过山坡到村墓地里去了，只留我独自活在被遗弃的世界里。我总是站在铁匠铺里同一个位置上，阴沉沉地盘算该从哪儿下手；我从来不知道该从哪儿下手，虽然我完全明白，世界的事务不重新运转则已，要运转，至少得先造出一只轮子，先有个开动。我想，每一个梦魇的受害者都被过度的责任感和“自知力不从心却必须完成使命”的意识压垮；可是，同“世界的守卫者”作对的赔率，恐怕再没有比在我这些反复重演的梦里更悬殊的了——这些梦，无疑是一半孩童版的《鲁滨逊漂流记》、一半村里那几个基督复临派的末日预言调成的。第二天早晨，常常可以看见我——一个六岁的纤弱小姑娘，外加脊柱弯曲的毛病——站在村铁匠铺门口，焦灼地望着那个穿红衬衫的壮汉做工。我把做轮子过程中能观察到的细节一一存进脑子里，有时还鼓起勇气多问几句。“铁是不是总得在水里滋一下？”我会问，心里想着这活儿干起来多可怕。“那可不！”好脾气的铁匠答道，“那样铁才硬。”我长叹一声，尽力担着我的责任走开。这心事我当然谁也没告诉，因为“从睡眠的田野吹来的风”这样的担子，自有它神秘得说不出口的地方，可它同时又是重得没法独个儿挑的担子。

My great veneration and pride in my father manifested itself in curious ways. On several Sundays, doubtless occurring in two or three different years, the Union Sunday School of the village was visited by strangers, some of those “strange people” who live outside a child’s realm, yet constantly thrill it by their close approach. My father taught the large Bible class in the lefthand corner of the church next to the pulpit, and to my eyes at least, was a most imposing figure in his Sunday frock coat, his fine head rising high above all the others. I imagined that the strangers were filled with admiration for this dignified person, and I prayed with all my heart that the ugly, pigeon-toed little girl, whose crooked back obliged her to walk with her head held very much upon one side, would never be pointed out to these visitors as the daughter of this fine man. In order to lessen the possibility of a connection being made, on these particular Sundays I did not walk beside my father, although this walk was the great event of the week, but attached myself firmly to the side of my Uncle James Addams, in the hope that I should be mistaken for his child, or at least that I should not remain so conspicuously unattached that troublesome questions might identify an Ugly Duckling with her imposing parent. My uncle, who had many children of his own, must have been mildly surprised at this unwonted attention, but he would look down kindly at me, and say, “So you are going to walk with me today?” “Yes, please, Uncle James,” would be my meek reply. He fortunately never explored my motives, nor do I remember that my father ever did, so that in all probability my machinations have been safe from public knowledge until this hour.

我对父亲的崇敬与骄傲，用古怪的方式表达出来。有几个星期日——想来分属两三个不同的年头——村里的联合主日学来了生客，就是那种住在孩子的疆界之外、却又频频贴近得叫孩子心惊的“陌生人”。父亲在教堂里讲坛旁左手角落教那个大型读经班，在我眼里，他穿上星期日的长礼服，那颗漂亮的头颅高出众人一头，实在是顶威严的人物。我想象那些生客对这位气派的人物满怀倾慕，便全心全意地祈祷，千万别有人把这丑陋的、内八字的小姑娘——她弯曲的脊背害她走路时脑袋歪向一边——指给这些来客看，说是这位体面人物的闺女。为了尽量减少人家把这两者连在一起的可能，每逢这种星期日，我不走在父亲身边——虽说同他并肩走是每星期的大事——而是一头贴牢在詹姆斯·亚当斯伯伯身边，指望人家把我错当成他的孩子，或者至少不至于那么显眼地没有归属，招来费事的盘问，把一只丑小鸭同她威严的家长对上号。伯伯自己儿女成群，对这份反常的亲近想必有点纳闷，可他总是和气地低头看看我，说：“今天要跟伯伯走啦？”“是，麻烦您了，詹姆斯伯伯。”我乖乖地答。幸而他从没追究过我的动机，父亲——就我记得——也从来没有；所以我的这些机关，多半直到此刻才算公之于众。

It is hard to account for the manifestations of a child’s adoring affection, so emotional, so irrational, so tangled with the affairs of the imagination. I simply could not endure the thought that “strange people” should know that my handsome father owned this homely little girl. But even in my chivalric desire to protect him from his fate, I was not quite easy in the sacrifice of my uncle, although I quieted my scruples with the reflection that the contrast was less marked and that, anyway, his own little girl “was not so very pretty.” I do not know that I commonly dwelt much upon my personal appearance, save as it thrust itself as an incongruity into my father’s life, and in spite of unending evidence to the contrary, there were even black moments when I allowed myself to speculate as to whether he might not share the feeling. Happily, however, this specter was laid before it had time to grow into a morbid familiar by a very trifling incident. One day I met my father coming out of his bank on the main street of the neighboring city which seemed to me a veritable whirlpool of society and commerce. With a playful touch of exaggeration, he lifted his high and shining silk hat and made me an imposing bow. This distinguished public recognition, this totally unnecessary identification among a mass of “strange people” who couldn’t possibly know unless he himself made the sign, suddenly filled me with a sense of the absurdity of the entire feeling. It may not even then have seemed as absurd as it really was, but at least it seemed enough so to collapse or to pass into the limbo of forgotten specters.

孩子的孺慕之情，那么感情用事，那么不讲道理，同想象的事物缠夹不清，实在难以解释。我一想到“陌生人”会知道我那位英俊的父亲拥有这么个不好看的小姑娘，就简直受不了。可即便在这份护着他不受命运作弄的骑士心肠里，把伯伯搭进去，我也不大心安；我安抚自己的良心说，反正在伯伯那里反差不那么扎眼，何况他自家的小闺女“也不是顶好看”。我不觉得我平日常常惦记自己的模样，除非它作为一件不般配的东西闯进父亲的生活；而且，尽管有源源不断的反面证据，也真有过晦暗的时刻，容我疑心他兴许也怀着同样的感觉。所幸这个鬼魂还来得及长成一个缠身的恶念，就被一件顶小的事送进了坟墓。有一天，我在邻城的大街上遇见父亲从他的银行里出来——那座城在我看来简直是社会与商业的大漩涡。他带着一点玩笑式的夸张，举高那顶锃亮的大礼帽，朝我郑重其事地一躬。这一下有身份的公开相认，这一下全无必要的相认——在一大群“陌生人”中间，他们绝无从知晓，除非他亲手打这个招呼——忽然叫我觉出这整个念头的荒唐。它当时也许还没有实际上那么荒唐，可至少荒唐得够了，就此坍塌，被打发进被遗忘的鬼魂的阴曹地府。

I made still other almost equally grotesque attempts to express this doglike affection. The house at the end of the village in which I was born, and which was my home until I moved to Hull House, in my earliest childhood had opposite to it﻿—only across the road and then across a little stretch of greensward﻿—two mills belonging to my father; one flour mill, to which the various grains were brought by the neighboring farmers, and one sawmill, in which the logs of the native timber were sawed into lumber. The latter offered the great excitement of sitting on a log while it slowly approached the buzzing saw which was cutting it into slabs, and of getting off just in time to escape a sudden and gory death. But the flouring mill was much more beloved. It was full of dusky, floury places which we adored, of empty bins in which we might play house; it had a basement, with piles of bran and shorts which were almost as good as sand to play in, whenever the miller let us wet the edges of the pile with water brought in his sprinkling pot from the millrace.

Mill at Cedarville.

In addition to these fascinations was the association of the mill with my father’s activities, for doubtless at that time I centered upon him all that careful imitation which a little girl ordinarily gives to her mother’s ways and habits. My mother had died when I was a baby and my father’s second marriage did not occur until my eighth year.

为表达这份狗一般的深情，我还做过别的几乎同样古怪的努力。我出生的那座房子在村尾，一直是我的家，直到我搬去赫尔馆（Hull House）为止；我最早的时候，它对面——只隔着一条马路，再隔一小片草皮——立着父亲的两座磨坊：一座磨面坊，邻近的农夫们把各样谷物送到那里；一座锯木坊，本地林木的圆木在那里锯成板材。后者的最大刺激，是坐在一根原木上，随它慢慢逼近那把它锯成板的嗡嗡作响的锯子，然后恰好在猝死和血光之前跳下来。可磨面坊更得宠。它满是幽暗的、扑着面粉的角落，我们爱极了；满是空粮柜，可以在里面过家家；还有一间地下室，麸皮和次粉堆得几乎同沙子一样好玩——只要磨坊主肯让我们用他从磨坊水渠里提来的喷壶水，把堆的边上打湿。

除了这些迷人之处，磨坊还同父亲的活动连在一起；因为毫无疑问，那时我把小姑娘认给母亲的那份细心的模仿，整个儿给了父亲。我母亲在我襁褓中就去世了，父亲续弦是在我八岁那年。

I had a consuming ambition to posses a miller’s thumb, and would sit contentedly for a long time rubbing between my thumb and fingers the ground wheat as it fell from between the millstones, before it was taken up on an endless chain of mysterious little buckets to be bolted into flour. I believe I have never since wanted anything more desperately than I wanted my right thumb to be flattened, as my father’s had become, during his earlier years of a miller’s life. Somewhat discouraged by the slow process of structural modification, I also took measures to secure on the backs of my hands the tiny purple and red spots which are always found on the hands of the miller who dresses millstones. The marks on my father’s hands had grown faint, but were quite visible when looked for, and seemed to me so desirable that they must be procured at all costs. Even when playing in our house or yard, I could always tell when the millstones were being dressed, because the rumbling of the mill then stopped, and there were few pleasures I would not instantly forego, rushing at once to the mill, that I might spread out my hands near the millstones in the hope that the little hard flints flying form the miller’s chisel would light upon their backs and make the longed-for marks. I used hotly to accuse the German miller, my dear friend Ferdinand, “of trying not to hit my hands,” but he scornfully replied that he could not hit them if he did try, and that they were too little to be of use in a mill anyway. Although I hated his teasing, I never had the courage to confess my real purpose.

我有一份火烧火燎的心愿：要长出一根磨坊主的大拇指。磨盘间磨出的麦粒，在被打上传送链那一串神秘小桶拿去筛成面粉之前，我会把它夹在拇指和指头间捻上半天，坐得心满意足。我相信，此后我再没有像盼望我的右手拇指变扁那样热切地盼望过什么——父亲早年做磨坊主，拇指就磨扁了。眼看这番身体改造进展缓慢，我有点泄气，便另想办法，要在我手背上弄到那种紫红小点——给磨盘凿齿的磨坊主手上总有。我父亲手上的印记淡了，可留心看还看得见，在我眼里那样可羡，必须不惜代价弄到手。哪怕在家里院子里玩，我总能知道什么时候在修磨盘，因为那时磨坊的轰隆声停了。几乎没有什么我不肯立刻撇下的乐趣，一溜烟奔到磨坊，把手背摊在磨盘近旁，指望磨匠凿子上迸出的小硬燧石能落在手背上，砸出我朝思暮想的印记。我常常气呼呼地控告那位德国磨匠、我的好朋友费迪南德，说他“存心不砸我的手”，可他不屑地回敬：他就是想砸也砸不着，再说这两只手也太小，在磨坊里压根使不上。我恨他的打趣，却始终没勇气说出我的真实目的。

This sincere tribute of imitation, which affection offers to its adored object, had later, I hope, subtler manifestations, but certainly these first ones were altogether genuine. In this case, too, I doubtless contributed my share to that stream of admiration which our generation so generously poured forth for the self-made man. I was consumed by a wistful desire to apprehend the hardships of my father’s earlier life in that faraway time when he had been a miller’s apprentice. I knew that he still woke up punctually at three o’clock because for so many years he had taken his turn at the mill in the early morning, and if by chance I awoke at the same hour, as curiously enough I often did, I imagined him in the early dawn in my uncle’s old mill reading through the entire village library, book after book, beginning with the lives of the signers of the Declaration of Independence. Copies of the same books, mostly bound in calfskin, were to be found in the library below, and I courageously resolved that I too would read them all and try to understand life as he did. I did in fact later begin a course of reading in the early morning hours, but I was caught by some fantastic notion of chronological order and early legendary form. 有一件事在我心里清清楚楚地凸着，它像一扇令人兴奋的窗，通向道德事业与严肃工作的广大世界；它必定发生得比这更早，因为1872年——我还不满十二岁——一天早晨我走进父亲的房间，见他手拿报纸坐在炉边，神色极为凝重；我急切地问出了什么事，他告诉我：朱塞佩·马志尼去世了。我连马志尼的名字都没听过；听完介绍，我还打算抬杠，一口咬定父亲并不认识他，他不是美国人，我不明白凭什么指望我们为他难过。父亲如何把我那些廉价的论据一一击垮，这段对话已无从追忆，但最终我得到了一样东西——我一向视为宝贵的财富：一种对人与人之间真实纽带的感觉——只要他们怀着同样的宏愿和同样的渴望，纵使国籍、语言、信仰各异，这纽带也真实存在；对美国要废除蓄奴的人、对意大利要挣脱哈布斯堡压迫的人，那一切在他们中间一文不值。总之，我为自己那点贫乏的爱国观念羞愧不已，走出房门时振奋地意识到：超乎个人的、国际间的关系是实实在在的事实，不是空话。我满心骄傲，因为我认识一个同伟大心灵交谈的人，一个真正为海外发生的事悲伤和欢喜的人。我一回想同父亲的这些早期谈话——还有另外几十次类似的谈话——心里就浮起布朗宁夫人的一行诗，做女儿的用它描写她同父亲的关系：——

“他用他那宽大的

男人的罩袍把我裹住，合不合身全不在乎。”
