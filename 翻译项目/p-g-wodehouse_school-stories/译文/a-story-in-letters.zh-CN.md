## L’Affaire Uncle John (A Story in Letters) / 约翰叔叔事件（书信体小说）

### I / 一

===Original===
From Richard Venables, of St. Austin’s School, to his brother Archibald Venables, of King’s College, Cambridge:

> Dear Archie—I take up my pen to write to you, not as one hoping for an answer, but rather in order that (you notice the Thucydidean construction) I may tell you of an event the most important of those that have gone before. You may or may not have heard far-off echoes of my adventure with Uncle John, who has just come back from the diamond-mines—and looks it. It happened thusly:
> Last Wednesday evening I was going through the cricket field to meet Uncle John, at the station, as per esteemed favour from the governor, telling me to. Just as I got on the scene, to my horror, amazement, and disgust, I saw a middle-aged bounder, in loud checks, who, from his looks, might have been anything from a retired pawnbroker to a secondhand butler, sacked from his last place for stealing the sherry, standing in the middle of the field, on the very wicket the Rugborough match is to be played on next Saturday (tomorrow), and digging—*digging*—I’ll trouble you. Excavating great chunks of our best turf with a walking-stick. I was so unnerved, I nearly fainted. It’s bad enough being captain of a School team under any circs, as far as putting you off your game goes, but when you see the wicket you’ve been rolling by day, and dreaming about by night, being mangled by an utter stranger—well! They say a cow is slightly irritated when her calf is taken away from her, but I don’t suppose the most maternal cow that ever lived came anywhere near the frenzy that surged up in my bosom at that moment. I flew up to him, foaming at the mouth. “My dear sir,” I shrieked, “*are* you aware that you’re spoiling the best wicket that has ever been prepared since cricket began?” He looked at me, in a dazed sort of way, and said, “What?” I said: “How on earth do you think we’re going to play Rugborough on a ploughed field?” “I don’t follow, mister,” he replied. A man who calls you “mister” is beyond the pale. You are justified in being a little rude to him. So I said: “Then you must be either drunk or mad, and I trust it’s the latter.” I believe that’s from some book, though I don’t remember which. This did seem to wake him up a bit, but before he could frame his opinion in words, up came Biffen, the ground-man, to have a last look at his wicket before retiring for the night. When he saw the holes—they were about a foot deep, and scattered promiscuously, just where two balls out of three pitch—he almost had hysterics. I gently explained the situation to him, and left him to settle with my friend of the check suit. Biffen was just settling down to a sort of Philippic when I went, and I knew that I had left the man in competent hands. Then I went to the station. The train I had been told to meet was the 5:30. By the way, of course, I didn’t know in the least what Uncle John was like, not having seen him since I was about one-and-a-half, but I had been told to look out for a tall, rather good-looking man. Well, the 5:30 came in all right, but none of the passengers seemed to answer to the description. The ones who were tall were not good looking, and the only man who was good looking stood five feet nothing in his boots. I did ask him if he was Mr. John Dalgliesh; but, his name happening to be Robinson, he could not oblige. I sat out a couple more trains, and then went back to the field. The man had gone, but Biffen was still there. “Was you expecting anyone today, sir?” he asked, as I came up. “Yes. Why?” I said. “That was ’im,” said Biffen. By skilful questioning, I elicited the whole thing. It seems that the fearsome bargee, in checks, was the governor’s “tall, good-looking man”; in other words, Uncle John himself. He had come by the 4:30, I suppose. Anyway, there he was, and I had insulted him badly. Biffen told me that he had asked who I was, and that he (Biffen) had given the information, while he was thinking of something else to say to him about his digging. By the way, I suppose he dug from force of habit. Thought he’d find diamonds, perhaps. When Biffen told him this, he said in a nasty voice: “Then, when he comes back will you have the goodness to tell him that my name is John Dalgliesh, and that he will hear more of this.” And I’m uncommonly afraid I shall. The governor bars Uncle John awfully, I know, but he wanted me to be particularly civil to him, because he was to get me a place in some beastly firm when I leave. I haven’t heard from home yet, but I expect to soon. Still, I’d like to know how I could stand and watch him ruining the wicket for our spot match of the season. As it is, it won’t be as good as it would have been. The Rugborough slow man will be unplayable if he can find one of these spots. Altogether, it’s a beastly business. Write soon, though I know you won’t.
> —Yours ever,
> Dick

===Chinese===
圣奥斯汀学校（St. Austin’s）的理查德·维纳布尔斯（Richard Venables），致剑桥大学国王学院（King’s College, Cambridge）的兄长阿奇博尔德·维纳布尔斯（Archibald Venables）：

> 亲爱的阿奇——我提笔给你写信，并非指望回音，而是为了（注意，这是修昔底德式的句法）向你通报一件大事——已往诸事之中最为重大的一桩。我同约翰叔叔（Uncle John）的那段历险，远方的风声你或许有所耳闻，或许没有。这位叔叔刚从钻石矿回来——瞧那副尊容便一望而知。事情经过如下：
> 上周三傍晚，我正穿过板球场前往车站迎接约翰叔叔——此行乃奉老爷子隆恩俯准、特降恩谕、钦命我前往。刚一到场，我便惊骇、错愕、且恶心地看见：一个身穿花哨格纹的中年粗汉——瞧那副长相，上可以是退休当铺老板，下可以是因偷雪利酒被上家辞退的二手段管家——正站在场地中央，恰好立在下周六（也就是明天）与拉格伯勒（Rugborough）比赛要用的那条球道（wicket）上，在挖——*挖*——尊驾听清楚了，是在挖。拿一根手杖，把我们最好的草皮大块大块地掘将起来。我五内俱焚，险些当场厥过去。无论何种情形，当校队队长这差事本身就够叫人心神不宁的了，可当你亲眼看见自己白天碾平、夜里梦见的球道，被一个彻头彻尾的陌生外人肆意糟蹋——唉！人们说，母牛丢了牛犊会略感愠怒，但我料想，古往今来最有母性的母牛，其怒火也万万不及我彼时胸中翻腾的狂怒。我口吐白沫地朝他飞扑过去。「亲爱的先生，」我尖叫道，「你*可*知道，你正在毁掉自有板球以来保养得最出色的一条球道？」他一脸茫然地望着我，说：「啥？」我说：「你觉得我们怎么可能在一片犁过的田里跟拉格伯勒比赛？」「俺听不懂，mister。」他答道。一个唤你作「mister」的男人，已自绝于文明社会之外，你对他稍加无礼，纯属合乎道义。于是我说：「那么你要么喝醉了，要么疯了，我祈祷是后者。」我记得这话出自某本书，书名忘了。这话似乎确实让他清醒了一点，但还没等他把他的意见组织成言语，场地管理员比芬（Biffen）来了——他就寝前要最后巡看一眼自己的球道。一看见那些坑——约莫一英尺深，东一个西一个，恰恰散布在三个球里有两个要落点的地方——他几乎当场歇斯底里。我温言向他说明了形势，便把他留给我那位格纹西装的朋友去发落。我走时，比芬正准备开讲一篇德摩斯梯尼式的长篇痛斥（Philippic），我心中笃定：此人已托付给胜任的双手。随后我去了车站。奉命要接的是 5:30 那班车。顺带一提，我自然丝毫不知约翰叔叔长什么模样——上回见他，我才一岁半——只奉到指示，要留心一位高个子、相当英俊的先生。5:30 那班倒是准点进了站，可乘客里没有一位对得上号：高个的都不英俊，唯一一位英俊的，穿上靴子也才五英尺整。我确实问过他是不是约翰·达尔格利什先生（Mr. John Dalgliesh）；怎奈他姓罗宾逊（Robinson），爱莫能助。我又干坐等了两班车，然后回到球场。那人已走了，比芬还在。「今儿个您是在等哪位吧，先生？」我走近时他问。「是啊。怎么？」我说。「方才那位，就是『他』嘞。」比芬说。我施展高超的盘问术，套出了全部原委。原来那位花格纹的、驳船夫模样的可怖恶汉，正是老爷子口中的「高个子、相当英俊的先生」；换言之，正是约翰叔叔本人。想必他是坐 4:30 那班来的。总之，人是到了，而我也已经把他得罪了个透。比芬告诉我，那位先生曾问起我是何人，而他（比芬）已如实奉告——彼时他正满脑子琢磨着，就那通乱挖还有什么别的话好跟那人讲。顺便说一句，我猜他挖坑纯属积习使然。没准儿还当真以为能挖出钻石来呢。比芬把这话转告他时，他用一种阴恻恻的嗓音说：「那么，等他回来，可否劳您大驾告知：我名叫约翰·达尔格利什，此事他还会听到下文。」而这「下文」，我怕是万分躲不掉了。我知道，老爷子极其看不上约翰叔叔，却又非要我对他格外客气周到，因为等我离校，还要仰仗他把我安插进某家讨厌的公司。家里尚未来信，不过料想必快。话虽如此，我倒要请教请教：眼看他毁掉我们本季度头一场重头戏的球道，我如何能袖手立在旁边？事已至此，球道再不能恢复本来的样子了。拉格伯勒那位慢速投手，只要摸着一处那样的坑洼，投出来的球就根本没法打。总而言之，这桩事实在可恶之至。速回信，虽然我知道你不会。
> ——你永远的，
> 迪克

### III / 三

===Original===
Letter from Mrs. James Anthony (née Miss Dorothy Venables) to her brother Richard Venables:

> Dear Dick—What *have* you been doing to Uncle John? Jim and I are stopping for a fortnight with father, and have just come in for the whole thing. Uncle John—*isn’t* he a horrible man?—says you were grossly insolent to him when he went down to see you. *Do* write and tell me all about it. I have heard no details as yet. Father refuses to give them, and gets simply *furious* when the matter is mentioned. Jim said at dinner last night that a conscientious boy would probably feel bound to be rude to Uncle John. Father said “Conscience be—”; I forget the rest, but it was awful. Jim says if he gets any worse we shall have to sit on his head, and cut the traces. He is getting so dreadfully *horsey*. Do write the very minute you get this. I want to know all about it.
> —Your affectionate sister,
> Dorothy

===Chinese===
詹姆斯·安东尼太太（Mrs. James Anthony，闺名多萝西·维纳布尔斯〔Dorothy Venables〕）致其弟理查德·维纳布尔斯的信：

> 亲爱的迪克——你到底对约翰叔叔干了什么呀？吉姆（Jim）和我正在父亲家小住两周，恰好撞上这整出好戏。约翰叔叔——他是不是个可怕的男人？——说你在他下去看你时对他无礼至极。千万千万写信把原委统统告诉我。细节我至今一无所闻：父亲拒绝透露，而且一提这事儿就*勃然大怒*。昨晚吃饭时吉姆说，一个凭良心办事的男孩，多半会觉得对约翰叔叔无礼乃是义不容辞。父亲说：「让良心见——」后半句我忘了，总之难听得很。吉姆说，他要是再这么恶化下去，我们就只好按住他的脑袋、割断他的缰绳了。他如今浑身的马味儿重得吓人。接信即刻回复，一分钟也别耽搁。我要知道全部内情。
> ——爱你的妹妹，
> 多萝西

### IV / 四

===Original===
Part of letter from Richard Venables, of St. Austin’s, to his father Major-General Sir Everard Venables, V.C., K.C.M.G.:

> … So you see it was really his fault. The Emperor of Germany has no right to come and dig holes in our best wicket. Take a parallel case. Suppose some idiot of a fellow (not that Uncle John’s that, of course, but you know what I mean) came and began rooting up your azaleas. Wouldn’t you want to say something cutting? I will apologize to Uncle John, if you like; but still, I do think he might have gone somewhere else if he really wanted to dig. So you see, etc., etc.

### V / 五

===Original===
Letter from Richard Venables, of St. Austin’s, to his sister Mrs. James Anthony:

> Dear Dolly—Thanks awfully for your letter, and thank Jim for his message. He’s a ripper. I’m awfully glad you married him and not that rotter, Thompson, who used to hang on so. I hope the most marvellous infant on earth is flourishing. And now about Uncle John. Really, I am jolly glad I did say all that to him. We played Rugborough yesterday, and the wicket was simply vile. They won the toss, and made two hundred and ten. Of course, the wicket was all right at one end, and that’s where they made most of their runs. I was wicket-keeping as usual, and I felt awfully ashamed of the beastly pitch when their captain asked me if it was the football-field. Of course, he wouldn’t have said that if he hadn’t been a pal of mine, but it was probably what the rest of the team thought, only they were too polite to say so. When we came to bat it was worse than ever. I went in first with Welch—that’s the fellow who stopped a week at home a few years ago; I don’t know whether you remember him. He got out in the first over, caught off a ball that pitched where Uncle John had been prospecting, and jumped up. It was rotten luck, of course, and worse was to follow, for by half-past five we had eight wickets down for just over the hundred, and only young Scott, who’s simply a slogger, and another fellow to come in. Well, Scott came in. I had made about sixty then, and was fairly well set—and he started simply mopping up the bowling. He gave a chance every over as regular as clockwork, and it was always missed, and then he would make up for it with two or three tremendous whangs—a safe four every time. It wasn’t batting. It was more like golf. Well, this went on for some time, and we began to get hopeful again, having got a hundred and eighty odd. I just kept up my wicket, while Scott hit. Then he got caught, and the last man, a fellow called Moore, came in. I’d put him in the team as a bowler, but he could bat a little, too, on occasions, and luckily this was one of them. There were only eleven to win, and I had the bowling. I was feeling awfully fit, and put their slow man clean over the screen twice running, which left us only three to get. Then it was over, and Moore played the fast man in grand style, though he didn’t score. Well, I got the bowling again, and halfway through the over I carted a half-volley into the Pav, and that gave us the match. Moore hung on for a bit and made about ten, and then got bowled. We made 223 altogether, of which I had managed to get seventy-eight, not out. It pulls my average up a good bit. Rather decent, isn’t it? The fellows rotted about a good deal, and chaired me into the Pav, but it was Scott who won us the match, I think. He made ninety-four. But Uncle John nearly did for us with his beastly walking-stick. On a good wicket we might have made any number. I don’t know how the affair will end. Keep me posted up in the governor’s symptoms, and write again soon.
> —Your affectionate brother,
> Dick
> P.S.—On looking over this letter, I find I have taken it for granted that you know all about the Uncle John affair. Probably you do, but, in case you don’t, it was this way. You see, I was going, etc., etc.

圣奥斯汀的理查德·维纳布尔斯致姐姐詹姆斯·安东尼太太的信：

> 亲爱的多莉——来信感激不尽，也替我谢谢吉姆的口信。他真是个妙人。你嫁的是他、而不是那个从前老缠着你不放的讨厌鬼汤普森（Thompson），我高兴极了。愿天下最神妙的婴孩茁壮成长。现在说约翰叔叔。说实在的，我把那些话统统说了出口，实在高兴得很。昨天我们对阵拉格伯勒，球道简直恶劣透顶。他们掷币获胜，拿下二百一十分。当然，球道有一端还像样，他们的分多半是在那一端得的。我照例担任三柱门守员（wicket-keeping），他们队长问我这是不是足球场的时候，我对这条可恶的球道羞愧难当。当然，他若不是我的朋友，绝不会说这话；但这多半也是他们全队的心声，只是其余诸位客气，不便出口。轮到我们击球时，情形更是雪上加霜。我和韦尔奇（Welch）率先出场——就是几年前在家里住过一个礼拜的那个家伙，不知你还记不记得他。第一个回合（over）他就出局了：一记球恰好落在约翰叔叔「勘探」过的地界，猛然弹起，被他截杀。这自然是倒霉透顶，而更糟的还在后头：到五点半，我们已失八座三柱门，得分刚过百，能上场的只剩纯粹抡大棒的小斯科特（Scott），外加另一名队友。于是斯科特上场。我那时已得六十分上下，站稳了阵脚——而他随即开始把对方的投球横扫一空。他每个回合准点奉送一次机会，回回无人接住；随后便用两三记惊天动地的猛轰找补回来——回回稳取四分。那不叫击球，倒更像打高尔夫。这般光景持续了些时候，我们已得一百八十几分，重又燃起希望。斯科特轰球时，我只管守住自己的三柱门。后来他被截杀，最后一人——一个姓摩尔（Moore）的——上了场。我把他排进队里，原是当投手（bowler）用的，不过他偶尔也能击两下子，所幸今日正是「偶尔」之一。只差十一分即可获胜，此刻面对投球的是我。我状态好极了，接连两记把对方慢速投手的球干干净净送过围屏，只剩三分便可拿下。那一回合打完，摩尔面对快球手应对得气派十足，虽然没得分。好啦，又轮到我迎击投球，回合中途，我把一记半高球轰进了休息亭（Pav），比赛就此到手。摩尔又支撑了片刻，得了十分上下，随后被投杀出局。我们总共得 223 分，其中我个人奋力拿下七十八分，未出局。这把我的平均分拉高了一大截。挺像样的吧？众人起哄了好一阵，还把我高抬进休息亭，不过依我看，赢下比赛的其实是斯科特——他得了九十四分。但约翰叔叔和他那根可恶的手杖，差一点就要了我们的命。球道若是完好，我们要得多少分有多少分。这事如何收场，我尚不得而知。随时向我通报老爷子的病情动向，并速再回信。
> ——爱你的弟弟，
> 迪克
> 又及——检读此信，我发现我已想当然地认定，你对约翰叔叔事件了如指掌。多半你确实知道；万一不知道，事情是这样的：你瞧，我当时正走着，云云，等等。

### VI / 六

===Original===
From Archibald Venables, of King’s College, Cambridge, to Richard Venables, of St. Austin’s:

> Dear Dick—Just a line to thank you for your letter, and to tell you that since I got it I have had a visit from the great Uncle John, too. He *is* an outsider, if you like. I gave him the best lunch I could in my rooms, and the man started a long lecture on extravagance. He doesn’t seem to understand the difference between the ’Varsity and a private school. He kept on asking leading questions about pocket-money and holidays, and wanted to know if my master allowed me to walk in the streets in that waistcoat—a remark which cut me to the quick, “that waistcoat” being quite the most posh thing of the sort in Cambridge. He then enquired after my studies; and, finally, when I saw him off at the station, said that he had decided not to tip me, because he was afraid that I was inclined to be extravagant. I was quite kind to him, however, in spite of everything; but I was glad you had spoken to him like a father. The recollection of it soothed me, though it seemed to worry him. He talked a good deal about it. Glad you came off against Rugborough.
> —Your ever,
> A. Venables

### VIII / 八

===Original===
Letter from Richard Venables, of St. Austin’s, to his father Major-General Sir Everard Venables, V.C., K.C.M.G.:

> Dear Father—Uncle John writes, in answer to my apology, to say that no apologies will meet the case; and that he has given his nomination in that rotten City firm of his to a fellow called Mortimer. But rather a decent thing has happened. There is a chap here I know pretty well, who is the son of Lord Marmaduke Twistleton, and it appears that the dook himself was down watching the Rugborough match, and liked my batting. He came and talked to me after the match, and asked me what I was going to do when I left, and I said I wasn’t certain, and he said that, if I hadn’t anything better on, he could give me a place on his estate up in Scotland, as a sort of land-agent, as he wanted a chap who could play cricket, because he was keen on the game himself, and always had a lot going on in the summer up there. So he says that, if I go up to the ’Varsity for three years, he can guarantee me the place when I come down, with a jolly good screw and a ripping open-air life, with lots of riding, and so on, which is just what I’ve always wanted. So, can I? It’s the sort of opportunity that won’t occur again, and you know you always said the only reason I couldn’t go up to the ’Varsity was, that it would be a waste of time. But in this case, you see, it won’t, because he wants me to go, and guarantees me the place when I come down. It’ll be awfully fine, if I may. I hope you’ll see it.
> —Your affectionate son,
> Dick
> P.S.—I think he’s writing to you. He asked your address. I think Uncle John’s a rotter. I sent him a rattling fine apology, and this is how he treats it. But it’ll be all right if you like this land-agent idea. If you like, you might wire your answer.
