## I At the Army School / I 在陆军学校

### I / 一

===Original===
I have said that Spring arrived late in 1916, and that up in the trenches opposite Mametz it seemed as though Winter would last forever. I also stated that *as for me, I had more or less made up my mind to die* because *in the circumstances there didn’t seem anything else to be done*. Well, we came back to Morlancourt after Easter, and on the same evening a message from the Orderly Room instructed me to proceed to the Fourth Army School next morning for a month’s refresher-course. Perhaps Colonel Kinjack had heard that I’d been looking for trouble. Anyhow, my personal grievance against the Germans was interrupted for at least four weeks, and a motorbus carried me away from all possibility of dying a murky death in the mine-craters.
Barton saw me off at the crossroads in the middle of the village. It was a fine day and he had recovered his good spirits. “Lucky Kangaroo﻿—to be hopping away for a holiday!” he exclaimed, as I climbed into the elderly bus. My servant Flook hoisted up my bulging valise, wiped his red face with his sleeve, and followed me to the roof. “Mind and keep Mr. Sherston well polished up and punctual on parade, Flook!” said Barton. Flook grinned; and away we went. Looking back, I saw Barton’s good-natured face, with the early sun shining on his glasses.
There were several of us on board (each Battalion in our Brigade was sending two officers) and we must have stopped at the next village to pick up a few more. But memory tries to misinform me that Flook and I were alone on that omnibus, with a fresh breeze in our faces and our minds “making a separate peace” with the late April landscape. With sober satisfaction I watched a train moving out of a station with rumble and clank of wheels while we waited at the crossing gates. Children in a village street surprised me: I saw a little one fall, to be gathered, dusted, cuffed and cherished by its mother. Up in the line one somehow lost touch with such humanities.
The War was abundantly visible in supply-convoys, artillery horse-lines, in the dirty white tents of a Red Cross camp, or in troops going placidly to their billets. But everyone seemed to be off duty; spring had arrived and the fruit trees were in blossom; breezes ruffled the reedy pools and creeks along the Somme, and here and there a peaceful fisherman forgot that he was a soldier on active service. I had been in close contact with trench warfare, and here was a demonstration of its contrast with cosy civilian comfort. One has to find things out as one goes along, I thought; and I was wholeheartedly grateful for the green grass and a miller’s wagon with four horses, and the spire of Amiens Cathedral rising above the congregated roofs of an undamaged city.

我说过，1916 年的春天来得很迟；而在马梅斯对面的战壕里，仿佛冬天要一直延续到永远。我也说过，*至于我，已多少打定了死的主意*，因为*在那种境况下，似乎再没有别的办法可想了*。嗯，复活节后我们回到莫朗库尔，当天傍晚营部勤务室便传来一道命令，令我次日前往第四集团军学校，参加为期一个月的复训班。说不定金杰克上校已听说我一直在自找麻烦。无论如何，我对德国人那一桩私怨至少被打断了四个星期，一辆长途汽车把我载走，使我彻底告别了在弹坑里稀里糊涂送命的可能。
巴顿在村子中央的十字路口为我送行。那是个好天，他的好兴致也恢复了过来。「好福气啊，袋鼠——一蹦一蹦地奔着度假去喽！」我正爬上那辆老客车，他这样嚷道。我的勤务兵弗卢克把我那鼓鼓囊囊的旅行袋扛了上去，用袖子抹了抹涨红的脸，跟着我爬上了车顶。「弗卢克，把舍尔斯顿先生伺候得精神点，操练要准点！」巴顿叮嘱道。弗卢克咧嘴一笑；车便开了。我回头望去，看见巴顿那张和善的脸，朝阳正照在他的眼镜上。
车上还有几个人（我们旅每个营都派两名军官），到下一个村子时想必又接了几个。可记忆却偏要骗我说，那辆公共汽车上只我和弗卢克两人，迎面是清新的微风，我们的心正与四月下旬的乡野风景「单独媾和」。车停在道口栅栏前等候时，我怀着一种清醒的满足，看着一列火车隆隆铿铿地驶出车站。村里街上的孩子们让我看了新奇：我瞧见一个小的摔倒在地，被母亲一把抱起，掸掸土，轻轻拍打两下，又疼爱起来。在前线上，人不知不觉就和这种人情味断了来往。
这场战争处处可见——辎重车队、炮兵马列、红十字会营地那些脏兮兮的白帐篷，或是安然走向驻地的队伍。可人人都仿佛下了班；春天已到，果树正在开花；微风拂过索姆河沿岸芦苇丛生的池塘与溪汊，这儿那儿有个安闲的垂钓者，竟忘了自己是个现役军人。我此前一直与堑壕战贴身相伴，眼前这一幕正演示着它与市民家居那份温馨适意的反差。我想，凡事都得一边走一边慢慢明白；而我由衷地感激那青青的草地、一辆四匹马拉的磨坊大车，以及亚眠大教堂的尖顶——它从未受损的城市一片攒聚的屋顶之上冉冉升起。

The Fourth Army School was at Flixécourt, a clean little town exactly halfway between Amiens and Abbeville. Between Flixécourt and the War (which for my locally experienced mind meant the Fricourt trenches) there were more than thirty English miles. Mentally, the distance became immeasurable during my first days at the School. Parades and lectures were all in the day’s work, but they failed to convince me of their affinity with our long days and nights in the Front Line. For instance, although I was closely acquainted with the mine-craters in the Fricourt sector, I would have welcomed a few practical hints on how to patrol those Godforsaken cavities. But the Army School instructors were all in favour of Open Warfare, which was sure to come soon, they said. They had learnt all about it in peacetime; it was essential that we should be taught to “think in terms of mobility.” So we solved tactical schemes in which the enemy was reported to have occupied some village several miles away, and with pencil and paper made arrangements for unflurried defence or blank-cartridged skirmishing in a land of field-day make-believe.
Sometimes a renowned big-game hunter gave us demonstrations of the art of sniping. He was genial and enthusiastic; but I was no good at rifle-shooting, and as far as I was concerned he would have been more profitably employed in reducing the numerical strength of the enemy. He was an expert on loopholes and telescopic-sights; but telescopic-sights were a luxury seldom enjoyed by an infantry battalion in the trenches.
The Commandant of the School was a tremendous worker and everyone liked him. His motto was “always do your utmost,” but I dare say that if he had been asked his private opinion he would have admitted that the School was in reality only a holiday for officers and N.C.O.s who needed a rest. It certainly seemed so to me when I awoke on the first morning and became conscious of my clean little room with its tiled floor and shuttered windows. I knew that the morning was fine; voices passed outside; sparrows chirped and starlings whistled; the bell in the church tower tolled and a clock struck the quarters. Flook entered with my Sam Brown belt and a jug of hot water. He remarked that we’d come to the right place, for once, and regretted that we weren’t there for the duration. Wiping my face after a satisfactory shave, I stared out of the window; on the other side of the street a blossoming apple-tree leant over an old garden wall, and I could see the friendly red roof of a dovecot. It was a luxury to be alone, with plenty of space for my portable property. There was a small table on which I could arrange my few books. Hardy’s *Far from the Madding Crowd* was one of them. Also Lamb’s *Essays* and *Mr. Sponge’s Sporting Tour*. Books about England were all that I wanted. I decided to do plenty of solid reading at the Army School.
Near by was the Mess Room where fourteen of us had our meals. A jolly-faced Captain from the Ulster Division had undertaken the office of Mess President and everyone was talkative and friendly. With half an hour to spare after breakfast, I strolled up the hill and smoked my pipe under a quick-set hedge. Loosening my belt, I looked at a chestnut tree in full leaf and listened to the perfect performance of a nightingale. Such things seemed miraculous after the desolation of the trenches. Never before had I been so intensely aware of what it meant to be young and healthy in fine weather at the outset of summer. The untroubled notes of the nightingale made the Army School seem like some fortunate colony which was, for the sake of appearances, pretending to assist the struggle from afar. It feels as if it’s a place where I might get a chance to call my soul my own, I thought, as I went down the hill to my first parade. If only they don’t chivvy us about too much, I added.﻿ ﻿… It was not unlike the first day of a public school term, and my form-master (we were divided into classes of twenty-eight) was a youngish Major in the Oxford and Bucks Light Infantry. He was an even tempered man, pleasant to obey and specially likeable through a certain shyness of manner. I cannot remember that any of us caused him any annoyance, though he more than once asked me to try and be less absentminded. Later in the year he was commanding a battalion, and I don’t doubt that he did it excellently.

第四集团军学校设在弗利克斯库尔，亚眠与阿布维尔正中间一座干净的小城。从弗利克斯库尔到这场战争（在我这深谙此地的人心里，它意味着弗里库尔的战壕），中间足有三十多英里。可在学校头几天，这段距离在心理上却变得无法丈量。出操、听课都属日常例行，可它们总叫我相信不了这跟前线的漫长日夜有何亲缘。比方说，弗里库尔防区那些弹坑我已了如指掌，倒真希望能讨几条怎样巡逻那些被上帝遗弃的窟窿的实用窍门。可陆军学校的教官们满口都是「运动战」，说这很快就要来临。这些他们在和平年代就学了个通透；至关重要的是，我们要受教「以机动性来思考」。于是我们做种种战术想定——据报敌军已占据数英里外的某村——而后用纸笔从容部署防御，或在演习的假想天地里来一场空包弹的 skirmish。
有时一位赫赫有名的大型猎物猎手来给我们示范狙击术。他为人热忱、兴致勃勃；可惜我对步枪射击一窍不通，就我而言，他若去削减敌军兵力倒更有用些。他是射击孔与瞄准镜的行家；可在战壕里的步兵营中，瞄准镜是少有的奢侈品。
学校的校长是个极其勤勉的人，人人都喜欢他。他的座右铭是「永远竭尽全力」，不过我敢说，若有人私下问他，他大概会承认这学校说到底不过是给需要休整的军官与军士们放个假。头一个早晨醒来，我觉察到自己那间干干净净的小屋——铺砖的地面、带百叶窗的窗户——情形确也如此。我知道外头是个好天；人声从窗外经过；麻雀啁啾，椋鸟鸣啭；教堂塔楼的钟悠悠地敲响，时钟报着刻数。弗卢克捧着我的萨姆·布朗武装带和一壶热水进来。他说这回我们可算来对了地方，又遗憾不能在这儿待到战争结束。一次舒坦的刮脸之后，我抹着脸，凝神望向窗外；街对面一棵开花的苹果树从一堵旧花园墙上探过身来，我还能望见一座鸽舍那和气的红屋顶。独处一室是种奢侈，宽敞得足够安置我那点随身家当。屋里有张小桌，可供我把所剩无几的几本书摆开。哈代的《远离尘嚣》是其中之一。还有兰姆的《伊利亚随笔》和《斯庞杰先生的游猎之行》。写英格兰的书，正是我想要的全部。我决计在陆军学校里好好做些扎实的阅读。
附近就是食堂，我们十四人在那里用饭。一位来自阿尔斯特师、生了一副快活面孔的上尉担任了食堂长，人人都健谈而友善。早饭后天还剩半个钟头，我信步爬上山岗，在一道绿篱荫下抽着烟斗。我松开腰带，望着枝叶满冠的栗子树，听一只夜莺唱得无懈可击。战壕的荒凉之后，这一切恍若奇迹。在此之前，我从未如此深切地体会到：在初夏的好天气里年轻而健康，意味着什么。夜莺那无忧无虑的啼啭，使陆军学校像极了某个幸运的殖民地，不过为了做做样子，才远远地装作在助这场战争一臂之力。这地方倒像能让我有机会把灵魂收归己有，我下山去赶头一次操练时这样想。只要他们别太拿我们折腾就好，我又添一句。……这一切倒很像是公学新学期开学头一天；我的级任老师（我们被分成二十八人一班）是牛津与白金汉轻步兵团一位年轻少校。他性情平和，让人乐意服从，又因带几分腼腆而格外讨人喜欢。我不记得我们当中有谁惹他生过气，尽管他不止一次请我努力少走点神。同年晚些时候，他便指挥一个营了，我毫不怀疑他干得极为出色。

Every afternoon at half-past five the School assembled to listen to a lecture. Eyeing an audience of about 300 officers and N.C.O.s, I improved my knowledge of regimental badges, which seemed somehow to affect the personality of the wearer. A lion, a lamb, a dragon or an antelope, a crown, a harp, a tiger or a sphinx, these devices differentiated men in more ways than one. But the regimental names were probably the potent factor, and my meditations while waiting for the lecturer would lead me along pleasant associative lanes connected with the English counties﻿—the difference between Durham and Devon for instance. There was food for thought also in the fact of sitting between a Connaught Ranger and a Seaforth Highlander, though both were likely to have been born in Middlesex. Queer, too, was the whole scene in that schoolroom, containing as it did a splendid sample of the Fourth Army which began the Somme Battle a couple of months afterwards. It was one of those peaceful war-pictures which have vanished forever and are rarely recovered even in imaginative retrospect.
My woolgatherings were cut short when the lecturer cleared his throat; the human significance of the audience was obliterated then, and its outlook on life became restricted to destruction and defence. A gas expert from G.H.Q. would inform us that “gas was still in its infancy.” (Most of us were either dead or disabled before gas had had time to grow up.) An urbane Artillery General assured us that high explosive would be our best friend in future battles, and his ingratiating voice made us unmindful, for the moment, that explosives often arrived from the wrong direction. But the star turn in the schoolroom was a massive sandy-haired Highland Major whose subject was “The Spirit of the Bayonet.” Though at that time undecorated, he was afterwards awarded the D.S.O. for lecturing. He took as his text a few leading points from the *Manual of Bayonet Training*.

> To attack with the bayonet effectively requires Good Direction, Strength and Quickness, during a state of wild excitement and probably physical exhaustion. The bayonet is essentially an offensive weapon. In a bayonet assault all ranks go forward to kill or be killed, and only those who have developed skill and strength by constant training will be able to kill. The spirit of the bayonet must be inculcated into all ranks, so that they go forward with that aggressive determination and confidence of superiority born of continual practice, without which a bayonet assault will not be effective.

He spoke with homicidal eloquence, keeping the game alive with genial and well-judged jokes. He had a Sergeant to assist him. The Sergeant, a tall sinewy machine, had been trained to such a pitch of frightfulness that at a moment’s warning he could divest himself of all semblance of humanity. With rifle and bayonet he illustrated the Major’s ferocious aphorisms, including facial expression. When told to “put on the killing face,” he did so, combining it with an ultra-vindictive attitude. “To instil fear into the opponent” was one of the Major’s main maxims. Man, it seemed, had been created to jab the life out of Germans. To hear the Major talk, one might have thought that he did it himself every day before breakfast. His final words were: “Remember that every Boche you fellows kill is a point scored to our side; every Boche you kill brings victory one minute nearer and shortens the war by one minute. Kill them! Kill them! There’s only one good Boche, and that’s a dead one!”
Afterwards I went up the hill to my favourite sanctuary, a wood of hazels and beeches. The evening air smelt of wet mould and wet leaves; the trees were misty-green; the church bell was tolling in the town, and smoke rose from the roofs. Peace was there in the twilight of that prophetic foreign spring. But the lecturer’s voice still battered on my brain. “The bullet and the bayonet are brother and sister.” “If you don’t kill him, he’ll kill you.” “Stick him between the eyes, in the throat, in the chest.” “Don’t waste good steel. Six inches are enough. What’s the use of a foot of steel sticking out at the back of a man’s neck? Three inches will do for him; when he coughs, go and look for another.”

每天下午五点半，全校集合听讲。我打量着约三百名军官与军士的听众，借机增进了对各团徽章的认识——这些徽章似乎在某种意义上影响着佩戴者的性情。一头狮子、一只羔羊、一条龙或一头羚羊、一顶王冠、一架竖琴、一只老虎或一尊斯芬克斯，这些图样以不止一种方式将人区别开来。但更具威力的，恐怕还是各团的名字；等待讲师开讲时，我的胡思乱想便顺着愉快的联想小径，溜到与英格兰各郡相关的话题上去——比如达勒姆与德文郡之间的差异。坐在一名康诺特 ranger 与一名海福德高地兵之间，也颇可玩味，虽说这两人八成都生在米德尔塞克斯。同样古怪的，是那间教室里的整个场面——其中正容纳着第四集团军一支精采的样本，两个月后，正是这支集团军发动了索姆河战役。这是那些已永远消逝的「宁静战争画面」之一，即便在最富想象力的回望里，也极少能再度拾起。
讲师清了清嗓子，我的白日梦便被打断；听众那作为人的意义旋即被抹去，其人生观也被压缩到只剩摧毁与防御。一位来自总司令部的毒气专家会告诉我们「毒气尚在襁褓之中」。（我们大多数人，还没等毒气长大成人，便已非死即残。）一位温文尔雅的炮兵将军向我们保证，在未来战役里高爆炸药将是我们最好的朋友，他讨好的语调使我们一时间忘了：炸药常常是从相反的方向飞来的。可教室里真正的明星，是一位身材魁伟、沙色头发的高地少校，他讲的题目是「刺刀之魂」。虽说当时他尚无勋章，后来却因讲课被授予优异服务勋章。他取材自《刺刀训练教范》中的若干要点，奉为讲义：

> 有效地以刺刀发起攻击，需要在高度亢奋、多半体力衰竭的状态下，具备良好的方向感、力量与速度。刺刀本质上是一种进攻性武器。在刺刀冲锋中，各阶官兵奋勇向前，非杀人即被杀；唯有经不断训练练出技艺与力量者，方能杀敌。刺刀之魂必须灌输给所有官兵，使其凭着不断练习所生的那股进取的决心与优越的自信而奋勇向前——若无此二者，刺刀冲锋便不会奏效。

他讲得杀气腾腾、雄辩滔滔，又用得体而恰如其分的笑话把场面撑得热络。他有一名中士从旁协助。那中士是个高大精壮的机器，被操练到一种骇人的境地，只消一声令下，便能卸下一切人性的伪装。他端着上了刺刀的步枪，把少校那些凶悍的格言一一演示出来，连面部表情也照做不误。一叫「换上杀人的脸」，他立马照办，并配上一副格外狠毒的姿态。「使敌人心生恐惧」是少校的主要箴言之一。看起来，人之被造，就是为了把德国人的命戳出来。听少校那口气，你会以为他每天早饭前都要亲手干上一回。他最后说道：「记住，弟兄们，你们每杀一个德国佬，就是给我方添上一分；每杀一个德国佬，就让胜利提早一分钟到来，让这场战争缩短一分钟。杀了他们！杀了他们！好德国佬只有一个，那就是死了的德国佬！」
过后我爬上山岗，到我钟爱的避难所——一片榛树与山毛榉的小树林——里去。傍晚的空气里有湿霉与湿叶的气味；树木是一种朦胧的绿；镇上的教堂钟声悠悠地敲响，屋顶上升起炊烟。在那预言般的外国春日的薄暮里，那里确有安宁。可讲师的声音仍在我脑中乱撞。「子弹与刺刀是兄妹。」「你不杀他，他就杀你。」「戳他两眼之间、喉咙、胸口。」「别糟蹋好钢。六英寸就够。一英尺长的钢条从人后脖颈戳出来有什么用？三英寸就够他受的了；他一咳嗽，你就去找下一个。」

### II / 二

===Original===
Whatever my private feelings may have been after the Major’s lecture, the next morning saw me practising bayonet-fighting. It was all in the day’s work; short points, long points, parries, jabs, plus the always to be remembered importance of “a quick withdrawal.” Capering over the obstacles of the assault course and prodding sacks of straw was healthy exercise; the admirable sergeant-instructor was polite and unformidable, and as I didn’t want him to think me a dud officer, I did my best to become proficient. Obviously it would have been both futile and inexpedient to moralize about bayonet-fighting at an Army School.
There is a sense of recovered happiness in the glimpse I catch of myself coming out of my cottage door with a rifle slung on my shoulder. There was nothing wrong with life on those fine mornings when the air smelt so fresh and my body was young and vigorous, and I hurried down the white road, along the empty street, and up the hill to our training ground. I was like a boy going to early school, except that no bell was ringing, and instead of Thucydides or Virgil, I carried a gun. Forgetting, for the moment, that I was at the Front to be shot at, I could almost congratulate myself on having a holiday in France without paying for it.
I also remember how I went one afternoon to have a hot bath in the Jute Mill. The water was poured into a dyeing vat. Remembering that I had a bath may not be of much interest to anyone, but it was a good bath, and it is my own story that I am trying to tell, and as such it must be received; those who expect a universalization of the Great War must look for it elsewhere. Here they will only find an attempt to show its effect on a somewhat solitary-minded young man.
At that time I was comfortably aware that the British Expeditionary Force in France was a prosperous concern. I have already remarked that the officers and N.C.O.s at the School epitomized a resolute mass of undamaged material; equally impressive was the equine abundance which I observed one afternoon when we were on our way to a “demonstration” at the Army Bombing School. Hundreds of light and heavy draft horses were drawn up along a road for an inspection by the Commander-in-Chief (a bodily presence which the infantry mind could not easily imagine). The horses, attached to their appropriate vehicles and shining in their summer coats, looked a picture of sleekness and strength. They were of all sorts and sizes but their power and compactness was uniform. The horsehood of England was there with every buckle of its harness brightened. There weren’t many mules among them, for mules were mostly with the Artillery, and this was a slap-up Army Service Corps parade, obviously the climax of several weeks’ preparation. I wished that I could have spent the afternoon inspecting them; but I was only a second-lieutenant, and the bus carried me on to study explosions and smoke-clouds, and to hear a lecture about the tactical employment of the Mills Bomb.

News of the Battalion came from the Quartermaster, to whom I had sent an account of my “cushy” existence. Dottrell wrote that things had been quiet up in the Line, but advised me to make the most of my rest-cure, adding that he’d always noticed that the further you got from the front-line the further you got from the War. His letter put me in the right frame of mind for returning to tours of trenches, though I should be sorry to say goodbye to young Allgood, with whom I was spending most of my spare time.
Allgood was quiet, thoughtful, and fond of watching birds. We had been to the same public school, though there were nearly ten years between us. He told me that he hoped to be a historian, and I listened respectfully while he talked about the Romans in Early Britain, which was his favourite subject. It was easy to imagine him as an undergraduate at Cambridge; travelling in Germany during the Long Vacation and taking a good degree. But his degree had been postponed indefinitely. He said he’d always wanted to go to Germany, and there seemed nothing incongruous in the remark; for the moment I forgot that every German we killed was a point scored to our side. Allgood never grumbled about the war, for he was a gentle soul, willing to take his share in it, though obviously unsuited to homicide. But there was an expression of veiled melancholy on his face, as if he were inwardly warned that he would never see his home in Wiltshire again. A couple of months afterwards I saw his name in one of the long lists of killed, and it seemed to me that I had expected it.

Our last day at the School was hot and cloudless. In the morning English and French Generals rolled up in their cars; there must have been about a hundred of them; it was not unlike an army of uniformed Uncles on Prize-giving Day. There were no prizes, naturally. But we did our best to show them how efficient we were, by running round the assault course in teams, stabbing the straw sacks. We also competed in putting up screw-pickets and barbed wire with rapidity and precision. Our exertions ended with a march past the Army Commander, and then we fell out to witness the explosion of two small mines. Earth and chalk heaved up at the blue sky, the ground vibrated, and there was a noise like a mad rainstorm, caused by the whizzing descent of clods and stones and the hiss of smaller particles. Finally, a fountain of dingy smoke arose and drifted away from the debris, and the Generals retired to have luncheon in the white château; and there, let us hope, they let their belts out a hole or two and allowed themselves a little relaxation from intellectual effort. Allgood said that he thought the French Generals looked much brainier than the British ones; but I told him that they must be cleverer than they looked, and anyhow they’d all got plenty of medal-ribbons.
