## IX

===Original===
The enquiry upon which I had now stirred myself to enter, could not be an easy one, but it should have seemed for the present to be narrowed down to a question about a single man. Perhaps it was from repugnance against consciously going about to hang a man who had sheltered under my roof, that I did not even then definitely put to myself the question of that man’s guilt. By some half-conscious sequence of thought I was led to begin my search far afield. It started with the two letters which had come for Peters from Mr. Charles Bryanston, or rather first with the later letter.

I had some time before written briefly and formally to Mr. Bryanston to acquaint him with the fact of Peters’ murder, but had, for a while since, thought no more of him. Now I began to do what one very seldom does, steadily and methodically think. I mooned up and down with a pipe in my garden or in the lanes. I sat, with those letters in my hand, alone before the fire. I sat at my writing-table with paper before me, and made incoherent jottings with a pencil. I should be afraid to say how often and how long I did all these seemingly idle things. Till at last, in the time between tea and dinner, with the children playing in the room, I arrived at actually spelling the matter out.

我如今强打精神着手进行的这项查究，注定不会轻松；不过就目前而论，它似乎本已收窄为关于单独一个人的问题。或许正是因为不忍存心奔走、亲手把一个曾在我屋檐下栖身的人送上绞架，直到那时，我也没有向自己明确摆出此人罪责的问题。循着某种半明半昧的思绪，我竟被引到远处去寻起线索来。入手处，便是查尔斯·布莱恩斯顿先生寄给彼得斯的那两封信——或者不如说，先从后寄到的那一封开始。

此前我曾简短而拘礼地致函布莱恩斯顿先生，把彼得斯遇害一事相告，此后一度没有再想起这个人。如今我开始做一件人们极难得做的事：沉下心来，有条有理地思考。我叼着烟斗，在花园里、在村巷间失魂落魄地踱来踱去。我把那两封信攥在手里，独自坐在炉火前。我坐在写字台前，面前摊着纸，用铅笔涂下些前言不搭后语的札记。这些看似闲散无谓的功课，我做了多少回、耗了多少时辰，我真不敢说。直到最后，在午后茶点与晚饭之间那段时间，孩子们还在屋里嬉戏，我终于把这桩事情真正一字一字拼解明白了。

“This time I will not delay my answer.” “This time﻿—” Then at other times he did delay his answer. That might have some significance when I turned to the earlier letter. “This time I will not delay my answer.” It was an answer to a question in a letter just received from Peters, an answer probably by return of post. Why not delay it this time as usual? Why, of course, because the question was one which both to Peters and to Bryanston seemed important, perhaps momentous. Simple enough so far. “Longhurst did sail in the *Eleanor*, and she did not go down.” It was clear enough that someone had thought that Longhurst had sailed in a ship that did go down. Peters had thought otherwise, and Peters was right. What of that? There is nothing momentous in that. Stop, though. It is not necessarily that. Someone need not have thought it﻿—he may have said it to Peters, and Peters may have thought it was a lie. And what did it matter, and why did someone say it? Well, of course, Longhurst would be dead if the ship had gone down; and Longhurst was not really dead, and someone was interested in saying that he was. Perhaps Longhurst was the next heir to some property, and search ought to have been made for him; and my mind wandered over all the stories I had ever read of lost heirs, in fact or in fiction. Or perhaps﻿—Who said Longhurst and his ship went down? “C.” said it, whoever “C.” might be.

「这一回我不会耽搁回信了。」「这一回——」那么，其余那些时候，他确是耽搁了回信的。这层意思，等我转到早先那封信时，或许自有其分量。「这一回我不会耽搁回信了。」这是对彼得斯刚寄到的那封信里某个问题的答复，多半还是趁下一班邮班当即回的。这一回为什么不照例耽搁？哦，当然是因为，那个问题在彼得斯与布莱恩斯顿两人看来都非同小可，说不定事关重大。至此为止，都简单得很。「朗赫斯特确实搭乘埃莉诺号出了海，而那船并没有沉。」显而易见，有人曾以为朗赫斯特乘的那艘船沉了。彼得斯不作此想，而彼得斯是对的。这又怎样？这里头谈不上什么事关重大。且慢，未必是那样。那人也未必真的作此想——他可能只是对彼得斯这么说过，而彼得斯可能认定那是句谎话。这又有什么要紧？那人为什么要那样说？嗯，当然啦，船若沉了，朗赫斯特便是死了；朗赫斯特其实没死，偏有人巴不得说他死了。说不定朗赫斯特是某宗产业的下一顺位继承人，本该着人寻访他的下落；我的思绪于是飘过了平生读过的种种失踪继承人的故事，无论实录还是虚构。再不然——是谁说朗赫斯特连人带船沉了海？是「C.」说的，管他这「C.」究竟是谁。

Then I took up the earlier letter. I knew from the other letter that this had been sent late. There was nothing further to be gained from the words of it, but a flood of suspicion broke upon me as I held it in my hand. Had “C.” another initial to his surname, a double name? Did I know this “C.”? Had I not seen this very letter in the hands of “C.”? Had I not thought it rather odd that he, a man so decidedly “all there,” had opened and read it before it was given to me? Had I not rather wondered at the pains he had kindly taken to help me with several letters before? Did he not laugh rather strangely as he read it, though I never heard him laugh at anything amusing? Did he not go away just after the letter came, though he had not been intending to go so soon? Was it conceivable that he knew that Peters had asked that question, and thought the first letter (“very uninforming,” as he called it) was the answer to that question, and an answer which made him safe? After that one laugh I thought he became suddenly downcast. Had he really read in that letter that he need not have feared Peters, and that he had﻿—yes, murdered him for nothing? Had the accident that Peters had written, perhaps long before, some unimportant question to Bryanston, and the accident that Bryanston had delayed his answer betrayed this man into leaving me alone with my letters a week too soon; and would this trifling mistake lead him to﻿—to the gallows? and I remembered with a start the grim end which I was preparing. Yes, all this was conceivable. There is an old maxim that you should beware of going back upon your first instinctive impressions of liking or dislike when you happen to have them. There are qualifications to it; the repulsions that start from ugliness or strangeness or difference of opinion may not be safe guides. But broadly the maxim is true. It was true in this instance. No, I too had never liked “C.”

随后我拿起早先那封信。从另一封信里我已得知，这一封当初寄出得很迟。信上的字句再榨不出更多的东西，可我把它捏在手里，一股怀疑的洪流当头浇下。「C.」的姓氏里，是不是还另有一个首字母，是个复姓？我认得这个「C.」么？我难道不曾亲眼看见，正是这封信捏在「C.」的手里？他——一个头脑那么分明「灵光」的人——竟在信交到我手上之前先拆开读过，我难道不曾觉得此事颇为蹊跷？此前他好心费神替我料理过好几封信，我难道不曾暗自称奇？他读这封信时难道不是笑得相当古怪么——尽管我从不曾听见他对任何有趣之事发笑？信刚到不久他岂不就动身走了么——尽管他原本并无意走得那么急？可不可能，他知道彼得斯问过那个问题，并以为头一封信——他称之为「言之无物」的那封——正是对那个问题的答复，而且是一封足以保他平安的答复？我记得，他单单笑了那一声之后，人便骤然委顿下去。他是否当真从那封信里读出了：他本不必惧怕彼得斯，而他竟——是啊——白白谋害了此人？莫非是两桩偶然——彼得斯或许早在此前许久，给布莱恩斯顿写信问过某个无关紧要的问题；布莱恩斯顿又耽搁了回信——诱使此人早了一周，撇下我和我的信件独自离去？而这桩细枝末节的差错，会不会把他引向——引向绞架？我不禁悚然一惊，想起自己正在筹办的，是怎样一个阴惨的结局。是的，这一切都讲得通。有句古老的箴言：你对一个人起了好恶的直觉印象——若碰巧起了——切莫轻易将它推翻。这话自有分寸的限制：那些由丑陋、怪异或意见相左而生的反感，未必是可靠的向导。但大体而言，这句箴言是对的。在这一桩事上，它也是对的。不错，我本来也从来不曾喜欢过「C.」。

It is strange that I should have received Mr. Bryanston’s answer the very next morning, a long, full, warmhearted letter on the death of the friend to whose letters in life﻿—and what letters Peters wrote!﻿—he made such scrappy replies. In a P.S. at the end, as if the writer had hesitated whether to write it, were the words: “It is curious and may be news to you that Mr. Peters, at the time he was murdered, was unravelling the mystery of another murder, committed, as he suspected, many years ago.”

So then, as I had half-guessed, Longhurst was dead. It was not that he was alive and Cartwright pretended he was dead, he was dead, and Cartwright had a motive for falsely pretending he was drowned.

说来也怪，恰恰在第二天早晨，我便收到了布莱恩斯顿先生的回信——一封悼念亡友的长信，写得情辞恳切、备极周详；而对这位朋友生前的来信——彼得斯写的是些什么样的信啊！——他素来只回以零碎草草的短简。信末有一则附言，落笔的人仿佛踌躇过该不该写：「说来奇怪，或许也是您未曾听说的：彼得斯先生遇害之时，正在解开另一桩谋杀案的疑团；据他猜想，那桩案子是许多年前犯下的。」

于是，正如我隐约猜中的那样，朗赫斯特死了。并非他人还活着、由卡特赖特谎称他已死——他是死了，而卡特赖特自有其动机，才要假称他溺毙身亡。
