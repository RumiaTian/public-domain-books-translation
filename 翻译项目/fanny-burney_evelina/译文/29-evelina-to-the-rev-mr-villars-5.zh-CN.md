## Letter / 书信
===Original===

XXVI Evelina to the Rev. Mr. Villars
Howard Grove, April 27.
O my dear Sir, I now write in the greatest uneasiness! Madame Duval has made a proposal which terrifies me to death, and which was as unexpected as it is shocking.
She had been employed for some hours this afternoon in reading letters from London: and, just about teatime, she sent for me into her room, and said, with a look of great satisfaction, “Come here, child, I’ve got some very good news to tell you: something that will surprise you, I’ll give you my word, for you ha’n’t no notion of it.”
I begged her to explain herself; and then, in terms which I cannot repeat, she said she had been considering what a shame it was to see me such a poor country, shamefaced thing, when I ought to be a fine lady; and that she had long, and upon several occasions, blushed for me, though she must own the fault was none of mine; for nothing better could be expected from a girl who had been so immured. However, she assured me she had, at length, hit upon a plan, which would make quite another creature of me.
I waited, without much impatience, to hear what this preface led to; but I was soon awakened to more lively sensations, when she aquainted me, that her intention was to prove my birthright, and to claim, by law, the inheritance of my real family!
It would be impossible for me to express my extreme consternation when she thus unfolded her scheme. My surprise and terror were equally great; I could say nothing: I heard her with a silence which I had not the power to break.
She then expatiated very warmly upon the advantages I should reap from her plan; talked in a high style of my future grandeur; assured me how heartily I should despise almost everybody and everything I had hitherto seen; predicted my marrying into some family of the first rank in the kingdom; and, finally, said I should spend a few months in Paris, where my education and manners might receive their last polish.
She enlarged also upon the delight she should have, in common with myself, from mortifying the pride of certain people, and showing them that she was not to be slighted with impunity.
In the midst of this discourse, I was relieved by a summons to tea. Madame Duval was in great spirits; but my emotion was too painful for concealment, and everybody enquired into the cause.

第二十六封 伊芙琳娜致尊敬的维拉尔斯牧师先生

霍华德林苑，4月27日。

哦，亲爱的先生，我此刻是在极端的不安中给您写信！杜瓦尔夫人提出了一个主张，把我吓坏了——它来得意外，又叫人震惊。

今天下午，她读伦敦来信读了几个钟头；快到喝茶的时分，她打发人叫我进她的房间，一脸大大的得意：“过来，孩子，我有个天大的好消息告诉你：保管你想不到，我说话算数，你猜也没处猜。”

我求她说明白；随后，用一种我没法复述的字眼，她说，她一直在想：眼看我这样一个可怜巴巴、乡下气的、见人就脸红的东西，本该做位阔太太，多不像话；好几回了，她都替我脸红——虽然她承认这不怪我；一个这样圈禁大的姑娘，还能指望什么更好的呢。不过她要我放心，她终于想出了一个主意，管保叫我脱胎换骨、判若两人。

我不很性急地等着听这段开场白的下文；可她一说破——她的打算，是要证实我的出身权，依法认领我真正家族的产业——我立刻醒到了更剧烈的感觉里！

她这样摊开她的计划时，我的惊骇无法表达。惊与惧一样大；我说不出话：我听她说着，沉默得没力气打破。

接着她热烈地铺陈我将从她的计划里捞到的好处；高谈我日后的尊荣；担保我准会鄙视我迄今见过的一切人和一切东西；预言我嫁进王国里头一等的人家；末了说，我该去巴黎住上几个月，让我的教养和举止受一受最后的打磨。

她还大大渲染了她会同我共享的那份快意——杀一杀某些人的骄气，叫他们看看，轻慢她是要付代价的。

这篇宏论讲到一半，一声唤茶的铃救了我。杜瓦尔夫人兴致极高；可我的激动藏不住，人人都问缘故。

I would fain have waived the subject, but Madame Duval was determined to make it public. She told them that she had it in her head to *make something* of me, and that they should soon call me by another name than that of Anville; and yet that she was not going to have the child married neither.
I could not endure to hear her proceed, and was going to leave the room; which, when Lady Howard perceived, she begged Madame Duval would defer her intelligence to some other opportunity; but she was so eager to communicate her scheme, that she could bear no delay; and therefore they suffered me to go without opposition. Indeed, whenever my situation or affairs are mentioned by Madame Duval, she speaks of them with such bluntness and severity, that I cannot be enjoined a task more cruel than to hear her.
I was afterwards accquainted with some particulars of the conversation by Miss Mirvan; who told me that Madame Duval informed them of her plan with the utmost complacency, and seemed to think herself very fortunate in having suggested it; but, soon after, she accidentally betrayed, that she had been instigated to the scheme by her relations the Branghtons, whose letters, which she received today, first mentioned the proposal. She declared that she would have nothing to do with any *roundabout ways*, but go openly and instantly to law, in order to prove my birth, real name, and title to the estate of my ancestors.
How impertinent and officious in these Branghtons, to interfere thus in my concerns! You can hardly imagine what a disturbance this plan has made in the family. The Captain, without enquiring into any particulars of the affair, has peremptorily declared himself against it, merely because it has been proposed by Madame Duval; and they have battled the point together with great violence. Mrs. Mirvan says, she will not even *think*, till she hears your opinion. But Lady Howard, to my great surprise, openly avows her appprobation of Madame Duval’s intention; however, she will write her reasons and sentiments upon the subject to you herself.
As to Miss Mirvan, she is my second self, and neither hopes nor fears but as I do.

我本想把这个话头岔开，可杜瓦尔夫人打定主意要公开。她告诉他们，她一心要把我“*造就成个人物*”，往后他们该改口叫我另一个姓，不叫安维尔了；不过呢，她又并不打算就叫这孩子嫁人。

我实在听不下去，正要出屋；霍华德夫人看出来了，求杜瓦尔夫人把她的高见换个时候再发表；可她急着宣布她的计划，一刻也等不得；于是他们也没拦我，由我去了。说真的，每逢杜瓦尔夫人谈起我的处境或我的事，总是那样直戳、那样不留情面，再没有比听她说更叫我受罪的差事了。

随后米尔文小姐把那场谈话的细节告诉了我；她说，杜瓦尔夫人向他们宣布计划时，一脸的志得意满，还自庆想出了这么个主意，福气不小；可不久她偶然漏了底：撺掇她的是她布兰顿家的亲戚，今天收到的信里头一回提起了这个主张。她声言，什么*绕弯子的路*她一概不走，要公开地、立刻地对簿公堂，好证实我的出身、我的真名，和我对祖先产业的权利。

这些布兰顿多么好事、多么爱管闲人闲事，这样插手我的事情！您简直想象不出，这个计划在这家里掀起了怎样的风波。舰长不问事情的原委，斩钉截铁地反对——只为它是杜瓦尔夫人提出来的；两人为这一点大动干戈。米尔文太太说，不听您的意见，她连*想*都不想。可霍华德夫人，大大出于我的意外，公开赞成杜瓦尔夫人的打算；不过，她的理由和感想，她会亲自写信告诉您。

至于米尔文小姐，她是我的第二个自我，不盼望、也不惧怕我所不盼望、不惧怕的。

And as to *me*﻿—I know not what to say, nor even what to wish; I have often thought my fate peculiarly cruel, to have but one parent, and from that one to be banished forever;﻿—while, on the other side, I have but too well known and felt the propriety of the separation. And yet, you may much better imagine, than I can express, the internal anguish which sometimes oppresses my heart, when I reflect upon the strange indifference that must occasion a father never to make the least enquiry after the health, the welfare, or even the life of his child!
O Sir, to *me* the loss is nothing!﻿—greatly, sweetly, and most benevolently have you guarded me from feeling it; but for *him*, I grieve indeed!﻿—I must be divested, not merely of all filial piety, but of all humanity, could I ever think upon this subject, and not be wounded to the soul.
Again I must repeat, I know not what to *wish*; think for me, therefore, my dearest Sir, and suffer my doubting mind, that knows not which way to direct its hopes, to be guided by your wisdom and unerring counsel.
Evelina.

至于*我*——我不知道说什么，连愿望什么都不知道；我常常想，我的命苦得出奇：只有一个亲人，又被那亲人永远地放逐；——可另一方面，这隔离的合宜，我又知道得太清、感得太切。然而，每当我想到那奇怪的无情——一个父亲，对孩子的安康、福祉、甚至生死，竟从不过问一声——那种咬着内心的痛楚，您想得到，远胜过我说得出。

哦，先生，*于我*，这损失算不得什么！——您伟大地、温存地、至仁至慈地护住了我，不叫我尝到它；可*为他*，我真心悲痛！——我必得剥尽做女儿的孝心，再剥尽为人的天良，才能想这个题目而不痛彻灵魂。

我再说一遍：我不知道该*愿望*什么；那么，替我想吧，最亲爱的先生，容我这颗不知把希望导向何方的疑惑的心，受您智慧和无误的指点引领。

伊芙琳娜。
