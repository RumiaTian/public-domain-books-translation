## XLIII An Old Lady’s Diary / XLIII 一位老太太的日记
===Original===

On the following afternoon Laura drove straight from the House of Detention to Russell Square. Her interview with her husband had been full of comfort. Mr. Leopold had been with his client, and Mr. Leopold was in excellent spirits. He had no doubt as to the issue of his case, even without Desrolles; and the detectives had very little doubt of finding Desrolles.
“A man of that age and of those habits doesn’t go far,” said the lawyer, speaking of this human entity with as much assurance as if he were stating a mathematical truth.
Laura got out of her cab before one of the dullest-looking houses in the big, handsome old square﻿—a house brightened by no modern embellishment in the way of Venetian blind or encaustic flower-box, but kept with a scrupulous care. Not a speck upon the window panes, not a spot upon the snow-white steps, the varnish of the door as fresh as if it had been laid on yesterday.
The door was opened by an old manservant in plain clothes. Laura grew hopeful at the sight of him. He looked like a man who had lived fifty years in one service﻿—the kind of man who begins as a knife-boy, and either stultifies a spotless career by going to America with the plate, or ends as a pious annuitant, in the odour of sanctity.
“Does Mrs. Malcolm still live here?” asked Laura.
“Yes, ma’am.”
“Is she at home?”
“I will inquire, ma’am, if you will be kind enough to give me your card,” replied the man, as much as to say that his mistress was a lady whose leisure was not to be irreverently disturbed. She was to be at home, as it pleased her sovereign will, and according to the quality and claims of her visitor.
Laura wrote upon one of her cards, “Stephen Malcolm’s daughter, Laura,” while the ancient butler produced a solid old George the Second salver whereon to convey the card with due reverence to his mistress.
The address upon the card looked respectable, and so did Laura, and upon the strength of these appearances the butler ventured to show the stranger into the dining room, where the furniture was of the good old Brobdingnagian stamp, and there was nothing portable except the fire-irons. Here Laura waited in a charnel-house atmosphere, while Mrs. Malcolm called up the dim shadows of the past, and finally came to the determination that she would hold parley with this young person who claimed to be of her kindred.

「那个年纪、那种习性的男人，走不远。」律师说道，谈起这个人时满口的笃定，仿佛在陈述一条数学真理。

劳拉在广场上一所最不起眼的房子前下了车——这座宽大气派的老广场上，这所房子不见任何现代装点，既无软百叶窗，也无彩绘陶花箱，却被照料得一丝不苟。窗玻璃上不见一粒尘点，雪白的台阶上不见一丝污迹，门上的清漆新鲜得像昨天才刷上去的。

开门的是一位穿便服的老男仆。劳拉一见他，心里便生出了希望。他看上去像是在一户人家当差当了五十年的人——这种人从擦刀小厮做起，要么卷了主人的银器逃往美洲，葬送一世清白，要么领着年金终老，做个虔诚的老仆，落得个圣徒的美名。

「马尔科姆太太还住在这儿吗？」劳拉问。

「是的，夫人。」

「她在家吗？」

「我去通传，夫人，劳驾您把名片给我。」男仆答道，那口气等于说：他的女主人是位夫人，她的清福不容人冒失打搅。见不见客，全凭她的圣裁，还得看来客的身份和来历。

劳拉在一张名片上写下「斯蒂芬·马尔科姆之女劳拉」，那位年迈的管家则捧出一面厚实的乔治二世时代的老托盘，好把名片毕恭毕敬地呈给女主人。

名片上的地址看着体面，劳拉本人也看着体面，凭这两点，管家才敢把这位生客让进饭厅。厅里的家具是一水儿老派的大人国气派，件件厚重，除了火炉用具，没有一件拿得动的东西。劳拉便在这坟堂般的气氛里等着，任马尔科姆太太把往事的暗影一一召回，终于打定主意，要同这个自称是她亲族的年轻人谈一谈。

冷冷清清地等了一阵，管家回来了，引着特雷弗顿太太登上那道宽阔而阴森的楼梯——灰褐色的墙壁俯视着石青色的地毯——一直走进那间宽大空旷的客厅。这客厅，向来是她童年最冷的记忆之一。

这是一间又长又高的屋子，陈设着纪念碑似的红木家具。碗橱好似坟墓——长沙发令人想起祭坛——屋子中央的桌子，厚重得如同达特穆尔荒野或布列塔尼沙原上东一处西一处冒出来的那种德鲁伊石碑。一张面色苍白的钟在白色大理石炉台上庄严地滴答作响，三扇高窗之间垂着重重灰褐色帷幔，只放进几道惨淡的天光。

在这陵寝般的屋子里，一炉黯淡而吝啬的火旁，坐着一位穿黑缎衣裳的老太太——还是那副身影，还是那身衣裳，劳拉多年前记得的：或者说，那衣裳像得叫人只当还是原来那一件。

「伯母，」劳拉怯生生地上前说，只觉得自己又变回了小孩子，注定要被单独关在这间可怕的屋子里，「您把我忘了吗？」

黑缎衣裳的老太太伸出手来——一只枯瘦的白手，戴着黑色露指手套，缀着几枚老式戒指。

「没有，亲爱的，」她答道，没有半点惊讶的表示，「我从不忘记任何人、任何事。我的记性好，眼力和耳朵也好。上天待我恩厚得很。你的名片起先叫我纳闷，可我细细一想，立刻就明白你是谁了。坐下吧，亲爱的。叫乔纳姆给你端一杯雪利酒来。」

老太太站起身，摇了铃。

「请您别费事，伯母，」劳拉说，「我从不喝雪利酒。我什么都不要，只想同您稍稍谈谈我可怜的父亲。」

「可怜的斯蒂芬，」马尔科姆太太答道，「太不知检点了，可怜的人。他不害别人，专害自己。这么说你嫁人了，亲爱的？不用了，乔纳姆，我侄女什么都不用。」这话是对管家说的。「我记得，你父亲的一位老朋友收养了你。可怜的斯蒂芬去世的第二天我去了奇斯威克，发现你已经被接走了。知道你有了着落，我很高兴；当然，我自己也会尽力的，设法把你送进什么慈善学堂，或者那一类的地方。这个家我可容不下孩子。孩子什么都弄得乱七八糟。

I hope your father’s friend has carried out his undertaking handsomely?”
“He was all goodness,” answered Laura. “He was more than a father to me. But I lost him two years ago.”
“I hope he left you independent?”
“He made me independent by a deed of trust, when I first went to him. He settled six thousand pounds for my benefit.”

But it has been great pleasure to me to look through the pages from time to time, and call up old days. It is almost like living over again. Kindly take my keys, Laura, and open the right-hand door of chiffonier.”
Laura obeyed. The interior of the chiffonier was divided into shelves, and on the uppermost of these shelves were neatly arranged three and twenty small volumes, bound in morocco, and lettered Diary, with the date of each year. The parliamentary records at Strawberry Hill are not more carefully kept than the history of Mrs. Malcolm’s life.
“Let me see,” she said. “Your father died in the winter of ’56; your poor mother a few months earlier. Bring me the volume for ’56.”
Laura handed the book to the old lady, who gave a gentle little sigh as she opened it.
“Dear me, how neatly I wrote in ’56,” she exclaimed. “My handwriting has sadly degenerated since then. We get old, my dear; we grow old without knowing it.”
Laura thought that in that monumental drawing room age might well creep on unawares. Life there must be a long hybernation.
“Let me see. I must find some of my conversations with your mother. ‘June 2. Read prayers. Breakfast. My rasher was cut too thick, and the frying was not up to cook’s usual mark.
