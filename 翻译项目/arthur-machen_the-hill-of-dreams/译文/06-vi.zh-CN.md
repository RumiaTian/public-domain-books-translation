## VI / 第六章

===Original===

It was in this winter after his coming to the grey street that Lucian first experienced the pains of desolation. He had all his life known the delights of solitude, and had acquired that habit of mind which makes a man find rich company on the bare hillside and leads him into the heart of the wood to meditate by the dark waterpools. But now in the blank interval when he was forced to shut up his desk, the sense of loneliness overwhelmed him and filled him with unutterable melancholy. On such days he carried about with him an unceasing gnawing torment in his breast; the anguish of the empty page awaiting him in his bureau, and the knowledge that it was worse than useless to attempt the work. He had fallen into the habit of always using this phrase “the work” to denote the adventure of literature; it had grown in his mind to all the austere and grave significance of “the great work” on the lips of the alchemists; it included every trifling and laborious page and the vague magnificent fancies that sometimes hovered below him. All else had become mere byplay, unimportant, trivial; the work was the end, and the means and the food of his life﻿—it raised him up in the morning to renew the struggle, it was the symbol which charmed him as he lay down at night. All through the hours of toil at the bureau he was enchanted, and when he went out and explored the unknown coasts, the one thought allured him, and was the colored glass between his eyes and the world. Then as he drew nearer home his steps would quicken, and the more weary and grey the walk, the more he rejoiced as he thought of his hermitage and of the curious difficulties that awaited him there. But when, suddenly and without warning, the faculty disappeared, when his mind seemed a hopeless waste from which nothing could arise, then he became subject to a misery so piteous that the barbarians themselves would have been sorry for him. He had known some foretaste of these bitter and inexpressible griefs in the old country days, but then he had immediately taken refuge in the hills, he had rushed to the dark woods as to an anodyne, letting his heart drink in all the wonder and magic of the wild land. Now in these days of January, in the suburban street, there was no such refuge.

He had been working steadily for some weeks, well enough satisfied on the whole with the daily progress, glad to awake in the morning, and to read over what he had written on the night before. The new year opened with faint and heavy weather and a breathless silence in the air, but in a few days the great frost set in. Soon the streets began to suggest the appearance of a beleaguered city, the silence that had preceded the frost deepened, and the mist hung over the earth like a dense white smoke. Night after night the cold increased, and people seemed unwilling to go abroad, till even the main thoroughfares were empty and deserted, as if the inhabitants were lying close in hiding. It was at this dismal time that Lucian found himself reduced to impotence. There was a sudden break in his thought, and when he wrote on valiantly, hoping against hope, he only grew more aghast on the discovery of the imbecilities he had committed to paper. He ground his teeth together and persevered, sick at heart, feeling as if all the world were fallen from under his feet, driving his pen on mechanically, till he was overwhelmed. He saw the stuff he had done without veil or possible concealment, a lamentable and wretched sheaf of verbiage, worse, it seemed, than the efforts of his boyhood. He was no longer tautological, he avoided tautology with the internal art of a leader-writer, filling his wind bags and mincing his words as if he had been a trained journalist on the staff of the *Daily Post*. There seemed all the matter of an insufferable tragedy in these thoughts; that his patient and enduring toil was in vain, that practice went for nothing, and that he had wasted the labour of Milton to accomplish the tenth-rate. Unhappily he could not “give in”; the longing, the fury for the work burnt within him like a burning fire; he lifted up his eyes in despair.

It was then, while he knew that no one could help him, that he languished for help, and then, though he was aware that no comfort was possible, he fervently wished to be comforted. The only friend he had was his father, and he knew that his father would not even understand his distress. For him, always, the printed book was the beginning and end of literature; the agony of the maker, his despair and sickness, were as accursed as the pains of labour. He was ready to read and admire the work of the great Smith, but he did not wish to hear of the period when the great Smith had writhed and twisted like a scotched worm, only hoping to be put out of his misery, to go mad or die, to escape somehow from the bitter pains. And Lucian knew no one else. Now and then he read in the paper the fame of the great *litérateurs*; the Gypsies were entertaining the Prince of Wales, the Jolly Beggars were dining with the Lord Mayor, the Old Mumpers were mingling amicably and gorgeously with the leading members of the Stock Exchange. He was so unfortunate as to know none of these gentlemen, but it hardly seemed likely that they could have done much for him in any case. Indeed, in his heart, he was certain that help and comfort from without were in the nature of things utterly impossible, his ruin and grief were within, and only his own assistance could avail. He tried to reassure himself, to believe that his torments were a proof of his vocation, that the facility of the novelist who stood six years deep in contracts to produce romances was a thing wholly undesirable, but all the while he longed for but a drop of that inexhaustible fluency which he professed to despise.

来到这条灰色街道之后的这个冬天，卢西安（Lucian）第一次尝到了凄凉的痛楚。他平生只识得孤独的欢愉，并且养成了那样一种心性：教人在光秃的山坡上也能觅得丰盛的伴侣，引人走进树林深处，在幽黑的水潭旁冥想。可如今，在他被迫合上写字台的空白间隙里，孤寂之感把他压倒，使他满怀难以言说的忧郁。这样的日子里，他胸中终日揣着一阵啮咬不休的煎熬：那是在写字台里等候他的空白书页的苦楚，是明知此时去碰「那部书」非但无益、反而更糟的了悟。他早已习惯，始终用「那部书」这个说法来指称文学这场历险；在他心里，这个词已长成炼金术士口中「大业」（the great work）所具有的那全部严峻而庄重的分量；它涵盖每一页琐碎劳苦的文字，也涵盖时而在他下方幽幽盘旋的那些恢弘而朦胧的奇想。其余一切都成了余兴，无关紧要，琐屑无聊；那部书是他的归宿，是他生命的凭借与食粮——清晨是它扶他起身，重整鏖战；夜里又是它化作咒符，安顿他入眠。在写字台前劳作的整段时光里，他如痴如醉；出门踏探未知的海岸时，也只有这一个念想引诱着他，像隔在他眼睛与世界之间的一块彩色玻璃。离家渐近，他的脚步便愈走愈快；归途愈是疲惫、愈是灰暗，他想到自己的隐庐、想到那里等候他的种种奇妙的难处，便愈是欢喜。可是一旦那官能毫无征兆地骤然消隐，一旦他的心智沦为一片无可救药的荒芜、再长不出任何东西，他便堕入一种凄恻入骨的悲苦，连野蛮人自己见了也要为他难过。在早年的乡间岁月里，他已尝过这些苦涩难宣的悲哀的些许滋味；但那时他随即便逃进山中，奔赴幽暗的树林如奔赴一剂止痛药，任他的心饮尽那片蛮荒之地的全部奇瑰与魔法。而如今，在这一月的天色里，在这条郊外的街上，再没有那样的避难所了。

此前几个星期他一直笔耕不辍，对每日的进展总体还算满意，清晨醒来也是欢喜的，乐意把前一晚写下的东西重读一遍。新年是在阴晦低沉的天气里开场的，空气里有一种屏息的寂静；不出几日，大冰冻降临了。很快，这些街道便显出一副被围困城池的模样，先于冰冻而至的寂静愈发深沉，雾霭如浓白的烟，笼罩着大地。一夜冷过一夜，人们似乎都不愿出门，到后来连通衢大道也空空荡荡、渺无人迹，仿佛居民们全都深藏匿伏。正是在这凄黯的时节，卢西安发觉自己陷于无力。他的思路猛然断裂；他硬着头皮写下去，怀着万一的希望，结果只越发惊骇地发现，自己落到纸上的尽是白痴般的文字。他咬紧牙关苦撑，心中作呕，只觉得整个世界都从脚底下塌陷了下去，机械地驱着笔往前赶，直到彻底被击溃。他把自己写出的东西看了个通透，无遮无掩、无从掩饰：一束可悲可悯的芜词冗语，看来比他少年时代的习作还要不如。他已不再颠三倒四地重复；他以社论作家内在的功夫回避重复，鼓起他的风袋，把字句剁成细末，活像《每日邮报》（*Daily Post*）编辑部里一名训练有素的记者。这些念头里简直含着一出不堪忍受的悲剧的全部材料：他坚忍持久的辛劳尽属徒然，磨练毫无所成，他枉费了弥尔顿（Milton）的劳作，只做出十流的货色。不幸的是，他不能「认输」；对那部书的渴念与狂热像一团烈火在他胸中燃烧；他绝望地举目仰望。

正是在那时，他明知无人能助，却因渴求援助而憔悴；也正是在那时，他明知慰藉无望，却热切地盼人安慰。他唯一的朋友是他的父亲，而他知道，父亲连他的苦楚都不会懂得。在父亲那里，印成的书从来就是文学的起点与终点；制作者的煎熬、他的绝望与沉疴，同分娩的阵痛一样被目为咒诅。他乐于阅读并叹赏大作家史密斯（the great Smith）的作品，却不愿听说大史密斯也曾像一条被剁伤未死的蛇般翻滚扭动，一心只求得以解脱，发疯也好，死了也好，好歹逃出那苦痛。而卢西安再不认识旁人。他偶尔在报上读到伟大*文豪*们的声名：「吉普赛人」在款待威尔士亲王，「快活乞丐」在与伦敦市长共餐，「老叫花子」正与证券交易所的头面人物融洽而豪奢地过从。他不曾有幸认识这些先生中的任何一位；不过无论如何，他们看来也帮不上他多少忙。的确，在他心底他认定：外来的援助与安慰，就事物的本性而言是全然不可能的；他的毁灭与悲哀都在内里，唯有自救一途可恃。他试着宽解自己，说服自己相信：他的磨难正是他天职的明证；那位深陷六年长约、专事炮制传奇的小说家的下笔万言，本是全然要不得的东西——可他自始至终渴念的，恰恰是他佯装鄙夷的那份永不枯竭的流畅，哪怕一滴也好。

He drove himself out from that dreary contemplation of the white paper and the idle pen. He went into the frozen and deserted streets, hoping that he might pluck the burning coal from his heart, but the fire was not quenched. As he walked furiously along the grim iron roads he fancied that those persons who passed him cheerfully on their way to friends and friendly hearths shrank from him into the mists as they went by. Lucian imagined that the fire of his torment and anguish must in some way glow visibly about him; he moved, perhaps, in a nimbus that proclaimed the blackness and the flames within. He knew, of course, that in misery he had grown delirious, that the well-coated, smooth-hatted personages who loomed out of the fog upon him were in reality shuddering only with cold, but in spite of common sense he still conceived that he saw on their faces an evident horror and disgust, and something of the repugnance that one feels at the sight of a venomous snake, half-killed, trailing its bleeding vileness out of sight. By design Lucian tried to make for remote and desolate places, and yet when he had succeeded in touching on the open country, and knew that the icy shadow hovering through the mist was a field, he longed for some sound and murmur of life, and turned again to roads where pale lamps were glimmering, and the dancing flame of firelight shone across the frozen shrubs. And the sight of these homely fires, the thought of affection and consolation waiting by them, stung him the more sharply perhaps because of the contrast with his own chills and weariness and helpless sickness, and chiefly because he knew that he had long closed an everlasting door between his heart and such felicities. If those within had come out and had called him by his name to enter and be comforted, it would have been quite unavailing, since between them and him there was a great gulf fixed. Perhaps for the first time he realized that he had lost the art of humanity forever. He had thought when he closed his ears to the wood whisper and changed the fauns’ singing for the murmur of the streets, the black pools for the shadows and amber light of London, that he had put off the old life, and had turned his soul to healthy activities, but the truth was that he had merely exchanged one drug for another. He could not be human, and he wondered whether there were some drop of the fairy blood in his body that made him foreign and a stranger in the world.

He did not surrender to desolation without repeated struggles. He strove to allure himself to his desk by the promise of some easy task; he would not attempt invention, but he had memoranda and rough jottings of ideas in his notebooks, and he would merely amplify the suggestions ready to his hand. But it was hopeless, again and again it was hopeless. As he read over his notes, trusting that he would find some hint that might light up the dead fires, and kindle again that pure flame of enthusiasm, he found how desperately his fortune had fallen. He could see no light, no color in the lines he had scribbled with eager trembling fingers; he remembered how splendid all these things had been when he wrote them down, but now they were meaningless, faded into grey. The few words he had dashed on to the paper, enraptured at the thought of the happy hours they promised, had become mere jargon, and when he understood the idea it seemed foolish, dull, unoriginal. He discovered something at last that appeared to have a grain of promise, and determined to do his best to put it into shape, but the first paragraph appalled him; it might have been written by an unintelligent schoolboy. He tore the paper in pieces, and shut and locked his desk, heavy despair sinking like lead into his heart. For the rest of that day he lay motionless on the bed, smoking pipe after pipe in the hope of stupefying himself with tobacco fumes. The air in the room became blue and thick with smoke; it was bitterly cold, and he wrapped himself up in his greatcoat and drew the counterpane over him. The night came on and the window darkened, and at last he fell asleep.

He renewed the effort at intervals, only to plunge deeper into misery. He felt the approaches of madness, and knew that his only hope was to walk till he was physically exhausted, so that he might come home almost fainting with fatigue, but ready to fall asleep the moment he got into bed. He passed the mornings in a kind of torpor, endeavoring to avoid thought, to occupy his mind with the pattern of the paper, with the advertisements at the end of a book, with the curious greyness of the light that glimmered through the mist into his room, with the muffled voices that rumbled now and then from the street. He tried to make out the design that had once colored the faded carpet on the floor, and wondered about the dead artist in Japan, the adorner of his bureau. He speculated as to what his thoughts had been as he inserted the rainbow mother-of-pearl and made that great flight of shining birds, dipping their wings as they rose from the reeds, or how he had conceived the lacquer dragons in red gold, and the fantastic houses in the garden of peach-trees. But sooner or later the oppression of his grief returned, the loud shriek and clang of the garden-gate, the warning bell of some passing bicyclist steering through the fog, the noise of his pipe falling to the floor, would suddenly awaken him to the sense of misery. He knew that it was time to go out; he could not bear to sit still and suffer. Sometimes he cut a slice of bread and put it in his pocket, sometimes he trusted to the chance of finding a public-house, where he could have a sandwich and a glass of beer. He turned always from the main streets and lost himself in the intricate suburban byways, willing to be engulfed in the infinite whiteness of the mist.

他把自己从白纸与闲笔那凄凉的凝望中驱赶出来，走进冰封空寂的街道，指望能从心里拔出那块燃烧的炭，火却没有熄。他狂乱地沿着铁硬狰狞的道路疾走，恍惚觉得那些迎面而过、欢欢喜喜赶去赴朋友与暖炉的人们，经过他时都畏缩着退进了雾里。卢西安想象，他那磨难与苦痛之火，必是以某种方式在他周身灼灼可见；他或许正移动在一圈光晕之中，昭示着内里的黑暗与火焰。他当然知道，自己是愁苦得神志昏乱了；那些从雾里朝他隐隐逼近的衣冠楚楚、礼帽光洁的人物，实际上只是冷得发抖；可尽管常识如此，他仍执意觉得自己在他们脸上看见了明白无误的恐怖与憎厌，还有几分人们看见一条毒蛇半死不活、拖着血污的可憎之躯爬出视野时的那种憎恶。卢西安存心往僻远荒凉的地方走；然而一旦当真挨到了旷野，知道了雾中逡巡的那片冰冷暗影是一块田地，他又渴念起人声与生活的低语，重新折回那些路上——苍白的灯微微闪烁，炉火的舞焰照过冻僵的灌木。这些家常炉火的光景，火边守候着的亲情与慰藉的念想，刺得他愈发锋痛：或许因为他自己的寒冷、疲惫与无助的病楚与之恰成对照，而更因为他知道，自己早已在心与这般幸福之间关上了一道永恒的门。倘若屋内的人走出来，唤着他的名字请他进去受一番安慰，也终归无用；因为他们与他之间，隔着一道命定的深渊。他大约是头一回意识到：他已经永远失去了人性的艺术。他原以为，当他塞住耳朵不再听林间的絮语，当他以街市的喧吟换掉法翁（faun）们的歌唱、以伦敦的暗影与琥珀色的灯火换掉黑色的水潭，他已然卸却旧日的生活，把灵魂转向了健全的营生；而真相是，他不过把一种毒品换成了另一种。他做不成人；他不免疑心，自己血液里掺着一滴精灵的血，才使他在世上成了一个异邦人、一个陌路客。

他并非不曾反复挣扎便向凄凉屈服。他设法以一桩轻省任务的许诺，诱自己回到书桌前；他不去尝试凭空创造，但笔记本里存着备忘和粗率的意想速记，他只须把现成在手的提示加以敷演。然而无望，一次又一次都是无望。他重读那些笔记，指望寻得一点暗示，能点亮死灰，重新燃起那纯净的热情之焰，却发现自己的运势坠落得何等凄惨。他以急切颤抖的手指匆匆涂下的那些字句里，他看不出一丝光、一毫色彩；他记得写下这些的时候它们何等辉煌，如今却全无意义，褪成了灰色。当初他满心欢喜地扑到纸上、以为它们许诺着欢乐时辰的那几行字，如今成了十足的呓语；即便懂得了其中的意思，那意思也显得愚蠢、乏味、平庸。他终于发现一样东西似乎还有一星指望，便打定主意尽力将它赋形，可第一段就叫他胆寒；那简直像出自一个愚钝学童的手笔。他把纸撕成碎片，关上书桌落了锁，沉重的绝望如铅沉入心底。那天余下的时光，他一动不动躺在床上，一斗接一斗地抽烟，指望用烟草的烟雾把自己熏得麻木。屋里的空气变得又蓝又浊；天寒彻骨，他裹上厚呢大衣，又拉过床罩盖在身上。夜色降临，窗户暗了下去，他终于睡着了。

他隔三差五重振旗鼓再试，结果只是更深地坠入悲苦。他觉出疯狂在步步逼近，知道自己唯一的指望，就是走到筋疲力尽，好回到家时几乎累得发晕，一上床便能睡去。他的上午都在一种昏沉里打发，竭力避开思想，让心神去忙活墙纸的花样、一本书末页的广告、透过雾霭渗进屋里来的那种古怪的灰色微光、街上不时隆隆传来的喑哑人语。他试着辨认地板上那张褪色地毯曾经有过的图样，悬想日本那位故去的艺人、他那写字台的装点者。他揣想那人嵌进虹彩的珠母、作出那一大群熠熠生辉的鸟儿——从芦苇间振翅而起时翼尖低回——时怀着怎样的心思；或者，他是怎样构想出红金色的髹漆蟠龙，和桃树园中那些奇幻的屋宇。可迟早他的悲愁总要重新压上心头；花园铁门刺耳的尖叫与哐啷、哪个骑车人在雾里穿行时的警铃、烟斗跌落地板的声响，都会猛然把他唤醒到苦楚的知觉里。他知道该出门了；他忍受不了干坐着受罪。有时他切一片面包揣进衣袋，有时就指望碰上一家酒馆，能吃一份三明治、喝一杯啤酒。他总是避开大街，把自己迷失在郊外错综的小巷里，甘愿被雾霭的无穷白色吞没。

The roads had stiffened into iron ridges, the fences and trees were glittering with frost crystals, everything was of strange and altered aspect. Lucian walked on and on through the maze, now in a circle of shadowy villas, awful as the buried streets of Herculaneum, now in lanes dipping onto open country, that led him past great elm-trees whose white boughs were all still, and past the bitter lonely fields where the mist seemed to fade away into grey darkness. As he wandered along these unfamiliar and ghastly paths he became the more convinced of his utter remoteness from all humanity, he allowed that grotesque suggestion of there being something visibly amiss in his outward appearance to grow upon him, and often he looked with a horrible expectation into the faces of those who passed by, afraid lest his own senses gave him false intelligence, and that he had really assumed some frightful and revolting shape. It was curious that, partly by his own fault, and largely, no doubt, through the operation of mere coincidence, he was once or twice strongly confirmed in this fantastic delusion. He came one day into a lonely and unfrequented byway, a country lane falling into ruin, but still fringed with elms that had formed an avenue leading to the old manor-house. It was now the road of communication between two far outlying suburbs, and on these winter nights lay as black, dreary, and desolate as a mountain track. Soon after the frost began, a gentleman had been set upon in this lane as he picked his way between the corner where the bus had set him down, and his home where the fire was blazing, and his wife watched the clock. He was stumbling uncertainly through the gloom, growing a little nervous because the walk seemed so long, and peering anxiously for the lamp at the end of his street, when the two footpads rushed at him out of the fog. One caught him from behind, the other struck him with a heavy bludgeon, and as he lay senseless they robbed him of his watch and money, and vanished across the fields. The next morning all the suburb rang with the story; the unfortunate merchant had been grievously hurt, and wives watched their husbands go out in the morning with sickening apprehension, not knowing what might happen at night.
