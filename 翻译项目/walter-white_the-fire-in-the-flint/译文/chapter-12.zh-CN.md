## XII / 十二

===Original===
From Judge Stevenson’s office Kenneth went directly to tell Jane of the interview. So absorbed was he in contemplation of the wider vision of the problem he was attacking which the judge’s words had given him, he forgot to telephone her to ask if it was agreeable for him to call at so unconventional an hour. He found her clad in a bungalow apron busily cleaning house and singing as she worked.

They sat on the steps of the back porch while he told her all that had been said. Taken out of his preoccupation with his own affairs, Kenneth had shaken off his negative air and now he talked convincingly of their plans. Jane said nothing until he had finished.

“That’s fine!” she exclaimed when he had ended. “Even if Judge Stevenson is doubtful of how much we can accomplish, we can do something. Now all that remains is for you to present your plan﻿—”

“Not mine, yours,” he corrected.

“No, it will have to be yours,” she answered. “You know how folks are in the South﻿—they think all that women can do is cook and keep house and bear children. If you want the thing to go, it’ll be best to make them think it’s your scheme.”

从史蒂文森法官的办公室出来，肯尼思径直去找简，把这次会面告诉她。法官的一番话，使他对正在着手解决的这个问题有了一个更开阔的视野，他沉浸在这一思索里，竟忘了先打个电话给她，问问这么个不合常礼的钟点登门她方不方便。他到时，见她穿着一件家常的围裙罩衣，正忙着打扫屋子，一边干活一边唱歌。

他俩在后廊的台阶上坐下，他把人家说的那一切原原本本告诉了她。肯尼思从对自己那一摊事的牵挂里抽身出来，已甩掉了原先那股消极的气色，如今讲起他们的计划来，说得煞有说服力。简一言不发，直等他说完。

「好极了！」他话音一落她便叫起来。「哪怕史蒂文森法官对我们能办成多少心存疑虑，我们总能做点什么。眼下剩下的事，就是你把你的计划端出来——」

「不是我的，是你的，」他纠正她。

「不，非得是你的不可，」她答道。「你是知道南方人是什么做派的——他们觉着女人能做的，无非是做饭、持家、生孩子。你要这事办得成，顶好让他们以为这是你的主意。」

===Original===
Kenneth demurred, but in vain. She would have it no other way. She felt no jealousy. She knew of the peculiar Southern prejudice which relegated women to a position of eternal inferiority. Though she felt the injustice of such arbitrary assumptions, she did not resent it. Like all women, coloured women, she realized that most of the spirit of revolt against the wrongs inflicted on her race had been born in the breasts of coloured women. She knew, and in that knowledge was content, that most of the work of the churches and societies and other organizations which had done so much towards welding the Negro into a racial unit had been done by women. It was amusing to see men, vain creatures that they are, preen themselves on what they had done. It was not so amusing when they, in their pride, sought to belittle what the women had done and take all the credit to themselves. Oh, well, what did it matter? The end was the all-important thing﻿—not the means. Jane appreciated Kenneth’s thoughtfulness and felt no tinge of jealousy if her idea﻿—their idea﻿—should be a success in forming societies to help poor, helpless Negroes out of the morass in which they were bogged. Of such material has the coloured woman been made by adversity.

===Chinese===
肯尼思推让，却属徒劳。她不肯有第二种安排。她心里没有半点嫉妒。她知道南方那种特有的偏见，把女人打发到一个永远低人一等的位置上。她虽感到这种武断成见的不公，却并不因此怀恨。和所有女人一样，和所有有色人（coloured）女人一样，她明白：反抗本族所受种种冤屈的那股反抗精神，大多都诞生在有色人女人的胸膛里。她知道——也因这一知道而心安——那些教会、社团以及其他种种组织所做的事，那些在把黑人（Negro）锻造成一个种族共同体上起了莫大作用的事，大半都是女人做出来的。看着那些男人——他们原是些爱虚荣的造物——在自己所做的事上自鸣得意，倒也好笑。可等到他们因着那份骄傲，竟要贬低女人们所做的，把一切功劳都揽到自己身上，那便不那么好笑了。唉，这又有什么要紧？要紧的是结局——不是手段。简感念肯尼思的体贴，纵然她的主意——他们的主意——果然办成了，办成了这些社团、把那些穷苦无告的黑人从他们深陷的泥沼里搭救出来，她也不会有半点嫉妒之心。磨难，便是由这样的材料，把有色人女人造就出来的。

She watched Kenneth as he told her the developments of which he had thought, the details he had worked out. Each day, it seemed to her, Kenneth became more keenly alive each day saw a brighter sparkle in his eyes, a springiness in his step that had not been there before. There are many men who could willingly have followed﻿—and do follow﻿—without revolt or much inward conflict a course of self-abnegation such as he had mapped out for himself. Not so, however, with Kenneth. He was almost puritanical in his devotion to the fixed moral code he had worked out for his own guidance. It was not a superimposed one, but an integral part of his very being. Nothing could have induced him to surrender to deliberate malice or guile or what he considered dishonesty or cowardice. His was a simple nature, free from the barnacles of pettiness which encumber the average man. He was not essentially religious in the accepted meaning of the word. He believed, though he had not thought much on the subject of religion, so immersed had he been in his beloved profession, in some sort of a God. Of what form or shape this being was, he did not know. He had more or less accepted the beliefs his environment had forced upon him. He doubted the malignity of the God described by most of the ministers he had heard. As a matter of fact, he was rather repelled and nauseated by the religion of the modern Church. Narrow, intolerant of contrary opinion, prying into the lives and affairs of its communicants with which it had no concern, its energies concentrated on raising money and not on saving souls, of little real help to intelligent people to enable them to live more useful lives here on earth, and centering instead on a mysterious and problematical life after death, he felt the Church of Jesus Christ had so little of the spirit of the Christ that he had little patience with it. He went to services more as a perfunctory duty than through any deep-rooted belief that he could get any real help from them in meeting the problems of life he faced. He bore the Church no grudge or ill will﻿—it simply was not a factor in the life of today as he saw it.

===Chinese===
她望着肯尼思，听他讲他所想到的进展，他所理出的细节。在她看来，肯尼思一天比一天活得更为热切，每一天都见他的眼里有一抹更亮的火花，脚步里有一股从前没有的轻快。世上有许多男人，本可以心甘情愿地追随——也确在追随——像他为自己规划的那样一条自我克制的路，既不反抗，内心也无多大挣扎。肯尼思却不是这样。他对自己所立、用以自率的那套固定道德准则，近乎清教徒般地恪守。那准则不是外加于他的，而是他整个存在里不可分的一部分。任凭什么也不能诱使他向蓄意的恶意与诡诈屈服，不能向他视为不诚实或怯懦之事屈服。他的天性简朴，没有那种附庸在寻常人身上的琐碎的藤壶。就「宗教」一词通行的意义而言，他本质上并不算虔诚。他沉浸于心爱的本行之中，对宗教这题目想得不多，但他相信冥冥中有那么一个上帝。这存在是何形何状，他并不知道。他多多少少接受了环境强加于他的那些信仰。他怀疑他所听过的大多数牧师所描绘的那个上帝，是否真怀着那种恶意。事实上，现代教会的宗教倒叫他相当反感、乃至作呕。狭隘，容不得半点异见，爱窥探那些与它毫不相干的信徒的私生活与私事，心思全用在募款上而不是救灵魂上，对于有心人在这世上过更有用的人生给不了多少实在的帮助，反倒把重心放在死后那缥缈而可疑的来生——他觉得耶稣基督的教会里，基督的精神实在所剩无几，他对它也就没什么耐心。他去做礼拜，更多的是尽一桩敷衍的本分，而非出于任何深信能从中得到真正的助益、去对付他所面对的人生难题。他对教会并不记恨、也不怀恶意——只是在他看来，它压根儿算不上今日生活里的一个因素。

===Original===
Nevertheless he had a deep religious or, better, an ethical sense. When he was about to return to Central City, that ethical code had been adapted to conditions he expected to find there. It was galling to him to accept a position of subserviency to things he knew were unjust and wrong, tacitly to admit his inferiority to men to whom he knew he was superior in morals and training and in all the decencies of life, solely because of the mere accident that they had been born with skins which were white and he with one which was not white. When doubts had assailed him, he had quieted or salved his conscience by the constant reminder that he was following such a course for greater eventual good. On his return, when he had found a course such as he had charted for himself was becoming increasingly difficult, he had refused to face the facts his mind told him were true and had plunged more deeply into his work, seeking in it an opiate. Only when Jane had confronted him with the utter futility of his course and had, in effect, accused him of being a moral quitter in considering only himself and blinding himself to the far greater problems of those so closely bound to him by race, did his eyes begin to be opened. Wearied of illusory hopes of peace through compromise, he had grasped the tangible reality of work towards a definite end, through means which he had created and which he would guide and develop as far as he could. With the buoyant hopes and ambitions of the young, especially of the very young, he felt that he had already created that which he was hoping to create.

然而他有一种深沉的宗教感——或者更确切地说，一种伦理感。在他即将返回中央城之际，他把这套伦理准则调整得合乎他预料将在那儿遇到的境况。要他去屈居于他明知不公不当之事之下，要他心照不宣地承认自己低人一等——低那些他在道德、教养以及为人处世的种种体面上都明知胜过一筹的人一等——仅仅因为他们碰巧生了一身白皮肤、而他生了一身非白的皮肤，这件事让他深感屈辱。当疑虑袭来时，他便时时提醒自己：走这条路，是为了日后更大的善——以此安抚、宽慰自己的良心。回乡之后，当他发现自己原先所拟的那条路越来越难走时，他不愿正视他心里明知为真的事实，反倒更深地一头扎进工作里，要在其中寻一剂麻醉。直到简拿他这条路的全然无用来当面诘问他，实际上等于指责他是个道德上的逃兵——只顾自己、对那些因种族而与他紧密相连的人那远为沉重的难题视而不见——他的眼睛才开始睁开。他已厌倦了靠妥协换取和平的虚妄希望，转而抓住了一个切实可触的现实：朝着一个明确的目标做事，所用的法子是他自己创造、要由他自己尽可能去引导、去发展的。带着年轻人、尤其是年纪尚轻之人那股蓬勃的希望与抱负，他觉得自己已经把那想要创造的东西创造出来了。

===Original===
Like a traveller who has lost his way in a dense forest, an indefinable restlessness had pervaded his being and made him sorely discontented. Now that he had found what seemed the path which would lead him into the clear, open air, the clouds of doubt and perplexity were cleared away just as the bright sun, as it bursts forth after a shower in spring, drives away the moisture in the air.

They sat there in the warm sunlight of early summer, dreaming and planning all the great things they were going to accomplish. It had rained earlier in the morning and from the ground rose a misty vapour. The odour of warm wet earth mingled with the aroma of the flowers. Hens scratched industriously for food to feed the cluster of tiny chicks around them. A cat sneaking along the fence slyly crept near. With a great fluttering of wings and raucous cackling, the hens drove him away. From afar off came the voices of two women, resting for a minute from their morning toil, gossiping with much loud laughter. It was a peaceful, restful scene. To Kenneth as he sat there, problems seemed remote and out of place in that place where all was so calm.

===Chinese===
像一个在密林中迷了路的旅人，一种说不清的不安曾弥漫他全身，叫他满心苦闷。如今他似乎找到了那条能引他走到开阔明亮处的路，疑虑与困惑的阴云便一扫而空——正如春日一阵雨后，明媚的阳光骤然迸射，把空气里的潮气驱散殆尽。

他俩就那样坐在初夏温暖的阳光里，梦想着、筹划着他们将要成就的那一切大事。早上早些时下过一场雨，地里升起一层薄薄的雾气。温热湿润的泥土气息和着花香混在一处。母鸡们勤快地刨着食，喂身边那一窝小鸡雏。一只猫顺着篱笆鬼鬼祟祟地摸近前来。母鸡们一阵猛烈的扑翅、粗嘎的咯咯大叫，把它赶跑了。远远传来两个女人的说话声，她们正歇一口气、暂缓早晨的劳作，大着声说笑闲聊。这是一幅安宁、恬静的画面。肯尼思坐在那里，觉得一切难题都显得遥远，在这般沉静的所在显得格格不入。

After Kenneth had gone, Jane sat for some time struggling with the problem she was facing. What was she to do? As a little girl she had loved Kenneth with a simple, childlike love though he, with the infinite difference of eight years of age, had paid no attention to her. She was not at all sure now of the nature of her feelings towards him. She liked him, it is true, but when it came to anything deeper than that, she was not so certain. She had been told, and had always believed, that love came as a blinding, searing, devastating passion which swept everything before it. She felt none of this passion and experienced no bit of that complete surrender which she had believed was a part of the thing called love. Jane was much in the position of the sinner on the mourner’s bench who had been told that when he became a Christian, angels and all sorts of heavenly apparitions would miraculously appear before him, and, seeing none, feels that he is being cheated.

Jane had seen in Kenneth’s eyes that soon he would make some sort of declaration of his love. What was she going to say? She did not know.﻿ ﻿…

So pleasant had it been sitting there in the warm sunlight talking with Jane, Kenneth had forgotten the time. Entering his office, he found half a dozen patients waiting somewhat impatiently for him. As he entered his private office, he heard old Mrs. Amos, in her chronic quarrelsomeness, mutter:

“Dat’s just what I allus say. Soon’s a nigger begin to get up in the world, he thinks hisself better’n us po’ folks. Thinks he can treat us any way he please.”

The next evening the seven men met again at Mr. Wilson’s. Kenneth stopped by for Mr. Phillips, but he did not see Jane. The Reverend Stewart, Tucker, Tracy, Swann, and Mr. Wilson sat awaiting them. Tom Tracy was exhibiting, somewhat proudly it seemed, a note he had found tacked to his door that morning. It was crudely lettered in red ink on a cheap-quality paper. It read:

**Nigger! You’ve ben talking too dam much! If you don’t shut your mouth we will shut it for you! And for good! Let this be a warning to you. Next time we will act!**

**K.K.K.**

Beneath the three initials was a crude skull and crossbones. Though all seven of the men knew that the warning was not to be disregarded, that it might possibly portend a serious attempt on the life of Tracy, that any or all of them present might receive a similar grim reminder of the ill will of the hooded band, there was a complete absence of fear as they sat around the table and conjectured as to the possible result of the warning. The calmness with which they accepted the omen of trouble would probably have amazed the senders of the warning. Perhaps the clearest indication of how little the South realizes the changes that have taken place in the Negro is this recrudescence of the Klan. Where stark terror followed in the wake of the Klan rides of the seventies, the net result of similar rides today is a more determined union of Negroes against all that the Klan stands for, tinctured with a mild amusement at the Klan’s grotesque antics. With such a reminder before them, the seven felt there was greater need than ever before for organization for mutual protection.

They discussed means of protecting Tracy, but he assured them he was amply able to take care of himself. He had sent his parents that day to stay with friends until the trouble had blown over, telling them nothing of the warning, as he did not want them to be worried by it. Two of his friends had agreed to stay with him at night. He was well supplied with ammunition and was sure the three of them could successfully repel any attack that might be made upon him. Such trying periods have happened to Negroes so frequently in the South that they have become inured to them. The subject was soon dropped.

Then Kenneth presented his plan. He outlined in detail how the society should be organized. He proposed that the first lodge be formed at Ashland, then gradually spread until there was a branch in every section of the county. They left until later the problem of extending the society’s activities to other parts of Georgia and the neighbouring States. Each member would be required to pay an initiation fee of one dollar. Men would pay monthly dues of fifty cents each, women twenty-five. The sums thus secured were to be pooled. Half of the amount was to purchase supplies like sugar, flour, shoes, clothing, fertilizer, seeds, farm implements, and the other things needed to satisfy the simple wants of the members. To make up any deficit, Kenneth and Mr. Phillips agreed to lend money that the supplies might be purchased for cash, effecting thereby a considerable saving. The other half was to be used as the nucleus of a defence fund with which a test case might be made in the courts when any member was unable to secure a fair settlement with his landlord.

===Chinese===
他们商量着怎样保护特雷西，他却向大家保证，自己完全有本事照料自己。他当天已把父母送去朋友家住，等这场风波过去再回来；那道警告他一点也没告诉他们，因为他不愿他们为此担惊受怕。他的两个朋友已答应夜里来陪他。他的弹药充足，深信他们三个人足以击退任何可能向他发动的袭击。这样的关口，对南方的黑人来说发生得实在太频繁了，他们早已习以为常。这话题很快便搁下了。

接着，肯尼思端出了他的计划。他详详细细地勾画出这个团体该如何组织。他提议，第一个分部先在阿什兰成立起来，再渐渐铺开，直到县里每一片地方都有一个支会。至于把这个团体的活动扩展到佐治亚其他地区及邻近各州的事，他们留待日后解决。每个成员须缴一元入会费。男会员每人每月缴月捐五角，女会员二角五分。这样收上来的款项要合在一处。其中一半用来采购物资——糖、面粉、鞋、衣物、肥料、种子、农具，以及会员们简朴所需的其他种种。若有亏空，由肯尼思和菲利普斯先生答应借钱填补，好让这些物资能用现金买进，借此省下一笔可观的花销。另一半则用作一笔辩护基金的底子，日后哪位会员与东家结算时不能得到公道，便可拿它到法庭上去打一个试验性的官司。

===Original===
Similarly were other details presented and discussed and adopted or modified. A name had to be chosen. Kenneth would have preferred a short, simple one, but here he was overruled. That it might appeal to the simple, illiterate class to which most of the prospective members belonged, a sonorous, impressive name was necessary. They decided on “The National Negro Farmers’ Cooperative and Protective League.”

At first the plan was considered a bit too ambitious, but as Kenneth warmed up to it in presenting it as simply and forcefully as he could, the objections, one by one, were overcome. One change, however, had to be made. It came from Hiram Tucker.

“Ain’t you figgerin’ on havin’ no signs and passwords and a grip like dey have with de Odd Fellers and de Masons and de Knights of Pythias?” he asked.

“I didn’t think that was necessary,” replied Kenneth.

“Well, lemme tell you somethin’, son. Ef you figgers on gettin’ a big passel of these cullud folks ’round here to jine in with us, you’ll have t’ have some ’ficials with scrumptious names, and passwords and grips. Dese here ign’ant folks needs somethin’ like dat to catch their ’magination. If you put dat in, they’ll jine like flies ’round molasses.”

===Chinese===
其他种种细节，也都这样一一摆出来，加以讨论，或采纳、或修改。还得给它起个名字。肯尼思本想取一个简短朴素的，可在这件事上他被推翻了。要让它对大多数未来成员所属的那个质朴、不识字的阶层有吸引力，一个响亮、气派的名字是必要的。他们议定，叫「全国黑人农户合作保护同盟（The National Negro Farmers’ Cooperative and Protective League）」。

起初大家觉得这计划未免有点好高骛远，可肯尼思越是讲得投入，尽可能把话说得浅显而有力量，那些反对意见便一条一条地被化解了。不过，有一处非改不可。这意见是海勒姆·塔克提出来的。

「你就不打算弄点暗号、口令，还有什么握手礼，像那个什么奇异兄弟会啦、共济会啦、毕达哥拉斯骑士团[2]啦他们那一套？」他问。

「我没觉得有这个必要，」肯尼思答道。

「嘿，那我跟你说，孩子。你要是指望着把这一带一大帮子有色人招进来入伙，你就得弄几个名头唬人的职衔，还得有口令、有握手礼。这帮子不识字的人哪，就吃这一套，非得有这玩意儿才勾得住他们的心思。你把这写进去，他们入起会来，就跟苍蝇围着糖蜜转似的。」

===Original===
Kenneth had hoped that the society would be run on a dignified and intelligent basis, but he realized that Hiram Tucker might be right after all. Most of the sharecroppers were ignorant﻿—at least, illiterate. Mere show and pomp and colourful uniforms and high-sounding names played a large part in their lives, which, after all, wasn’t so much a racial as a human trait. Hadn’t the Ku Klux Klan outdone, in absurdity of name and ceremony and dress, anything that Negroes had ever even thought of?

This question was disposed of, after more discussion, by the adoption of Hiram Tucker’s suggestion. Kenneth was appointed to work out the details of organization, and the meeting adjourned. The National Negro Farmer’s Cooperative and Protective League had been born.

===Chinese===
肯尼思原本指望这个团体能在一个体面而讲理智的基础上运转，可他意识到，到底还是海勒姆·塔克说得对。那些佃农大多蒙昧无知——至少是不识字。光是排场、铺张、花花绿绿的制服和响亮的名头，在他们生活里就占了很大的分量，而这说到底，与其说是哪一族的特性，不如说是人的天性。那三K党在名号、仪式和衣着上的荒唐，岂不是黑人连想都想不到的吗？

这一问题，经过进一步讨论，因采纳海勒姆·塔克的建议而告解决。肯尼思被委派去把组织的各项细节拟订出来，会议便散了。全国黑人农户合作保护同盟（The National Negro Farmers’ Cooperative and Protective League）[3]就此诞生了。
