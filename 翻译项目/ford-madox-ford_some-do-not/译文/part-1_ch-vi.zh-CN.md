Tietjens lit a pipe beside the stile, having first meticulously cleaned out the bowl and the stem with a surgical needle, in his experience the best of all pipe-cleaners, since, made of German silver, it is flexible, won’t corrode and is indestructible. He wiped off methodically, with a great dock-leaf, the glutinous brown products of burnt tobacco, the young woman, as he was aware, watching him from behind his back. As soon as he had restored the surgical needle to the notebook in which it lived, and had put the notebook into its bulky pocket, Miss Wannop moved off down the path: it was only suited for Indian file, and had on the left hand a ten-foot, untrimmed quicken hedge, the hawthorn blossoms just beginning to blacken at the edges and small green haws to show. On the right the grass was above knee high and bowed to those that passed. The sun was exactly vertical; the chaffinchs said: “Pink! pink!”: the young woman had an agreeable back.
This, Tietjens thought, is England! A man and a maid walk through Kentish grass fields: the grass ripe for the scythe. The man honourable, clean, upright; the maid virtuous, clean, vigorous: he of good birth; she of birth quite as good; each filled with a too good breakfast that each could yet capably digest. Each come just from an admirably appointed establishment: a table surrounded by the best people: their promenade sanctioned, as it were, by the Church﻿—two clergy﻿—the State: two Government officials; by mothers, friends, old maids.﻿ ﻿… Each knew the names of birds that piped and grasses that bowed: chaffinch, greenfinch, yellow-ammer (*not*, my dear, hammer; *ammer* from the Middle High German for “finch”), garden warbler, Dartford warbler, pied-wagtail, known as “dishwasher.” (These *charming* local dialect names.) Marguerites over the grass, stretching in an infinite white blaze: grasses purple in a haze to the far distant hedgerow: coltsfoot, wild white clover, sainfoin, Italian rye grass (all technical names that the best people must know: the best grass mixture for permanent pasture on the Wealden loam). In the hedge: Our lady’s bedstraw: dead-nettle: bachelor’s button (but in *Sussex* they call it ragged robin, my dear): So interesting! cowslip (paigle, you know, from old French *pasque*, meaning Easter): burr, burdock (farmer that thy wife may thrive, but not burr and burdock wive!)

“被芒特比的客名单除名，不好受，”她说。“他们家的聚会有趣。可我老得不在乎了，再说他们惦记我的谈锋，胜过我惦记他们的。当然，我护着我的女儿，对着那群猫猴。当然，我死心塌地护着瓦伦丁。她就是同有妇之夫同居、生私生孩子，我也护她。可我不赞成，我不赞成女权分子：我鄙夷她们的目标：我憎恶她们的手段。我认为年轻姑娘不该同陌生男人搭话。瓦伦丁同你搭了话，瞧给你惹来多少麻烦。我不赞成。我是女人：可我是自己闯出来的；别的女人肯干、有力气，也闯得出来。我不赞成！可别以为我会回头咬任何一个女权分子，单个的、成群的；我的瓦伦丁或任何别的。别以为我会说一句将来传得出去的反对她们的话——你不会去传的。或者写一个字反对她们。不，我是女人，我站在我的性别一边！”
她劲头十足地起身：
“我得去写小说了，”她说。“周一那期的稿子今晚要赶火车寄走。你进我书房去：瓦伦丁会给你纸、墨水、十二种笔尖。沃诺普教授的书满屋都是。瓦伦丁要在凹室里打字，你将就些。我两部连载同时写着，一部打字稿，一部手稿。”
蒂金斯说：
“那您呢！”

“我告诉你，一点不额外添麻烦。瓦伦丁和我向来自己铺床。我们不喜欢外人动我们贴身的东西。这一带我们要多少帮手有多少，多出三倍都请得到。我们在这儿人缘好。你们添的活，添的帮手就抵了。我们请得起仆人。可瓦伦丁和我喜欢夜里家里只有我们俩。我们非常亲。”
她走到门口，又飘回来补一句：
“你不知道，我心里总放不下那个可怜的女人和她的丈夫。咱们全都得尽力帮他们。”随后她一惊，喊道：“哎呀老天，我耽误你干活了……书房在那边，穿过那道门。”
她匆匆穿过另一道门，想必是沿着走廊去了，一路喊：
“瓦伦丁！瓦伦丁！去书房陪克里斯托弗。马上……马……”她的声音远去了。
