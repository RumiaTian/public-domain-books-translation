## XXIV / 第二十四章

===Original===
As I have said, Palafox pulled us together; and although we abandoned almost all of the Calle de Pabostre, we remained strong in the Puerta Quemada. If the battle was bloody until three, the hour when we centred in the Plaza de la Magdalena, it was not less bloody there until night. The French began to raise works in the houses ruined by the mines, and it was curious to see how among the masses of rubbish and beams small armed squares and covered ways were made and platforms to connect the artillery. That was a battle which every moment appeared less and less like any other known warfare.

From this new phase of contest resulted an advantage for us and a hindrance for the French. The demolition of the houses permitted them to place some new pieces, but the men were unprotected. To our misfortune, we could not avail ourselves of this because of the explosions. Fright made us think the danger multiplied a hundredfold, when in reality it was diminished. Not wishing to do less than they in that fiery duel, the Saragossans began to burn the houses in the Calle de Pabostre which they could not hold.

Besiegers and besieged, desirous of coming to an end of this, and not being able to attain it in such intricate burrowing warfare, began to destroy, one side by mining, the other by burning, remaining unprotected like the gladiator who throws away his shield.

===Chinese===
正如我前面所说，帕拉福克斯把我们收拢到一处；我们虽然放弃了帕博斯特雷街几乎全部地段，在克马达门却依旧守得牢固。到三点钟我们收缩集结于拉马格达莱纳广场（Plaza de la Magdalena）那一刻为止，这一仗打得血肉横飞；此后在那里直到入夜，惨烈也分毫未减。法军开始在被坑道炸毁的房屋里修筑工事；说来稀奇，但见成堆的瓦砾和梁木之间，辟出了一个个小小的武装方阵、一道道掩蔽道，还搭起了接连炮位的平台。这一场仗，每时每刻都越来越不像人们所知的任何一种战争。

战事的这一新阶段，于我们是便利，于法军是阻碍。房屋一经拆毁，他们固然得以安放几门新炮，操炮的人却暴露无遗。倒霉的是，由于接连的爆炸，我们无法利用这个破绽。恐惧使我们觉得危险陡增了百倍，实际上它倒是减小了。萨拉戈萨人不肯在这场烈火对决中甘居人后，便动手焚烧帕博斯特雷街上那些守不住的房屋。

围城者与被围者都巴望早点了结，却又在这般盘根错节的掘地战争中了结不得，便都动手毁灭起来——一方用坑道爆破，一方用纵火焚烧——双双不设遮护，恰似丢掉盾牌的角斗士。

===Original===
What an afternoon! What a night! Arriving here, I pause, wearied and breathless. My recollections are obscured, dimmed as my thoughts and my feelings were dimmed on that dreadful night. There came indeed a moment when being unable to resist longer, my body, like that of others of my comrades who had the fortune or misfortune to be still alive, dragged itself back across the gutters, stumbling over unburied bodies that seemed less than human among the debris. My feelings had flung me into an extreme of delirium, and I did not clearly know where I was. My idea of living was a confused, vague mixture of unheard-of miseries. It did not seem as if it was day, because in so many places the murk hung low, obscuring everything. Nor could I think it night, for flames like those we imagine in hell reddened the city on every side.

I only know that I dragged myself, stepping upon bodies, some dead and some still moving, and that farther on, always farther on, I thought I might find a piece of bread and a mouthful of water. What horrible mental dejection! What hunger! What thirst! I saw many running swiftly. I cried out to them. I saw their strange shadows throwing grotesque figures upon the neighboring walls. They were going and coming, I know not whence nor where. I was not the only one who, with body and soul exhausted after so many hours of fighting, had given out completely. Many others who had not the steel nerves of the Aragonese were dragging themselves along like myself, and we begged one another for a little water. Some, more fortunate than the rest, had the strength to look about among the corpses and find crusts of rations not eaten, fragments of meat, cold and dirty on the ground, which they devoured with avidity.

Somewhat revived, we went on looking, and I took my part of the tidbits of the feast. I did not know if I was wounded. Some of those who were talking with me, telling me of their dreadful hunger and thirst, had terrible wounds and burns and contusions. At last we came to some women who gave us water to drink, although it was muddy and warm. We disputed over the jug, and then in the hands of one of the dead we found a kerchief containing two dried sardines and little cakes. Encouraged by these repeated finds, we went on pillaging, and at last the little which we were able to eat, and, more than anything else, the dirty water we drank, gave us back a little strength.

===Chinese===
好一个下午！好一个夜晚！写到这里，我疲惫已极，喘息不定，只得停笔。我的回忆昏昧了，正如在那个可怕的夜晚，我的思绪与情感一般昏昧。确曾有那么一个时刻，我再也支撑不住；我的身子——同我那些有幸亦或不幸还活着的伙伴的身子一样——拖着爬过一道道阴沟，跌跌撞撞，绊在那些未及掩埋的尸身上，它们夹在残砖碎瓦间，几乎不成人形了。情绪把我抛进了谵妄的绝境，我不很清楚自己身在何处。我对活着的意念，成了一团混沌，含混地搅和着种种闻所未闻的苦楚。说这是白天吧，不像，因为黑沉沉的浊雾在那么多地方低低压着，遮蔽了一切；说这是黑夜吧，也不能，因为恰似我们想象中地狱的烈焰，从四面八方映红了全城。

我只记得自己在爬，脚底下踩着尸身，有的已死，有的还在动；只记得再往前，一直往前，我总以为能寻着一小块面包、一口水。心头何等可怕的颓丧！何等的饿！何等的渴！我望见许多人飞跑而过，我朝他们呼喊。我望见他们诡异的影子在近旁的墙上投下奇形怪状的身形。他们来来去去，我不知从哪里来、往哪里去。苦战了那么多钟头、身心俱竭而彻底垮掉的，并非我一人。还有许多没有阿拉贡人那副钢铁神经的人，也同我一样拖着身子爬行，我们彼此乞讨一点水。有些人运气好些，还有力气在死尸中间翻拣，找到没吃过的口粮硬皮、掉在地上又冷又脏的碎肉，便贪婪地吞吃下去。

缓过一点劲来，我们继续搜寻，我也在这场宴席上分得了一份珍馐。我不知道自己是否挂了彩。同我搭话的那几个人，一面诉说着他们可怕的饥渴，身上带着骇人的伤口、烧伤和瘀伤。最后我们遇到几个女人，给了我们水喝，虽然是浑的、温的。我们为着水罐你争我夺，随后又在一个死人手里发现一块手帕，里头包着两条干沙丁鱼和几块小饼。这一连串的意外所得令我们壮起胆来，继续搜掠；到头来，能吃下的那一点点东西，尤其是喝下去的脏水，总算给我们挣回了几分气力。

===Original===
I now felt myself able to walk a little, although with difficulty. I saw that my clothing was all soaked with blood. Feeling a lively smarting in my right arm, I supposed that I was severely wounded; but the hurt turned out to be an insignificant contusion, and the stains on my clothing came from creeping along through the pools of blood and mud.

I could now think clearly again. I could see plainly, and could hear distinctly the shouts and the hurried footsteps, the cannon-shots near and afar in dreadful dialogue. Their crashings here and yonder seemed like questions and replies.

The burning went on. There was a dense cloud over the city formed of dust and smoke, which, with the splendor of the flames, revealed horrible unearthly scenes like those of dreams.

===Chinese===
这时我觉得自己能走上几步了，尽管颇为吃力。我看见浑身衣裳都被血浸透了。右臂一阵火辣辣的刺痛，我只当自己伤得不轻；细看之下，那不过是无关紧要的一处瘀伤，衣上的血渍，是匍匐爬行时蹭过一洼洼血水泥泞沾上的。

我这时又能清醒地思考了。看得真切，也听得分明：呼喊声，匆促的脚步声，远处近处的炮声，在进行一场可怖的对话。这边的轰响与那边的炸裂，俨然一问一答。

大火还在烧。城市上空罩着尘埃与浓烟凝成的沉沉一幕，它连同烈焰的辉光，映现出种种可怖而非人间的景象，宛如梦中所见。

===Original===
The mangled houses, with their windows and openings glaring with the light like hellish eyes, the projecting angles of the smoking ruins, and the burning beams formed a spectacle less sinister than that of those leaping and unwearied figures that did not cease to move about here and there, almost in the centre of the flames. They were the peasants of Saragossa, who were still fighting with the French, and disputing with them every hand’s breadth of this hell.

I found myself in the Calle de Puerta Quemada. That which I have described was seen by looking in two directions from the Seminary, and from the entrance of the Calle de Pabostre. I went on a few steps, but fell again, overcome by fatigue. A priest, seeing me covered with blood, came up to me and began to talk to me of the future life, and of the eternal rewards destined for those who die for their country. He told me that I was not wounded; but that hunger, weariness, and thirst had prostrated me, and that I seemed to have the early symptoms of the epidemic. Then the good friar, in whom I recognized at once Father Mateo del Busto, seated himself beside me, sighing deeply.

“I can keep up no longer. I believe that I am going to die.”

===Chinese===
残破的房屋，窗洞门口喷吐着火光，宛如一只只地狱的眼睛；冒烟的废墟支棱出的墙角，燃烧的梁木——这一切构成的景象，其狰狞还逊于那些腾跃不倦的身影：他们几乎就在火焰正中，来来去去，穿梭不停，没有片刻止歇。那是萨拉戈萨的乡民，他们还在同法军厮杀，同他们争夺这片地狱里的每一掌之地。

我发觉自己正在克马达门街（Calle de Puerta Quemada）上。我方才描摹的种种景象，从神学院那边朝两个方向望去可以看见，从帕博斯特雷街口望去也可以看见。我朝前走了几步，又被疲惫压得跌倒在地。一位神父见我浑身是血，走上前来，开始对我讲来世，讲为国捐躯者命定得享的永恒报偿。他对我说我并没有受伤，只是饥饿、劳顿与干渴把我撂倒了，而且我似乎已露出时疫的初期症状。随后这位好修士——我一眼认出，正是马特奥·德尔·布斯托神父——在我身旁坐下，深深叹息。

“我撑不住了。我想我就要死了。”

“Is your reverence wounded?” I asked, seeing a linen cloth bound upon his right arm.

“Yes, my son. A ball has destroyed my shoulder and arm. I am in the greatest pain, but I must bear it. Christ suffered more for us. Since daybreak I have been busy, caring for the wounded and pointing the dying to heaven. I have not rested a moment for sixteen hours, nor have I eaten nor drank anything. A woman tied this linen on my right arm, and I went about my work. I believe that I shall not live long. What a death! My God, and all these wounded with no one to take care of them! But, oh, I can no longer stand! I am dying! Have you seen that trench which is at the end of the Calle de los Clavos? Over there poor Coridon is lying, lifeless, the victim of his own courage. We were passing along there to take care of some of the wounded, when we saw, near the garden of San Augustine, a group of Frenchmen who were passing from one house to another. Coridon, whose impetuous blood impelled him to the most daring acts, threw himself upon them. They bayoneted him, and flung him in the ditch. How many victims in a single day, Araceli! Indeed, you are fortunate in not being hurt. But you will die of the epidemic, and that is worse. Today I have given absolution to sixty who were dying of the epidemic. I give it to you also, my friend, because I know you have committed no sins, only peccadilloes, and that you have borne yourself valiantly in these days. How is it? Do you feel worse? Truly you are yellower than these corpses about us. To die of the epidemic during this horrible siege is to die for one’s country. Courage, young man! Heaven is open to receive you, and the Virgin del Pilar will welcome you with her mantle of the stars. Life is nothing. How much better it is to die honorably, and to gain eternal glory by the suffering of a day! In the name of God, I forgive you your sins!”

Then after murmuring the prayer appropriate to the occasion, he blessed me, and pronounced the *Ego te absolvo*, and then lay down upon the ground. He looked very badly, and although I did not call myself well, I thought myself in a better state of health than the good friar. That was not the only time when the confessor died before the dying one, and the physician before the patient.

I spoke to Father Mateo, and he did not answer me, except with piteous moans. I went a little way to look for someone who might be able to help him. I met several men and women, and told them, “Father Mateo del Busto is over there and cannot move;” but they took no notice of me and went on. Many of the wounded called upon me, begging for aid; but I took no notice at all of them. Near the Coso, I met a child of eight or ten years, who was alone, and weeping in the sorest distress. I stopped him. I asked him where his parents were, and he pointed to a place near where there was a great number of the wounded and dead. Afterwards I met the same child in several places, always alone and always crying aloud very bitterly. No one cared for him. No one tried to take any of the wounded to the churches, because all or nearly all were crowded. The cellars and lower rooms which at first had been considered good places of refuge, were now infected with a death-dealing atmosphere. I directed my steps towards the centre of the Coso, because they said that there they were giving out something to eat, but I received nothing. 我们没有依他的恳求，四个人把他抬了起来；可是抬出去没有多远，他已经死在我们怀中了。

伙伴们朝前沿跑去，我正要跟上，恰好瞥见一个人，那副模样引住了我的目光。是坎迪奥拉。他从近旁一座宅子里出来，衣裳烧得焦黑，两手紧攥着一只鸡，那禽被擒在手里，咯咯地叫。我在街当中拦住他，问起他女儿和奥古斯丁。他答话时心神大乱——

“我的女儿——我不知道——在那儿——在什么地方。全完了，全完了！我把一切都丢了。字据，字据烧掉了。总算运气，我从宅子里逃了出来，逃出来的时候撞见这只鸡，它跟我一样，正躲那可怕的烈火。昨天，一只母鸡还值五个杜罗（duros）呢。可我的字据！皮拉尔至圣圣母啊，还有你，我心头的小圣多明尼基托，你们怎么忍心让我的字据烧掉？它们，至少它们，本来是可以救出来的呀！你愿意帮帮我吗？装字据的那个白铁盒还在那儿，被一根大梁死死压住。你能上哪儿替我找到半打人来？好上帝啊，这议会，这些官府，这位总司令，他们心里都在想些什么？”他接着又朝过路的人喊：“喂，老乡，朋友，亲爱的人，来试试能不能把那根塌在墙角里的梁抬起来。噢，朋友们，把你们正抬往医院的那个垂死的人放下，来帮帮我吧。噢，没心肝的萨拉戈萨人哪，上帝就是这样惩罚你们！”见没有人来帮，他便走进宅子，随即又冲出来，绝望地喊道：“抢救什么都来不及了！全都烧着了。噢，我的皮拉尔圣母，你为什么不给我行个奇迹？为什么不赐我巴比伦火窑里那些少年所得的恩典，好让我迎着烈火的锋头闯进去，救出我的字据！”
