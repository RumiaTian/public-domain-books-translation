## XXVII / 二十七
===Original===

Being really happy or really miserable makes people like you and like being with you. They need not know the cause. Someone will speak now, in a moment.﻿ ﻿… Miriam tried to return to the falling rain, the soft light in it, the soft light on the greenery, the intense green glow everywhere﻿ ﻿… misty green glow. But her eyes fell and her thoughts went on. They would have seen. Her face must be speaking of their niceness in coming out on the dull day so that she might drive about once more in Lord Lansdowne’s estate. Someone will speak. Perhaps they had not found forgetfulness in the green through the rain under the grey. Moments came suddenly in the lanes between the hedges, like that moment that always came where the lane ran up and turned and the fields spread out in the distance. But usually you could not forget the chaise and the donkey and the people. In here amongst the green something always came at once and stayed. Perhaps they did not find it so, or did not know they found it, because of their thoughts about the “fine estate.” They seemed quite easy driving in the lanes, as easy as they ever seemed when one could not forget them. What were they doing when one forgot them? They knew one liked some things better than others; or suddenly liked everything very much indeed﻿ ﻿… she said you were apathetic﻿ ﻿… what does that mean﻿ ﻿… what did she mean﻿ ﻿… with her one could see nothing and sat waiting﻿ ﻿… I said I don’t think so, I don’t think she is apathetic at all. Then they understood when one sat in a heap.﻿ ﻿… They had been pleased this morning because of one’s misery at going away. They did not know of the wild happiness in the garden before breakfast nor that the garden had been so lovely because the strain of the visit was over, and London was coming. They did not know that the happiness of being in amongst the greenery today, pouring out one’s heart in farewell to the great trees had grown so intense because the feeling of London and freedom was there. They could not see the long rich winter, the lectures and books, out of which something was coming.﻿ ﻿…
“It’s a pity the rain came.”
Ah no, that is not rain. It is not raining. What is “raining”? What do people *think* when they say these things?
“We are like daisies, *drenched* in dew.” She pursed up her face towards the sky.
They laughed and silence came again. Heavy and happy.

“可惜下雨了。”
啊不，那不是雨。没有在下雨。“下雨”是什么？人们说这些话的时候，脑子里*想*的是什么？
“我们像雏菊，*浸*在露水里。”她朝天空噘起了脸。
他们笑了，随后又是沉默。沉甸甸的、快活的。

“I’m glad you came up. I want to ask you what is to be done about Hendie.”
Miriam looked about the boudoir. Mrs. Green had hardly looked at her. She was smiling at her fancy work. But if one did not say something soon she would speak again, going on into things from her point of view. Doctor and medicine. Eve liked it all. She *liked* Mrs. Green’s clever difficult fancy work and the boudoir smell of Turkish beans and the house and garden and the bazaars and village entertainments and the children’s endless expensive clothes and the excitements and troubles about that fat man. Down here she was in a curious flush of excitement all the time herself.﻿ ﻿…
“I think she wants a rest.”
“I told her so. But resting seems to make her worse. We all thought she was worse after the holidays.”
Miriam’s eyes fell before the sudden glance of Mrs. Green’s blue green eye. She must have seen her private vision of life in the great rich house﻿ ﻿… misery, death with no escape. But they had Eve. Eve did not know what was killing her. She liked being tied to people.
“She is very nervous.”
“Yes. I know it’s only nerves. I’ve told her that.”
“But you don’t know what nerves are. They’re not just nothing.﻿ ﻿…”
“*You’re* not nervous.”
“Don’t you think so?”
“Not in the way Hendie is. You’re a solid little person.”
Miriam laughed and thought of Germany and Newlands and Banbury Park. But this house would be a thousand times worse. There was no one in it who knew anything about anything. That was why when she was not too bad Eve thought it was good for her to be there.
“I think she’s very happy here.”
“I’m glad you think that. But something must be done. She can’t go on with these perpetual headaches and sleeplessness and attacks of weepiness.”
“I think she wants a long rest.”
“What does she do with her holidays? Doesn’t she rest then?”
“Yes, but there are always *worries*,” said Miriam desperately.
“You have had a good deal of worry﻿—how is your father?”
How much do you know about that.﻿ ﻿… How does it strike you.﻿ ﻿…
“He is all right, I think.”
“He lives with your eldest sister.”
“Yes.”
“That’s very nice for him. I expect the little grandson will be a great interest.”
“Yes.”
“And your youngest sister has a little girl?”
“Yes.”
“Do you like children?”
“Yes.”
“I expect you spend a good deal of your time with your sisters.”
“Well﻿—it’s a fearful distance.”

“你上楼来我很高兴。我想问问你，亨迪该怎么办。”
米丽亚姆环顾这间闺房。格林太太几乎没有看她。她正对着自己的绣活微笑。可要是人不及早说点什么，她就要又开口了，顺着她的见解把事情一路说下去。请医生，吃药。伊芙喜欢这一切。她*喜欢*格林太太那精巧费神的绣活，闺房里土耳其咖啡豆的气味，这房子和花园，义卖会和村里的游艺会，孩子们没完没了的贵重衣裳，还有围着那个胖子转的热闹和麻烦。在这底下，她自己整个人始终处在一种奇异的亢奋之中。……
“我想她需要休息。”
“我跟她这么说过。可休息好像反倒让她更坏了。假期过后我们都觉得她坏了些。”
米丽亚姆在格林太太那只蓝绿色的眼睛蕦地扫来的一瞥前垂下了目光。她一定看出了她私下里对这种深宅大院生活的设想……苦难，无处可逃的死亡。可他们有伊芙。伊芙不知道是什么在耗她的命。她喜欢被拴在人身边。
“她神经很不好。”
“是啊。我知道那不过是神经的问题。这话我跟她说过。”
“可你不知道神经不好是什么。那不是小事。……”
“*你*就没有这毛病。”
“你不这么觉得？”
“不像亨迪那样。你是个结实的小人儿。”
米丽亚姆笑了，想起德国，想起纽兰兹，想起班伯里公园（Banbury Park）。可这座房子还要糟上一千倍。屋里没有一个人对任何事有半点见识。所以当她自己还不太坏的时候，伊芙就觉得她待在这儿是好的。
“我看她在这儿很快活。”
“你这么想我很高兴。可总得想点办法。她不能老这样头痛、失眠、一阵阵地哭。”
“我想她需要长期休息。”
“她假期都怎么过的？那时候不是休息吗？”
“是休息，可总有*烦心的事*。”米丽亚姆绝望地说。
“你也操了不少心——你父亲怎么样？”
这些事你知道多少。……这事在你听来是怎么回事。……
“我想他还好。”
“他跟你大姐住在一起。”
“是的。”
“那对他很好。我想那个小外孙会是他的一大乐事。”
“是的。”
“你最小的妹妹有个小女孩？”
“是的。”
“你喜欢孩子吗？”
“喜欢。”
“我想你大部分时间都跟姐姐们待在一起。”
“呃——路远得吓人。”

“Well﻿—I’m fearfully *busy*.”
“I expect you are. I think it’s wonderful. But you must be awfully lonely sometimes.”
Miriam fidgeted and wondered how to go.

“Perhaps she’ll be better in the winter. I think she’s really better in the cold weather.”
“Well﻿—we’ll hope so,” said Mrs. Green getting up. “I can’t think what’s the matter with her. There’s nothing to worry her down here.”
“No,” said Miriam emphatically in a worldly tone of departure. “Thank you so much for having me,” she said feebly as they passed through the flower-scented hall the scent of the flowers hanging delicately within the stronger odour of the large wood-fire.
“I’m glad you came. We thought it would be nice for both of you.”
“Yes it was very kind of you. I’m sure she wants a complete rest.” Away from us away from you in some new place.﻿ ﻿…
In the open light of the garden Mrs. Green’s eyes were almost invisible points. She ought to do her hair smaller. The fashionable bundle of little sausages did not suit a large head. The eyes looked more sunken and dead than Eve’s with her many headaches. But she was strong﻿—a strong hard thundercloud at breakfast. Perhaps very unhappy. But wealthy. Strong, cruel wealth, eating up lives it did not understand. How did Eve manage to read *Music and Morals* and Olive Schreiner here?
