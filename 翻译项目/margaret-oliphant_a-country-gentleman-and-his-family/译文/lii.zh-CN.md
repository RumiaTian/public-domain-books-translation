## LII / LII / 第五十二章
===Original===

That night Lady Markland did not close her eyes. The strength of resistance, of indignation, of self-assertion, failed her, as was inevitable in the long and slow hours, during which she looked out, at first with a certainty, then with a hope, that Theo would come back. He must come back, she said to herself, even if all were over, which seemed impossible, impossible!﻿—all in an hour or two, in one afternoon, when she thought no evil. Still the most prosaic of considerations, the least important, his clothes, if nothing more, must bring him back. She went on saying this to herself, till from a half scorn which was in it at first it came to a kind of despair. He must come back, at all events, for his clothes! She could scarcely bear Geoff all the afternoon, though it was for him all this misery was. She never could, nor would, give up her child: but his society was intolerable to her for the moment; and she felt that if Theo came and found them together he might think﻿—he would have a certain right to think. It was a relief to her when at last Geoff went to bed, silenced in all his questions, chilled, terrified, yet still heroically restraining himself, and making up his mind that he was to be sent away. After this she felt a kind of relief, a freedom in being left to herself, in wandering about the rooms and looking out in succession at every window that commanded the avenue. When the hour came to shut up the house she gave the butler an elaborate explanation; how Mr. Warrender had been obliged to return to the Warren about some business, how it was possible that he might not come back that night; in fact, she did not expect him that night, but still he might return. It was not necessary that anyone should sit up, oh no, not necessary at all. She would hear him if he came, or he could let himself in. “But I really do not expect him tonight. He has﻿—business,” she said, with a smile, which the butler thought not at all like my lady. She was not given to explanations in an ordinary way. She was very kind and considerate; but she was always a great lady, and not expansive to her servants. She smiled in a strange conciliatory way, as if begging him to believe her, and explained, to make it all right. The butler was not deceived. When was any butler ever deceived by such pretences? He knew better﻿—he knew that something had happened.

那一夜，马克兰夫人没有合眼。抗拒的力量、愤慨的力量、自申的力量，在漫长而缓慢的时辰里渐渐弃她而去——这原是不可避免的。那些时辰里，她起初笃定、继而存希地望着外面，盼着西奥回来。他一定会回来的，她对自己说，哪怕一切都完了——一切怎么就会完了呢，不可能，不可能！——就在一两个钟头里，就在一个下午，而她一点坏事也没想。哪怕最势利的考虑、最无足轻重的考虑——他的衣裳，光为这个也该把他带回来。她一遍遍这样对自己说，说着说着，从起初那点自嘲的意味，说成了一种绝望。无论如何他必须回来，为了他的衣裳！整个下午她几乎忍受不了杰夫，虽然这一切苦痛全是为了他。她永远不会、也永不肯舍弃她的孩子：可此刻他的陪伴让她无法承受；而且她想，倘若西奥回来撞见他们俩在一起，他也许会想——他就有了几分权利去想。最后杰夫上床去了，她才松了一口气：孩子被自己那些问话噇住，又冷又怕，却仍英勇地忍着，自己拿定主意要被送走了。此后她反倒觉出一种解脱，一种独处的自由：在各间屋里游落，换着从每一扇望得见林蔭道的窗口望出去。到了闭门的时辰，她给管家做了一大篇解释：沃伦德先生因沃伦庄上有事不得不回去，今夜也许回不来；其实她并不指望他今夜回来，不过他兴许会回。不必留人守夜，哦不，完全不必。他若回来她自会听见，他自己也进得了门。“可我今晚真不指望他回来。他——有事，”她笑着说；那笑在管家看来，全不像夫人的做派。平日她不作兴解释。她和蕄又体谅；但她素来是位贵妇，对仆从不亲昵多话。此刻她带着一种奇怪的讨好之态笑着，仿佛求他相信她，又解释一番，好把一切囫囵过去。管家没有受骗。这种装点几时骗倒过哪个管家？他心里有数——他晓得出事了。

He told the company downstairs that he made no doubt there had been a row, and most likely about Master Geoff, and that they might make up their minds to see rare changes. They were all making their comments upon this in the servants’ hall, while Lady Markland, standing at the window, looked out with a sort of desperation, shaping the figure of Theo a hundred times in the distance, scarcely able to restrain the impulse to go out and look for him; saying to herself, no longer scornfully, but with the profoundest tragic gravity, that he must come back, if only for his clothes! It was a dim summer night, the sky veiled with clouds, and after midnight fitfully lit by the gleam of a waning moon. She went from window to window noiselessly, thinking that now one, now another, had the most perfect command of the avenue; hearing a hundred sounds of footsteps, even of distant wheels and horses’ hoofs, which seemed to beat upon the ground far off, and never came to anything; then when the dawn began to be blue in the sky, threw herself upon her bed and hid her face, knowing that all was over, and that he would come back no more.
Scarcely less was the consternation in the Warren when Theo, pale and silent, wrapped in silence as in a cloak, making no reply to the questions asked, ordering his old room to be made ready without any explanation, came back to the already excited house. Dick and Chatty and all their affairs were forgotten in the extraordinary new event. “Oh, Theo, what has happened,” Mrs. Warrender cried, “what has happened? Are you not going home?”
“This is my home, I suppose,” he said, “unless you have any objections,” which closed her mouth. She thought there must have been a quarrel, and that Lady Markland had resented Theo’s treatment of Geoff, which his mother immediately began to justify to herself; saying that of course he did not mean to hurt the child, but that a person put in charge of the children of another, in any case, must have some power of correcting them when they wanted correction, and with great wonder and indignation at his wife had yet a wondering question in her mind﻿—what would she herself have done if anyone had corrected Theo so when he was a boy? She did all she could to urge him to return, sitting up till very late, keeping the groom awake for possible orders. “Frances will be very anxious,” she said to her son.

他告诉楼下众人，说八成是吵了一架，多半为了杰夫少爷，让他们预备着看稀罕的变化吧。仆人厅里正议论纷纷，马克兰夫人立在窗前，带着一种绝望望出去，在远处一次次勾出西奥的身影，几乎按捺不住要出门寻他的冲动；她对自己说——已不再带嘲讽，而是带着最深沉的悲剧性的庄重——他必须回来，哪怕只为他的衣裳！那是一个昏暯的夏夜，天遮着云，午夜之后偶尔透出一弯残月的微光。她悄没声地从一扇窗走到另一扇窗，觉得这一扇、那一扇，总有一扇能把林蔭道望得最透；一百次听见脚步声，甚至远处的车声、马蹄声，仿佛远远敲在地上，却从不曾成真；待天边泛起蓝色晨光，她扑倒在床上，把脸埋起来，明白一切都完了，他再不会回来了。
西奥回到家那座已然喣动的宅子时，沃伦庄里的惊惶也不相上下：他脸色苍白，沉默寡言，沉默裹着他像一领斗篷，问什么都不答，只吩咐把他的旧房间收拾出来，不置一句解释。迪克同查蒂同他们的一切，都被这桩罕见的新事挤忘了。“哦，西奥，出什么事了，”沃伦德太太叫道，“出什么事了？你不回家了？”
“这里就是我的家吧，”他说，“除非你们有异议。”这话堵住了她的嘴。她想，必定是吵架了，马克兰夫人恼了西奥待杰夫的那一套；她当娘的立刻在心中替儿子开脱：他当然不是存心伤那孩子，可一个人受托照管别人的孩子，总得有点管教的权柄吧；她一面又惊文气地对那位妻子，心里却压着一个纳闷的问题——若当年有人这样管教小时候的西奥，她自己又会怎样？她竭力劝他回去，熬到很晚，让马夫醒着随时听候差道。“弗朗西丝会急坏的，”她对儿子说。

“She has no reason to be anxious; she knows where I am.” “Oh, Theo, don’t let it come to a quarrel,” Mrs. Warrender urged imploringly, with tears in her eyes. Her attitude put him in mind of his wife’s attitude as she stood holding out her hands, and was intolerable to him. “Good night, mother. I am going to bed,” he said. Mrs. Warrender was as restless as Lady Markland. She had come and listened to his breathing outside his door, and seen that his light was out, and that he had actually gone to bed, as he said, before she would allow herself to be convinced. It was a quarrel, then; and what was to come of it﻿—what was to come of it? Lady Markland was very yielding and gentle, but Theo! Theo was not yielding. Mrs. Warrender, too, lay down when it was nearly morning, as miserable as could be.
And yet none of them, not even the chief actors, who were both at the pitch of desperation, really believed that what this meant was a breach which should last for years. Even they would not have believed it had it been put to them. That it should not all come right was incredible. But as a matter of fact it did not come right. Lady Markland was not by nature the yielding and anxious woman whom for this year of troubled wedlock she had appeared; and everybody knew that Theo was neither persuadable nor reasonable, but had the hottest temper, the most rigid will, of his own, and that ingenuity in finding himself in the right which gives a fatal character to every quarrel. Lady Markland was willing to make any concession but the one which he required, the abandonment of Geoff. But he would make no concession; he stood upon his rights. With all the fervour and absolutism of inexperience he stood fast. No, nothing less than everything, nothing but entire submission, nothing but obedience. Alarmed and anxious friends gathered to the fray, as was inevitable, and everything was made worse. The result was that within a few weeks Theo Warrender had gone off with a burning sense of injury and wrong, to travel he did not much care where, to forget himself he did not much care how; and Lady Markland, feeling as if she had awakened suddenly from a strange dream, a dream full of fever and unrest, of fugitive happiness but lasting trouble, came to herself all alone with the two little babies, in a strange solitude which was no longer natural, and with Geoff.

“她没有理由着急；她知道我在哪儿。”“哦，西奥，别闹到吵架的地步，”沃伦德太太含泪恳求。她这副姿态，让他想起妻子亱出双手立着的姿态，令他无法忍受。“晚安，母亲。我去睡了，”他说。
沃伦德太太同马克兰夫人一样不能安宁。她到他门外听过他的呼吸，见他的灯灭了，见他果真如所说的那样上了床，才肯让自己相信。那么是吵架了；往后会如何呢——往后会如何？马克兰夫人性子柔顺温和，可西奥呢！西奥是不柔顺的。沃伦德太太也是捱到快天亮才躺下，愁得不能再愁。
然而他们当中谁——连两位主角自己也不例外，两人都已绝望到极点——都不真信这意味的是一场将持续多年的决裂。即便当面说给他们听，连他们自己也不会信。这一切竟不能重归于好，是难以置信的。可事实上，它没有重归于好。马克兰夫人并非天性就是这一年间 troubled 姻缘里显出的那个柔顺惶恐的女人；而人人知道，西奥既劝不动，也不讲理，火气最暴，意志最僵，还有那种事事自占理的巧动——正是它，给每一场争吵都添上决绝的颜色。马克兰夫人什么都可以让，只除了他所要的那一样：舍弃杰夫。而他什么都不让；他站在自己的权利上。带着无阅历者全部的炽烈与专断，他屹立不动。不，少了全副不算，只要彻底的臣服，只要服从。惊惶而焦急的朋友们照例蜂拥赴这场争执，结果弄得更僵。结局是：不出几个星期，西奥·沃伦德揣着一腔灼痛的委屈与不公，出走天涯——去旅行，去哪儿他不太在乎；去忘掉自己，怎么忘他也不太在乎；而马克兰夫人，仿佛从一场奇异的梦里猛然醒来——那梦满是热度与不宁，有倏忽的幸福，更有绵长的烦恼——独自回到自己身边，守着两个小小的婴儿，守着一种再不自然的奇异的孤独，还有杰夫。

She had chosen, who could say wrongly?﻿—and yet in a way which set wrong all the circumstances of her life.
This was how for the moment her second venture came to an end. Theo went forth upon the world for that Wanderyear in which so much of the superfluous vigour of life is so often expended, which it would have been so well for everybody he had taken before: and stormed about the world for a time, no one knowing what volcanoes were exploding in his soul. How much he gathered of better wisdom it is not within the limits of this history to say.
The happy ones were Dick and Chatty, who began their life together as if there had been no cloud upon it. He had fully lived out his Wanderyear, and had paid dearly for the follies, which had been done with no evil meaning on his part, but in all honour and good intention, bitterly foolish though they were. And perhaps he never was very wise, nor rose above the possibility of being taken in, which is a peculiarity of many generous spirits. But why should we say they were the happy ones? The really happy ones were Minnie and her Eustace, who never felt themselves to be in the wrong, or were anything less than the regulators of everybody’s life and manners wherever they went. It was Mrs. Eustace Thynne’s conviction to the last that all the misfortunes which temporarily befell her sister were owing to the fact that she herself was not on the spot to regulate affairs; and that Theo, if he had taken her advice, would never have placed himself in the way of the trouble which had overwhelmed his life.

她的选择，谁能说是选错了？——只是那选法，把她一生的种种境况全都摆错了位。
她这第二段姻缘，就这样暂时落了幕。西奥出门去闯他那漫游之年——多少生命的过剩精力，往往就挥霍在这样的年头里；他若早先就把这一年游掉，于人人本是多么好的事。他到世界上咆哮了一阵，没人知道他灵魂里爆发着几座火山。他究竟汲取了多少更好的智慧，不在本史的范围内。
幸福的人是迪克同查蒂，他们开始了共同生活，仿佛不曾有过一丝阴云。他早把漫游之年过足了，也为那些荒唐事付足了代价——那些事他并无恶意，全出于体面与好心，只是蠢得厉害。或许他也从来算不上聪明，也从未高出于受骗的可能——这是许多慷慨灵魂的特性。可我们何必说他们才是幸福的人？真正幸福的是明妮同她的尤斯塔斯，他们从不觉得自己有错，走到哪里，都径直是众人生活与举止的调节器。直到最后，尤斯塔斯·泰恩太太都坚信：她妹妹一度遭逢的一切不幸，全因她本人不在场调理事务；而西奥若听了她的劝，绝不会站到那场覆灭了他一生的祸事跟前去。
