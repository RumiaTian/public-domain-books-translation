## LXXIX The Last Days of Mary Masters / 七十九 玛丽·马斯特斯最后的日子
===Original===

The triumph of Mary Masters was something more than a nine days’ wonder to the people of Dillsborough. They had all known Larry Twentyman’s intentions and aspirations, and had generally condemned the young lady’s obduracy, thinking, and not being slow to say, that she would live to repent her perversity. Runciman who had a thoroughly warmhearted friendship for both the attorney and Larry had sometimes been very severe on Mary. “She wants a touch of hardship,” he would say, “to bring her to. If Larry would just give her a cold shoulder for six months, she’d be ready to jump into his arms.” And Dr. Nupper had been heard to remark that she might go farther and fare worse. “If it were my girl I’d let her know all about it,” Ribbs the butcher had said in the bosom of his own family. When it was found that Mr. Surtees the curate was not to be the fortunate man, the matter was more inexplicable than ever. Had it then been declared that the owner of Hoppet Hall had proposed to her, all these tongues would have been silenced, and the refusal even of Larry Twentyman would have been justified. But what was to be said and what was to be thought when it was known that she was to be the mistress of Bragton? For a day or two the prosperity of the attorney was hardly to be endured by his neighbours. When it was first known that the stewardship of the property was to go back into his hands, his rise in the world was for a time slightly prejudicial to his popularity; but this greater stroke of luck, this latter promotion which would place him so much higher in Dillsborough than even his father or his grandfather had ever been, was a great trial of friendship.
Mrs. Masters felt it all very keenly. All possibility for reproach against either her husband or her stepdaughter was of course at an end. Even she did not pretend to say that Mary ought to refuse the squire. Nor, as far as Mary was concerned, could she have further recourse to the evils of Ushanting, and the peril of social intercourse with ladies and gentlemen. It was manifest that Mary was to be a lady with a big house, and many servants, and, no doubt, a carriage and horses. But still Mrs. Masters was not quite silenced. She had daughters of her own, and would solace herself by declaring to them, to her husband, and to her specially intimate friends, that of course they would see no more of Mary.

At this time Reginald Morton himself was still at Hoppet Hall, and had declared that he would remain there till after his marriage. Lady Ushant was living at the big house, which was henceforth to be her home. Mary was her visitor, and was to be married from Bragton as though Bragton were her residence rather than the squire’s. The plan had originated with Reginald, and when it had been hinted to him that Mary would in this way seem to slight her father’s home, he had proposed that all the Masters should come and stay at Bragton previous to the ceremony. 可这封信并没把他“捞”去；他没被捞去，我也不打算附和莫顿先生，说他就不值钱。有些事，男人的心该默默受着，不哼一声；可要在众目之下、装出世人指望男子汉该有的那副斯多噶式的淡漠去受，它受不住。他若去了，是当着贺客谈笑呢，还是沉着脸、郁郁着？是装得忘了他的爱呢，还是顶着失恋的样子在贺客中间转？要他落进那种近乎手足的情分——她盼的这份情分再自然不过——是办不到的，至少眼下办不到。可他既已决定宽恕她，那天下午便亲自过了河到宅子里来，自己送回信。开门的霍普金斯太太告诉他太太们在不在家，随后他被引进一间空屋等着。玛丽掠下正读到一半的那页书，尽快赶到他跟前，一路小跑下楼，只觉血直往脸上涌。“你会来的，拉里，”她说。
“不，马斯特斯小姐。”
“在我不叫莫顿太太之前，叫我玛丽吧，”她强笑着说。“我一直都是玛丽。”随后她放声哭了。“为什么——为什么你不肯来？”
“我只会像个鬼魂一样杵来杵去。婚礼上的男人该怎么欢，我欢不起来。我看男人办不了这种事。”她抬眼望着他的脸，恳求他——不是求他来，这个她此刻已知无望——而是求他用某种方式说出：她对他的罪，他宽恕了。“可我会想着你，愿你顺遂。”
