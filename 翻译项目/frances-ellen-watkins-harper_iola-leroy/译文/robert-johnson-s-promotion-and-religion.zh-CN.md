## VI Robert Johnson’s Promotion and Religion / 第六章 罗伯特·约翰逊的晋升与信仰

===Original===
Robert Johnson, being able to meet the army requirements, was enlisted as a substitute to help fill out the quota of a Northern regiment. With his intelligence, courage, and prompt obedience, he rose from the ranks and became lieutenant of a colored company. He was daring, without being rash; prompt, but not thoughtless; firm, without being harsh. Kind and devoted to the company he drilled, he soon won the respect of his superior officers and the love of his comrades.
“Johnson,” said a young officer, Captain Sybil, of Maine, who had become attached to Robert, “what is the use of your saying you’re a colored man, when you are as white as I am, and as brave a man as there is among us. Why not quit this company, and take your place in the army just the same as a white man? I know your chances for promotion would be better.”
“Captain, you may doubt my word, but today I would rather be a lieutenant in my company than a captain in yours.”
“I don’t understand you.”
“Well, Captain, when a man’s been colored all his life it comes a little hard for him to get white all at once. Were I to try it, I would feel like a cat in a strange garret. Captain, I think my place is where I am most needed. You do not need me in your ranks, and my company does. They are excellent fighters, but they need a leader. To silence a battery, to capture a flag, to take a fortification, they will rush into the jaws of death.”

===Chinese===
罗伯特·约翰逊（Robert Johnson）体格合格，达到了军队的入伍要求，便作为替身兵应征，补足了一个北方团的兵额。凭着他的聪明、勇气和雷厉风行的服从，他从行伍间擢升起来，当上了有色人种连的一名中尉。他胆大而不鲁莽，敏捷而不轻率，坚定而不严苛。他对自己操练的那支连队又和善又尽心，很快就赢得了上级军官的敬重与战友们的爱戴。
“约翰逊，”缅因州（Maine）来的年轻军官西比尔上尉（Captain Sybil）说——他已经同罗伯特结下了情谊——“你明明跟我一样白，人也同我们中间任何一个人一样勇敢，一口咬定自己是有色人种，这有什么用？干吗不离开这个连，像白人一样到军队里去占你自己的位置？我知道，那样你晋升的机会会好得多。”
“上尉，您也许不肯信我的话，可是今天，我宁可在自己的连里当中尉，也不愿到您的连里当上尉。”
“我不明白你的意思。”
“唉，上尉，一个人一辈子都是有色人种，要他一下子变白，可有点难。我要真去试一试，准会觉得像一只闯进陌生阁楼的猫。上尉，我想，我的位置应该在最需要我的地方。您那边用不着我，我的连队却用得着。他们都是顶出色的战士，可他们需要一位领头的人。要压哑一门炮，要夺取一面军旗，要拿下一座工事，他们都会一头冲进死亡的虎口。”

“Yes, I have often wondered at their bravery.”
“Captain, these battles put them on their mettle. They have been so long taught that they are nothing and nobody, that they seem glad to prove they are something and somebody.”
“But, Johnson, you do not look like them, you do not talk like them. It is a burning shame to have held such a man as you in slavery.”
“I don’t think it was any worse to have held me in slavery than the blackest man in the South.”
“You are right, Johnson. The color of a man’s skin has nothing to do with the possession of his rights.”
“Now, there is Tom Anderson,” said Robert, “he is just as black as black can be. He has been bought and sold like a beast, and yet there is not a braver man in all the company. I know him well. He is a noble-hearted fellow. True as steel. I love him like a brother. And I believe Tom would risk his life for me any day. He don’t know anything about his father or mother. He was sold from them before he could remember. He can read a little. He used to take lessons from a white gardener in Virginia. He would go between the hours of 9 p.m. and 4 a.m. He got a book of his own, tore it up, greased the pages, and hid them in his hat. Then if his master had ever knocked his hat off he would have thought them greasy papers, and not that Tom was carrying his library on his head. I had another friend who lived near us. When he was nineteen years old he did not know how many letters there were in the A.B.C.s. One night, when his work was done, his boss came into his cabin and saw him with a book in his hand. He threatened to give him five hundred lashes if he caught him again with a book, and said he hadn’t work enough to do. He was getting out logs, and his task was ten logs a day. His employer threatened to increase it to twelve. He said it just harassed him; it set him on fire. He thought there must be something good in that book if the white man didn’t want him to learn. One day he had an errand in the kitchen, and he heard one of the colored girls going over the A.B.C.s. Here was the key to the forbidden knowledge. She had heard the white children saying them, and picked them up by heart, but did not know them by sight. He was not content with that, but sold his cap for a book and wore a cloth on his head instead. He got the sounds of the letters by heart, then cut off the bark of a tree, carved the letters on the smooth inside, and learned them. He wanted to learn how to write. He had charge of a warehouse where he had a chance to see the size and form of letters. He made the beach of the river his copybook, and thus he learned to write. Tom never got very far with his learning, but I used to get the papers and tell him all I knew about the war.”

“是啊，他们的勇敢，我常常觉得惊奇。”
“上尉，这些战斗激起了他们的斗志。这么长久以来，一直有人教他们，说他们什么也不是、谁也不是；如今他们像是巴不得证明，他们是有点什么的、是个人物。”
“可是，约翰逊，你长得不像他们，说话也不像他们。把像你这样的一个人按在奴隶制里，真是莫大的耻辱。”
“我不觉得把我在奴隶制里扣着，就比把南方皮肤最黑的人扣着更糟。”
“你说得对，约翰逊。一个人皮肤的颜色，同他权利的归属毫不相干。”
“就说汤姆·安德森（Tom Anderson）吧，”罗伯特说，“他黑得不能再黑了。他像牲口一样被人买进卖出，可是全连再找不出一个比他更勇敢的人。我跟他熟得很。他是个心地高贵的人，像钢铁一样可靠。我爱他，就像爱自己的兄弟。而且我相信，汤姆随便哪一天都肯为我舍出性命。他一点也不知道自己的父亲母亲是什么人。还没到记事的年纪，他就被人从他们身边卖掉了。他识得几个字。他从前在弗吉尼亚（Virginia）跟一个白人园丁上课，总在晚上九点到凌晨四点之间去。他自己弄到一本书，把书撕开，给书页抹上油，藏进帽子里。这样一来，主人就算把他的帽子打落在地，也只会当那是几张油乎乎的纸，决想不到汤姆是把他的图书馆顶在脑袋上。我还有一个朋友，住在我们家附近。他十九岁了，还不知道字母表（A.B.C.s）里一共有多少个字母。一天夜里，他活儿干完了，老板走进他的小木屋，看见他手里拿着一本书。老板威胁说，再要逮着他拿书，就抽他五百鞭子，还说他的活儿不够多。他是往外拖运木头的，每天的定额是十根原木，雇主还威胁要加到十二根。他说，这简直是折磨他；这话把他心里点着了火。他想，那本书里必定有什么好东西，不然白人怎么不肯让他学。有一天，他到厨房去办事，听见一个有色人种姑娘在背字母表。这可是通往禁书知识的钥匙。她是听白人孩子们念熟的，靠耳朵记在了心里，见了字形却认不出来。他还不以此为足，把便帽卖掉换了一本书，头上改裹一块布。他把字母的读音背熟，然后剥下一块树皮，在光滑的树皮里子上刻出字母，一个个认会了。他还想学写字。他管着一座货栈，有机会看到字母的大小和形体。他把河边的沙滩当作习字本，就这样学会了写字。汤姆的学问从没学多深，可我常弄到报纸，把我所知道的战事都讲给他听。”

===Original===
“How did you get the papers?”
“I used to have very good privileges for a slave. All of our owners were not alike. Some of them were quite clever, and others were worse than git out. I used to get the morning papers to sell to the boarders and others, and when I got them I would contrive to hide a paper, and let some of the fellow-servants know how things were going on. And our owners thought we cared nothing about what was going on.”
“How was that? I thought you were not allowed to hold meetings unless a white man were present.”
“That was so. But we contrived to hold secret meetings in spite of their caution. We knew whom we could trust. My ole Miss wasn’t mean like some of them. She never wanted the patrollers around prowling in our cabins, and poking their noses into our business. Her husband was an awful drunkard. He ran through every cent he could lay his hands on, and she was forced to do something to keep the wolf from the door, so she set up a boardinghouse. But she didn’t take in Tom, Dick, and Harry. Nobody but the big bugs stopped with her. She taught me to read and write, and to cast up accounts. It was so handy for her to have someone who could figure up her accounts, and read or write a note, if she were from home and wanted the like done. She once told her cousin how I could write and figure up. And what do you think her cousin said?”
“ ‘Pleased,’ I suppose, ‘to hear it.’ ”
“Not a bit of it. She said, if I belonged to her, she would cut off my thumbs; her husband said, ‘Oh, then he couldn’t pick cotton.’ As to my poor thumbs, it did not seem to be taken into account what it would cost me to lose them. My ole Miss used to have a lot of books. She would let me read any one of them except a novel. She wanted to take care of my soul, but she wasn’t taking care of her own.”

“你是怎么弄到报纸的？”
“作为一个奴隶，我从前享有的待遇算是很好的。我们的主人各家并不一样。有的相当不赖，有的可就坏得没边儿了。我常领了晨报来，卖给寄宿的客人和别的人；领到报纸，我就想法子藏下一份，让几个一同当差的伙伴知道局势怎么样了。我们的主人还当我们对这些事毫不放在心上呢。”
“那是怎么回事？我原以为你们不许集会，除非有个白人在场。”
“确是那样。可他们尽管百般提防，我们还是想法子开秘密集会。我们信得过谁，心里有数。我的老女主人不像有些人家那样刻薄。她从来不愿意让巡逻队在我们的小木屋里转来转去，把鼻子伸进我们的事务里来。她丈夫是个不折不扣的酒鬼，凡是他抓得到手的钱，一个子儿不剩全挥霍光了；她不得不想个法子把饿狼挡在门外，于是就开了一处供膳寄宿屋。可她收留的不是张三李四随便什么人。在她那儿落脚的，尽是有头有脸的大人物。她教我读书、写字，还教我算账。有个能替她把账目一笔笔算清、她人不在家时需要写个条子读封信也有人能办的人，实在方便。她有一回跟她表亲夸说我会写字会算账。您猜她表亲说了什么？”
“我猜呀，她准是‘听了很高兴’。”
“一点儿也不。她说，我若是归了她，她就要砍掉我的两个大拇指；她丈夫说：‘哦，那他就摘不成棉花啦。’至于我那对可怜的大拇指——没了它们我得付出什么代价，倒像是没有人往心里去。我的老女主人从前存着好多书。随便哪本她都肯让我读，只除了小说。她要照管我的灵魂，她自己的灵魂却不去照管。”

“Wasn’t she religious?”
“She went for it. I suppose she was as good as most of them. She said her prayers and went to church, but I don’t know that that made her any better. I never did take much stock in white folks’ religion.”
“Why, Robert, I’m afraid you are something of an infidel.”
“No, Captain, I believe in the real, genuine religion. I ain’t got much myself, but I respect them that have. We had on our place a dear, old saint, named Aunt Kizzy. She was a happy soul. She had seen hard times, but was what I call a living epistle. I’ve heard her tell how her only child had been sold from her, when the man who bought herself did not want to buy her child. Poor little fellow! he was only two years old. I asked her one day how she felt when her child was taken away. ‘I felt,’ she said, ‘as if I was going to my grave. But I knew if I couldn’t get justice here, I could get it in another world.’ ”
“That was faith,” said Captain Sybil, as if speaking to himself, “a patient waiting for death to redress the wrongs of life.”
“Many a time,” continued Robert, “have I heard her humming to herself in the kitchen and saying, ‘I has my trials, ups and downs, but it won’t allers be so. I specs one day to wing and wing wid de angels, Hallelujah! Den I specs to hear a voice sayin’, “Poor ole Kizzy, she’s done de bes’ she kin. Go down, Gabriel, an’ tote her in.” Den I specs to put on my golden slippers, my long white robe, an’ my starry crown, an’ walk dem golden streets, Hallelujah!’ I’ve known that dear, old soul to travel going on two miles, after her work was done, to have someone read to her. Her favorite chapter began with, ‘Let not your heart be troubled, ye believe in God, believe also in Me.’ ”

“那她难道不虔诚信教吗？”
“她对那一套可卖力呢。我看她跟他们大多数人一样，也算不坏了。她念祷告，上教堂，可我不知道这些让她变好了多少。白人的宗教，我从来就不怎么当真。”
“哎呀，罗伯特，我看你多少有点像个不信教的人了。”
“不，上尉，我信的是又真又正的宗教。我自己拥有的不多，可我敬重那些有信仰的人。我们那儿有一位亲爱的老圣徒，名叫基齐大婶（Aunt Kizzy）。她是个快活的灵魂。她吃过苦日子，可她是我所说的一封活生生的书信。我听她讲过，她唯一的孩子怎样被人从她身边卖掉，因为买下她本人的那个男人不肯买她的孩子。可怜的小家伙！他才两岁。有一天我问她，孩子被夺走的时候，她心里是什么滋味。‘我当时觉得，’她说，‘就像自己正走向坟墓。可我知道，若是这世上讨不到公道，我还能在另一个世界讨到。’”
“那就是信心，”西比尔上尉说，像是自言自语，“耐心等候死亡来昭雪生前所受的冤屈。”
“多少回，”罗伯特接着说，“我听见她在厨房里独自哼着，自言自语：‘俺也有俺的磨难，起起落落，可不会老是这样。俺盼着有一天，跟天使们比翼齐飞，哈利路亚！到那时俺盼着听见一个声音说：“可怜的老基齐，她尽了她的力。加百列，下去把她领进来。”到那时俺盼着穿上俺的金拖鞋、俺雪白的长袍、俺缀星的冠冕，去把那些黄金的街道走个遍，哈利路亚！’这位亲爱的老灵魂，干完了活儿，还常常走上将近两英里路，去请人念书给她听。她顶爱的一章，开头是：‘你们心里不要忧愁，你们信神，也当信我。’”

“I have been deeply impressed,” said Captain Sybil, “with the childlike faith of some of these people. I do not mean to say that they are consistent Christians, but I do think that this faith has in a measure underlain the life of the race. It has been a golden thread woven amid the sombre tissues of their lives. A ray of light shimmering amid the gloom of their condition. And what would they have been without it?”
“I don’t know. But I know what she was with it. And I believe if there are any saints in glory, Aunt Kizzy is one of them.”
“She is dead, then?”
“Yes, went all right, singing and rejoicing until the last, ‘Troubles over, troubles over, and den my troubles will be over. We’ll walk de golden streets all ’roun’ in de New Jerusalem.’ Now, Captain, that’s the kind of religion that I want. Not that kind which could ride to church on Sundays, and talk so solemn with the minister about heaven and good things, then come home and light down on the servants like a thousand of bricks. I have no use for it. I don’t believe in it. I never did and I never will. If any man wants to save my soul he ain’t got to beat my body. That ain’t the kind of religion I’m looking for. I ain’t got a bit of use for it. Now, Captain, ain’t I right?”

“这些人身上那种孩子般的信心，给我留下了极深的印象，”西比尔上尉说，“我并不是说他们个个都是表里如一的基督徒，可我确实认为，这种信心在某种程度上托住了这个种族的生活。它是一条金线，织在他们晦暗的生命织物中间；是一缕光线，在他们境况的阴郁里闪烁。要是没有它，他们又会变成什么样子呢？”
“我不知道。可我知道，有了它，她成了什么样的人。而且我相信，荣耀里若真有圣徒，基齐大婶必是其中一位。”
“那么，她已经过世了？”
“是的，她走得很安好，一路唱着、欢欣着直到最后：‘苦难过去了，苦难过去了，俺的苦难就要过去了。俺们要把新耶路撒冷的黄金街道走个遍。’上尉，我要的就是这一种宗教。不是那种礼拜天坐着车子上教堂、同牧师一脸庄严地谈论天国和美善之事、回到家却像千块砖头劈头盖脸砸下来一样落在仆人头上的宗教。那种宗教我派不上用场。我不信它。我从来不信，也永远不信。谁要想拯救我的灵魂，就用不着先毒打我的身子。那不是我要找的宗教。它对我一点儿用处也没有。上尉，难道我说得不对吗？”

“Well, yes, Robert, I think you are more than half right. You ought to know my dear, old mother who lives in Maine. We have had colored company at our house, and I never saw her show the least difference between her colored and white guests. She is a Quaker preacher, and don’t believe in war, but when the rest of the young men went to the front, I wanted to go also. So I thought it all over, and there seemed to be no way out of slavery except through the war. I had been taught to hate war and detest slavery. Now the time had come when I could not help the war, but I could strike a blow for freedom. So I told my mother I was going to the front, that I expected to be killed, but I went to free the slave. It went hard with her. But I thought that I ought to come, and I believe my mother’s prayers are following me.”
“Captain,” said Robert, rising, “I am glad that I have heard your story. I think that some of these Northern soldiers do two things﻿—hate slavery and hate niggers.”
“I am afraid that is so with some of them. They would rather be whipped by Rebels than conquer with negroes. Oh, I heard a soldier,” said Captain Sybil, “say, when the colored men were being enlisted, that he would break his sword and resign. But he didn’t do either. After Colonel Shaw led his charge at Fort Wagner, and died in the conflict, he got bravely over his prejudices. The conduct of the colored troops there and elsewhere has done much to turn public opinion in their favor. I suppose any white soldier would rather have his black substitute receive the bullets than himself.”

“嗯，是啊，罗伯特，我看你说对的可不止一半。你真该认识一下我那住在缅因州的亲爱的老母亲。我们家接待过有色人种的客人，我从没见她对有色人种客人和白人客人显出过一丝一毫的差别。她是一位贵格会（Quaker）的传道人，不信战争这一套；可是别的年轻人都上了前线，我也想去。我把前前后后想了一遍：要走出奴隶制，除了打这场战争，好像再没有别的路。我从小受的教导，是憎恨战争、痛恨奴隶制。如今时候到了——这场战争我拦不住，可我能为自由挥出一击。于是我告诉母亲，我要上前线去，多半会战死，可我是去解放奴隶的。她心里难受极了。可我觉得我理应来，而且我相信，我母亲的祷告一直跟着我呢。”
“上尉，”罗伯特站起身来说，“听了您的故事，我很高兴。我看，这些北方兵做的是两件事——恨奴隶制，也恨黑鬼。”
“恐怕有些人确实是这样。他们宁可挨叛军的揍，也不愿跟黑人一道打胜仗。哦，当初征募有色人种入伍的时候，我听一个士兵说过，”西比尔上尉说，“他要折断佩刀、辞官不干。可他两样都没有做。肖上校（Colonel Shaw）率队冲锋瓦格纳堡（Fort Wagner）、战死在那场恶战里之后，他就勇敢地跨过了自己的偏见。有色人种部队在那儿和在别处的表现，让舆论大大转而对他们有利了。我想，随便哪个白人士兵，都宁可叫自己的黑人替身兵去挨子弹，而不是自己挨。”
