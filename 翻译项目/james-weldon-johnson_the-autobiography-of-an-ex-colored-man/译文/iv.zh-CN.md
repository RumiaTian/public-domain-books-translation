## IV / 四

===Original===
The farther I got below Washington, the more disappointed I became in the appearance of the country. I peered through the car windows, looking in vain for the luxuriant semitropical scenery which I had pictured in my mind. I did not find the grass so green, nor the woods so beautiful, nor the flowers so plentiful, as they were in Connecticut. Instead, the red earth partly covered by tough, scrawny grass, the muddy, straggling roads, the cottages of unpainted pine boards, and the clay-daubed huts imparted a “burnt up” impression. Occasionally we ran through a little white and green village that was like an oasis in a desert.
When I reached Atlanta, my steadily increasing disappointment was not lessened. I found it a big, dull, red town. This dull red color of that part of the South I was then seeing had much, I think, to do with the extreme depression of my spirits﻿—no public squares, no fountains, dingy streetcars, and, with the exception of three or four principal thoroughfares, unpaved streets. It was raining when I arrived and some of these unpaved streets were absolutely impassable. Wheels sank to the hubs in red mire, and I actually stood for an hour and watched four or five men work to save a mule, which had stepped into a deep sink, from drowning, or rather suffocating in the mud. The Atlanta of today is a new city.
On the train I had talked with one of the Pullman car porters, a bright young fellow who was himself a student, and told him that I was going to Atlanta to attend school. I had also asked him to tell me where I might stop for a day or two until the University opened. He said I might go with him to the place where he stopped during his “layovers” in Atlanta. I gladly accepted his offer and went with him along one of those muddy streets until we came to a rather rickety looking frame house, which we entered. The proprietor of the house was a big, fat, greasy-looking, brown-skin man. When I asked him if he could give me accommodations, he wanted to know how long I would stay. I told him perhaps two days, not more than three. In reply he said, “Oh, dat’s all right den,” at the same time leading the way up a pair of creaky stairs. I followed him and the porter to a room, the door of which the proprietor opened while continuing, it seemed, his remark, “Oh, dat’s all right den,” by adding, “You kin sleep in dat cot in de corner der. Fifty cents, please.” The porter interrupted by saying, “You needn’t collect from him now; he’s got a trunk.” This seemed to satisfy the man, and he went down, leaving me and my porter friend in the room. I glanced around the apartment and saw that it contained a double bed and two cots, two washstands, three chairs, and a timeworn bureau, with a looking-glass that would have made Adonis appear hideous. I looked at the cot in which I was to sleep and suspected, not without good reasons, that I should not be the first to use the sheets and pillowcase since they had last come from the wash. When I thought of the clean, tidy, comfortable surroundings in which I had been reared, a wave of homesickness swept over me that made me feel faint. Had it not been for the presence of my companion, and that I knew this much of his history﻿—that he was not yet quite twenty, just three years older than myself, and that he had been fighting his own way in the world, earning his own living and providing for his own education since he was fourteen﻿—I should not have been able to stop the tears that were welling up in my eyes.

过了华盛顿越往南走，我对乡野的容貌就越发失望。我眯眼贴着车窗张望，徒劳地寻找心里描画过的那种丰茂的亚热带风光。草不及康涅狄格的绿，树林不及那里的美，花也不及那里的多。取而代之的，是半掩着坚韧稀疏野草的红土，泥泞拖沓的道路，没上过漆的松木板小屋，还有糊着泥巴的棚舍，处处透着一股“烧焦了”的印象。偶尔火车穿过一座白绿相间的小村，宛若沙漠里的一片绿洲。
到了亚特兰大，我一路上步步加深的失望并未减轻。我发现它是座灰扑扑的红色大城。我当时所见的南方这一带的这种暗红色，我想，同我情绪的极度低落大有关系——没有公共广场，没有喷泉，电车黯淡破旧，除了三四条要道，街道一律没有铺过路面。我到的那天正下着雨，几条土路简直无法通行。车轮陷进红泥，直没到毂；我竟实实在在站了一个钟头，看四五个人奋力搭救一头骡子——它踩进一个深坑，眼看要在泥里淹死，不如说闷死。今日的亚特兰大已是一座崭新的城市。
在火车上，我同普尔曼卧车的一个搬运侍者攀谈过；他是个机灵的年轻人，自己也是个学生。我告诉他我要去亚特兰大上学，还请他指点我在开学前可以在哪儿落脚一两天。他说，我可以随他到他“歇班”时在亚特兰大投宿的地方去。我欣然领受了他的好意，随他走过一条泥泞的街道，来到一座看上去相当歪斜的木板房前，走了进去。房主是个大块头、肥胖、油光满面、棕色皮肤的男人。我问他能不能给我安排个铺位，他先要知道我住多久。我说大概两天，至多三天。他答道：“哦，那就好办了。”说着便领路走上一段吱呀作响的楼梯。我跟着他和侍者走进一个房间，房主推开门，那句“那就好办了”似乎还在往下续：“你就睡角落里那张小床。五角钱，劳驾。”侍者插嘴道：“这会儿先别收他的钱；他带着箱子呢。”这话似乎让他满意了，他便下楼去，把我同我的侍者朋友留在房里。我环视这间屋子，见里面有一张双人床、两张小床、两个洗脸架、三把椅子，还有一张年深日久的五斗柜，柜上的穿衣镜足以把阿多尼斯照成丑八怪。我望了望我即将睡的那张小床，心生疑窦——而且不无道理——自打那床单枕套上次从洗衣坊回来，我不会是头一个用它们的人。一想起我自幼长大的那洁净、整齐、舒适的环境，一阵思乡的浪潮扑头盖来，我几乎要晕过去。要不是同伴在场，要不是我知道他这几点来历——他还不到二十岁，恰好比我大三岁，而且从十四岁起便独自在世界里打拼，自食其力，自筹学费——我决计止不住正往眼眶里涌的泪水。

I asked him why it was that the proprietor of the house seemed unwilling to accommodate me for more than a couple of days. He informed me that the man ran a lodging house especially for Pullman porters, and as their stays in town were not longer than one or two nights, it would interfere with his arrangements to have anyone stay longer. He went on to say, “You see this room is fixed up to accommodate four men at a time. Well, by keeping a sort of table of trips, in and out, of the men, and working them like checkers, he can accommodate fifteen or sixteen in each week and generally avoid having an empty bed. You happen to catch a bed that would have been empty for a couple of nights.” I asked him where he was going to sleep. He answered, “I sleep in that other cot tonight; tomorrow night I go out.” He went on to tell me that the man who kept the house did not serve meals, and that if I was hungry, we would go out and get something to eat.
We went into the street, and in passing the railroad station I hired a wagon to take my trunk to my lodging place. We passed along until, finally, we turned into a street that stretched away, up and down hill, for a mile or two; and here I caught my first sight of colored people in large numbers. I had seen little squads around the railroad stations on my way south, but here I saw a street crowded with them. They filled the shops and thronged the sidewalks and lined the curb. I asked my companion if all the colored people in Atlanta lived in this street. He said they did not and assured me that the ones I saw were of the lower class. I felt relieved, in spite of the size of the lower class. The unkempt appearance, the shambling, slouching gait, and loud talk and laughter of these people aroused in me a feeling of almost repulsion. Only one thing about them awoke a feeling of interest: that was their dialect. I had read some Negro dialect and had heard snatches of it on my journey down from Washington; but here I heard it in all of its fullness and freedom. I was particularly struck by the way in which it was punctuated by such exclamatory phrases as “Lawd a mussy!” “G’wan, man!” “Bless ma soul!” “Look heah, chile!” These people talked and laughed without restraint. In fact, they talked straight from their lungs and laughed from the pits of their stomachs. And this hearty laughter was often justified by the droll humor of some remark. I paused long enough to hear one man say to another: “Wat’s de mattah wid you an’ yo’ fr’en’ Sam?” and the other came back like a flash: “Ma fr’en’? He ma fr’en’? Man! I’d go to his funeral jes’ de same as I’d go to a minstrel show.” I have since learned that this ability to laugh heartily is, in part, the salvation of the American Negro; it does much to keep him from going the way of the Indian.
The business places of the street along which we were passing consisted chiefly of low bars, cheap dry-goods and notion stores, barber shops, and fish and bread restaurants. We, at length, turned down a pair of stairs that led to a basement and I found myself in an eating-house somewhat better than those I had seen in passing; but that did not mean much for its excellence. The place was smoky, the tables were covered with oilcloth, the floor with sawdust, and from the kitchen came a rancid odor of fish fried over several times, which almost nauseated me. I asked my companion if this was the place where we were to eat. He informed me that it was the best place in town where a colored man could get a meal. I then wanted to know why somebody didn’t open a place where respectable colored people who had money could be accommodated. He answered, “It wouldn’t pay; all the respectable colored people eat at home, and the few who travel generally have friends in the towns to which they go, who entertain them.” He added, “Of course, you could go in any place in the city; they wouldn’t know you from white.”

我问他，房主为什么似乎不愿意留我住两天以上。他告诉我，那人开的是一家专为普尔曼侍者服务的客栈，而这些人进城不过停留一两夜，谁住得久了，就搅了他的安排。他接着说：“你瞧，这间房是照一次住四个人布置的。这样一来，他把这些人进出城的班次记成一张表，像走跳棋一样调度他们，每星期就能安置十五六个人，而且大体上不会空出一张床。你算是赶上了一张本来要空两夜的好床。”我问他打算睡哪儿。他答道：“今晚我睡那边那张小床；明晚我就出车了。”他又告诉我，开店的那人不供饭，我若饿了，我们就出去找点吃的。
我们上了街，路过火车站时，我雇了一辆板车把箱子送去住处。我们一路走去，最后拐进一条街，它起伏着向两头伸展出去，足有一两英里；在这里，我头一回见到大群大群的有色人。南来的路上，我也在火车站周围见过一小伙一小伙的，可这里，我看见整条街都被他们挤满了。他们塞满了店铺，拥满了人行道，在路缘石边站成排。我问同伴，亚特兰大的有色人是不是都住在这条街上。他说不是，还叫我放心，我见到的这些人属于下层。尽管这“下层”人数可观，我还是松了口气。这些人蓬头垢面的模样、拖沓懒散的步态、高声的谈笑，在我心里激起一种近乎厌恶的感觉。唯有一处让我起了兴味：他们的方言。黑人方言我读过一些，从华盛顿南下的一路上也听过几耳朵；可在这里，我才听到它十足酣畅的全貌。方言里点缀着的那些感叹语尤其让我着迷：“老天爷可怜见！”“去你的吧！”“我的天！”“你听着，孩子！”这些人谈笑无拘无束。说实在的，他们说话是直从肺腑里出来的，笑是从肚肠深处发出来的。而这爽朗的大笑，又往往确有令人解颐的妙语作底子。我驻足了一会儿，听见一个人对另一个说：“你跟你那朋友山姆是咋回事？”对方闪电般回敬：“我的朋友？他是我的朋友？老兄！他死了出殡，我会去凑热闹，就像去看一场黑脸滑稽戏。”后来我才明白，这放声大笑的本事，一定程度上正是美国黑人的自救之道；它大大地拦住了他，使他不曾走上印第安人的那条路。
我们经过的这条街上的铺面，主要是低档酒馆、廉价布店杂货店、理发店，还有卖鱼和面包的小饭馆。最后我们顺一段台阶拐进一间地下室，我发现自己站在一家饭铺里，比我一路掠过的那些稍强些；可这也不说明它好在哪儿。铺子里烟雾腾腾，桌面铺着油布，地上撒着锯末，厨房里飘出一股鱼回锅炸过几道的哈喇味，几乎叫我作呕。我问同伴，我们要吃饭的就是这地方？他告诉我，这已是全城有色人能吃上饭的最好的馆子了。我于是想知道，为什么没人开一家体面馆子，接待那些有钱的体面有色人。他答道：“开那个不划算；体面的有色人都在家里吃饭，少数出门在外的，到了要去的城镇一般也都有朋友款待。”他又补了一句：“当然啰，城里随便哪家馆子你都进得去；人家看不出你跟白人有什么两样。”

I sat down with the porter at one of the tables, but was not hungry enough to eat with any relish what was put before me. The food was not badly cooked; but the iron knives and forks needed to be scrubbed, the plates and dishes and glasses needed to be washed and well dried. I minced over what I took on my plate while my companion ate. When we finished, we paid the waiter twenty cents each and went out. We walked around until the lights of the city were lit. Then the porter said that he must get to bed and have some rest, as he had not had six hours’ sleep since he left Jersey City. I went back to our lodging house with him.
When I awoke in the morning, there were, besides my newfound friend, two other men in the room, asleep in the double bed. I got up and dressed myself very quietly, so as not to awake anyone. I then drew from under the pillow my precious roll of greenbacks, took out a ten-dollar bill, and, very softly unlocking my trunk, put the remainder, about three hundred dollars, in the inside pocket of a coat near the bottom, glad of the opportunity to put it unobserved in a place of safety. When I had carefully locked my trunk, I tiptoed toward the door with the intention of going out to look for a decent restaurant where I might get something fit to eat. As I was easing the door open, my porter friend said with a yawn, “Hello! You’re going out?”
I answered him, “Yes.”
“Oh!” he yawned again, “I guess I’ve had enough sleep; wait a minute, I’ll go with you.” For the instant his friendship bored and embarrassed me. I had visions of another meal in the greasy restaurant of the day before. He must have divined my thoughts, for he went on to say, “I know a woman across town who takes a few boarders; I think we can go over there and get a good breakfast.” With a feeling of mingled fears and doubts regarding what the breakfast might be, I waited until he had dressed himself.
When I saw the neat appearance of the cottage we entered, my fears vanished, and when I saw the woman who kept it, my doubts followed the same course. Scrupulously clean, in a spotless white apron and colored head-handkerchief, her round face beaming with motherly kindness, she was picturesquely beautiful. She impressed me as one broad expanse of happiness and good nature. In a few minutes she was addressing me as “chile” and “honey.” She made me feel as though I should like to lay my head on her capacious bosom and go to sleep.

我同侍者在一张桌边坐下，可胃口不足，端上来的东西吃不出什么滋味。饭菜烧得不算坏；可铁餐刀餐叉该好好擦一擦了，盘碟杯盏也该洗一洗、擦干了。同伴大嚼的时候，我只是把自己盘里的东西拨来拨去。吃完，我们各付了侍者两角钱，走了出去。我们在街上逛到城市点起灯火。然后侍者说，他得上床歇歇了，自打离开泽西城，他还没睡足过六个钟头。我便随他回我们的客栈去了。
早晨醒来，房里除了我这位新交的朋友，双人床上还睡着两个人。我起了床，蹑手蹑脚穿好衣服，怕惊醒任何人。然后我从枕头底下摸出我那卷宝贝的绿背钞票，抽出一张十元的，又轻轻开了箱锁，把余下的约三百元放进箱底一件上衣的内袋里，庆幸得了个机会，趁人不备把这笔钱藏进安全的地方。仔细锁好箱子后，我便踮着脚朝房门走去，打算出去找一家像样的馆子，弄点能入口的东西吃。我正轻轻拉开门，我的侍者朋友打着哈欠说：“哟！你要出去？”
我答他：“是啊。”
“哦！”他又打了个哈欠，“我觉也睡足了；等一下，我陪你去。”那一刻，他的友情让我又烦又窘。我眼前浮现出昨天那家油腻饭铺的另一顿饭。他必定猜着了我的心思，因为他接着说：“城那头有位大娘，收几个搭伙的房客；我想咱们可以上那儿吃顿好早饭。”怀着对那顿早饭吉凶未卜、七上八下的心情，我等着他穿好衣服。
一见我们走进的那座小屋整洁的模样，我的恐惧消散了；一见掌屋的那位妇人，我的疑虑也随之而去。她洁净得一丝不苟，系着雪白的围裙，包着花头巾，圆圆的脸焕发着慈母般的和善，美得如画中人。她给我的印象，是一片宽阔无边的幸福与和气。没几分钟，她便“孩子”、“心肝”地唤起我来。她让我直想枕着她那宽阔的胸脯，睡上一觉。

And the breakfast, simple as it was, I could not have had at any restaurant in Atlanta at any price. There was fried chicken, as it is fried only in the South, hominy boiled to the consistency where it could be eaten with a fork, and biscuits so light and flaky that a fellow with any appetite at all would have no difficulty in disposing of eight or ten. When I had finished, I felt that I had experienced the realization of at least one of my dreams of Southern life.
During the meal we found out from our hostess, who had two boys in school, that Atlanta University opened on that very day. I had somehow mixed my dates. My friend the porter suggested that I go out to the University at once and offered to walk over and show me the way. We had to walk because, although the University was not more than twenty minutes’ distance from the center of the city, there were no streetcars running in that direction. My first sight of the School grounds made me feel that I was not far from home; here the red hills had been terraced and covered with green grass; clean gravel walks, well shaded, led up to the buildings; indeed, it was a bit of New England transplanted. At the gate my companion said he would bid me goodbye, because it was likely that he would not see me again before his car went out. He told me that he would make two more trips to Atlanta and that he would come out and see me; that after his second trip he would leave the Pullman service for the winter and return to school in Nashville. We shook hands, I thanked him for all his kindness, and we said goodbye.
I walked up to a group of students and made some inquiries. They directed me to the president’s office in the main building. The president gave me a cordial welcome; it was more than cordial; he talked to me, not as the official head of a college, but as though he were adopting me into what was his large family, personally to look after my general welfare as well as my education. He seemed especially pleased with the fact that I had come to them all the way from the North. He told me that I could have come to the school as soon as I had reached the city and that I had better move my trunk out at once. I gladly promised him that I would do so. He then called a boy and directed him to take me to the matron, and to show me around afterwards. I found the matron even more motherly than the president was fatherly. She had me register, which was in effect to sign a pledge to abstain from the use of intoxicating beverages, tobacco, and profane language while I was a student in the school. This act caused me no sacrifice, as, up to that time, I was free from all three habits. The boy who was with me then showed me about the grounds. I was especially interested in the industrial building.
The sounding of a bell, he told me, was the signal for the students to gather in the general assembly hall, and he asked me if I would go. Of course I would. There were between three and four hundred students and perhaps all of the teachers gathered in the room. I noticed that several of the latter were colored. The president gave a talk addressed principally to newcomers; but I scarcely heard what he said, I was so much occupied in looking at those around me. They were of all types and colors, the more intelligent types predominating. The colors ranged from jet black to pure white, with light hair and eyes. Among the girls especially there were many so fair that it was difficult to believe that they had Negro blood in them. And, too, I could not help noticing that many of the girls, particularly those of the delicate brown shades, with black eyes and wavy dark hair, were decidedly pretty. Among the boys many of the blackest were fine specimens of young manhood, tall, straight, and muscular, with magnificent heads; these were the kind of boys who developed into the patriarchal “uncles” of the old slave regime.

再说那顿早饭，虽则简单，却是亚特兰大任何馆子里花任何价钱也吃不到的。有炸鸡——只有南方才有那种炸法；有玉米粥，熬到用叉子便能叉起的稠度；还有小圆面包，松酥得入口即化，只要胃口还像样的人，干掉八个十个毫不费力。吃完，我觉得自己总算亲历了南方生活梦想中的至少一样。
饭桌上，我们从女房东那里——她有两个儿子在上学——得知亚特兰大大学当天就开学。我不知怎么把日子记岔了。我的侍者朋友建议我立刻就到学校去，还主动提出陪我走一趟，给我引路。我们只能步行，因为大学离市中心虽不过二十分钟的路，电车却不朝那个方向开。头一眼望见校园，我便觉得自己仿佛离家不远；这里的红土山丘已修成层层梯台，覆着绿草；洁净的砾石小径，浓荫夹道，直通楼房；说实在的，这是一角移栽过来的新英格兰。在大门口，同伴说他就此别过，因为出车前恐怕再见不着我了。他告诉我，他还要来亚特兰大跑两趟，会出来看我；还说第二趟跑完，他今冬便辞了普尔曼的差使，回纳什维尔的学校去。我们握了手，我谢过了他所有的好意，便道了别。
我走到一群学生跟前打听了些事。他们指点我到主楼里的校长室去。校长给了我热诚的欢迎；岂止热诚；他同我谈话，不是以一所学院的行政首长的身份，倒像是把我收养进他那个大家庭，要亲自照管我的起居冷暖，也照管我的学业。我千里迢迢从北方来投他们，这一点似乎尤其让他高兴。他告诉我，我原可以一到城里就来学校的，还说我不如马上把箱子搬出来。我欣然应允照办。随后他唤来一个男孩，叫他带我去见舍监，之后再领我各处看看。我觉得这位舍监的慈母之风，比校长的父辈之心还有过之。她让我登记注册——实际上是签署一份誓约：在校求学期间，不沾酒，不吸烟，不出恶言。这一举动于我毫无牺牲，因为直到那时，这三样习惯我一样也没有。随后那男孩领我参观了校园。我对那座工艺楼尤其感兴趣。
他告诉我，铃声一响，便是学生到大礼堂集合的号令，他问我要不要去。我当然要去。礼堂里聚集着三四百名学生，教师们恐怕也到齐了。我注意到后者中有几位是有色人。校长作了讲话，主要是对着新生讲的；可他说了些什么，我几乎全没听进，我的心思都用在打量四周的人上了。他们形形色色，肤色各异，而以较聪慧的类型居多。肤色从漆黑一直排到纯白，还有浅色的头发和眼睛。尤其在女孩中间，有许多人白得让人难以相信她们身上流着黑人的血。而且我也不由得注意到，许多女孩——特别是那些匀净的浅棕色皮肤、黑眼睛、深色波浪发的——着实漂亮。男孩中间，好些肤色最黑的是青年男子中的上选之材，高挑、挺直、筋骨结实，头颅生得堂堂；这一类男孩，日后便长成旧奴隶制度下那种族长般的“大叔”。

When I left the University, it was with the determination to get my trunk and move out to the school before night. I walked back across the city with a light step and a light heart. I felt perfectly satisfied with life for the first time since my mother’s death. In passing the railroad station I hired a wagon and rode with the driver as far as my stopping-place. I settled with my landlord and went upstairs to put away several articles I had left out. As soon as I opened my trunk, a dart of suspicion shot through my heart; the arrangement of things did not look familiar. I began to dig down excitedly to the bottom till I reached the coat in which I had concealed my treasure. My money was gone! Every single bill of it. I knew it was useless to do so, but I searched through every other coat, every pair of trousers, every vest, and even each pair of socks. When I had finished my fruitless search, I sat down dazed and heartsick. I called the landlord up and informed him of my loss; he comforted me by saying that I ought to have better sense than to keep money in a trunk and that he was not responsible for his lodgers’ personal effects. His cooling words brought me enough to my senses to cause me to look and see if anything else was missing. Several small articles were gone, among them a black and gray necktie of odd design upon which my heart was set; almost as much as the loss of my money I felt the loss of my tie.
After thinking for a while as best I could, I wisely decided to go at once back to the University and lay my troubles before the president. I rushed breathlessly back to the school. As I neared the grounds, the thought came across me, would not my story sound fishy? Would it not place me in the position of an impostor or beggar? What right had I to worry these busy people with the results of my carelessness? If the money could not be recovered, and I doubted that it could, what good would it do to tell them about it? The shame and embarrassment which the whole situation gave me caused me to stop at the gate. I paused, undecided, for a moment; then, turned and slowly retraced my steps, and so changed the whole course of my life.
If the reader has never been in a strange city without money or friends, it is useless to try to describe what my feelings were; he could not understand. If he has been, it is equally useless, for he understands more than words could convey. When I reached my lodgings, I found in the room one of the porters who had slept there the night before. When he heard what misfortune had befallen me, he offered many words of sympathy and advice. He asked me how much money I had left. I told him that I had ten or twelve dollars in my pocket. He said: “That won’t last you very long here, and you will hardly be able to find anything to do in Atlanta. I’ll tell you what you do, go down to Jacksonville and you won’t have any trouble to get a job in one of the big hotels there, or in St. Augustine.” I thanked him, but intimated my doubts of being able to get to Jacksonville on the money I had. He reassured me by saying: “Oh, that’s all right. You express your trunk on through, and I’ll take you down in my closet.” I thanked him again, not knowing then what it was to travel in a Pullman porter’s closet. He put me under a deeper debt of gratitude by lending me fifteen dollars, which he said I could pay back after I had secured work. His generosity brought tears to my eyes, and I concluded that, after all, there were some kind hearts in the world.
I now forgot my troubles in the hurry and excitement of getting my trunk off in time to catch the train, which went out at seven o’clock. I even forgot that I hadn’t eaten anything since morning. We got a wagon﻿—the porter went with me﻿—and took my trunk to the express office. My new friend then told me to come to the station at about a quarter of seven and walk straight to the car where I should see him standing, and not to lose my nerve. I found my role not so difficult to play as I thought it would be, because the train did not leave from the central station, but from a smaller one, where there were no gates and guards to pass. I followed directions, and the porter took me on his car and locked me in his closet. In a few minutes the train pulled out for Jacksonville.
I may live to be a hundred years old, but I shall never forget the agonies I suffered that night. I spent twelve hours doubled up in the porter’s basket for soiled linen, not being able to straighten up on account of the shelves for clean linen just over my head. The air was hot and suffocating and the smell of damp towels and used linen was sickening. At each lurch of the car over the none-too-smooth track I was bumped and bruised against the narrow walls of my narrow compartment. I became acutely conscious of the fact that I had not eaten for hours. Then nausea took possession of me, and at one time I had grave doubts about reaching my destination alive. If I had the trip to make again, I should prefer to walk.

离开大学时，我已拿定主意，天黑前取回箱子，搬到学校去。我脚步轻快、心情轻松地穿城走回。自母亲去世以来，我头一回对生活感到全然的满足。路过火车站，我雇了一辆板车，同车夫一道坐到我的住处。我同房东结清了账，上楼去收起几件留在外面的东西。一开箱，一支疑心的箭便射穿了我的心；箱里东西的摆布看着眼生。我激动地往下翻，直翻到箱底那件藏着我财宝的上衣。我的钱没了！一张不剩。明知道无用，我还是把其余每件上衣、每条裤子、每件背心，甚至每双袜子都搜了一遍。这徒劳的搜寻做完，我坐下来，又茫然，又心碎。我把房东叫上来，告知了我的损失；他安慰我说，把钱放在箱子里，我该更明白事理些，房客的私人物品，他概不负责。他这番冷话倒把我激得清醒了几分，使我想去看看还丢了别的什么。又有几样小东西不见了，其中有一条黑灰相间、花样别致的领带，那是我一心喜爱的；领带的失去，几乎同失去钱一样让我难过。
尽我所能盘算了一阵之后，我明智地决定立刻回大学去，把我的难处禀明校长。我气喘吁吁地奔回学校。将近校门时，一个念头掠过心头：我这番话听起来岂不蹊跷？岂不把我摆到骗子或告帮叫花子的位置上？我有什么权利，拿自己疏忽的后果去搅扰这些忙碌的人？钱若是找不回来——我本就疑心找不回——告诉他们又有何益？整个处境给我的羞惭与窘迫，使我在大门口停住了。我踌躇片刻，难以决断；随后，我转过身，慢慢循原路走了回去——就这样，改变了我一生的整个航向。

这时，赶在七点钟的火车开车前把箱子及时运走的忙乱和兴奋，让我忘了烦恼。我甚至忘了自己从清早起还没吃过东西。我们雇了辆板车——那侍者陪我同去——把箱子送到了快运营业所。随后我的新朋友叮嘱我，七点差一刻左右到车站来，径直走向他站着的车厢，沉住气别慌。我发现这出戏演起来并不像想的那么难，因为火车不从中央车站发车，而是从一个较小的车站发，那里没有关卡和查票的门岗。我照吩咐做了，侍者把我带上车，锁进了他的小间。几分钟后，火车开动，向杰克逊维尔驶去。
