## VI Phineas and His Old Friends / 六 菲尼亚斯与旧友们
===Original===

Phineas Finn returned from Tankerville to London in much better spirits than those which had accompanied him on his journey thither. He was not elected; but then, before the election, he had come to believe that it was quite out of the question that he should be elected. And now he did think it probable that he should get the seat on a petition. A scrutiny used to be a very expensive business, but under the existing law, made as the scrutiny would be in the borough itself, it would cost but little; and that little, should he be successful, would fall on the shoulders of Mr. Browborough. Should he knock off eight votes and lose none himself, he would be member for Tankerville. He knew that many votes had been given for Browborough which, if the truth were known of them, would be knocked off; and he did not know that the same could be said of any one of those by which he had been supported. But, unfortunately, the judge by whom all this would be decided might not reach Tankerville in his travels till after Christmas, perhaps not till after Easter; and in the meantime, what should he do with himself?
As for going back to Dublin, that was now out of the question. He had entered upon a feverish state of existence in which it was impossible that he should live in Ireland. Should he ultimately fail in regard to his seat he must﻿—vanish out of the world. While he remained in his present condition he would not even endeavour to think how he might in such case best bestow himself. For the present he would remain within the region of politics, and live as near as he could to the whirl of the wheel of which the sound was so dear to him. Of one club he had always remained a member, and he had already been reelected a member of the Reform. So he took up his residence once more at the house of a certain Mr. and Mrs. Bunce, in Great Marlborough Street, with whom he had lodged when he first became a member of Parliament.
“So you’re at the old game, Mr. Finn?” said his landlord.
“Yes; at the old game. I suppose it’s the same with you?” Now Mr. Bunce had been a very violent politician, and used to rejoice in calling himself a Democrat.
“Pretty much the same, Mr. Finn. I don’t see that things are much better than they used to be. They tell me at the *People’s Banner* office that the lords have had as much to do with this election as with any that ever went before it.”

And so he was established once more in his old rooms in Great Marlborough Street; and as he sat back in the armchair, which he used to know so well, a hundred memories of former days crowded back upon him. Lord Chiltern for a few months had lived with him; and then there had arisen a quarrel, which he had for a time thought would dissolve his old life into ruin. Now Lord Chiltern was again his very intimate friend. And there had used to sit a needy moneylender whom he had been unable to banish. Alas! alas! how soon might he now require that moneylender’s services! And then he recollected how he had left these rooms to go into others, grander and more appropriate to his life when he had filled high office under the State. Would there ever again come to him such cause for migration? And would he again be able to load the frame of the looking-glass over the fire with countless cards from Countesses and Ministers’ wives? He had opened the oyster for himself once, though it had closed again with so sharp a snap when the point of his knife had been withdrawn. Would he be able to insert the point again between those two difficult shells? Would the Countesses once more be kind to him? Would drawing-rooms be opened to him, and sometimes opened to him and to no other?

> 听弟媳说您在哈灵顿庄园同他们住了一阵，我非常高兴。您同奥斯瓦尔德、维奥莱特聚在一处，多么像旧日——比您住在都柏林自然多了。我想象不出您过下议院、唐宁街、俱乐部以外的生活；也不愿想。而一听说您在哈灵顿庄园，我就知道您正在回到那几样的路上。
> 快告诉我，奥斯瓦尔德同维奥莱特的日子过得怎样。他自然从不写信。他属于那种男人：一结婚，便认定总算寻着了一个可以代行他一直荒废的义务的人。维奥莱特写，可关于他们自己，她说得几乎没有。她的信很好，满纸逸事，文笔漂亮——是那种值得收藏、值得印出来的信；可从来不是家信。她把自家身为猎犬队夫人那点苦处发挥得妙到毫巅；只是那苦处分明是编的，那手艺却分明是真。她告诉我，可怜的巴尔多克夫人怎样把她女儿的不幸讲给您听，那副讲法连我都笑了；若印出来，管保叫后来千千万万人笑。可她的内里生活、她的娃娃、她丈夫之为丈夫——她一个字不提。这些您都亲眼见了；您身上女人的那一面也够使，定能告诉我他们过得怎样。我确知他们快活，因为维奥莱特的常识比我认得的任何女人都多。
> 还有坦克维尔那桩事，千万讲给我听。表哥巴林顿来信说，议席您笃定拿得到。他断言布罗伯勒先生几乎无意恋战——虽说天下再没有比他更恋战的人，只要行贿拉得动选民。可巴林顿似乎认为，您这回办得漂亮，靠的是——用他的话说——套索外头跑。我们实在没料到您对教会下这样重的手。别以为我抱怨。为我自己，一想到将来的分离就痛心；可它既躲不掉，出自您的手不正如出自任何人的手？您心里到底爱不上一家新教的居高临下之教会，这几乎不可能。

> 关于肯尼迪先生，我所知的这样少，听到的又这样少，真是稀奇。没有一个人我可以打听他。上会期他没出席，这我知道；我们猜他如今把议席也丢了。我怕他身体坏了——或者更糟：他脑子叫生活的阴翳损了。我料他一头住在洛克林特。他不时恳求我回去，尽他屋檐下的本分。他的要求不立于他自己的感情，也不立于对我尚存感情的假定。他不提幸福。他不给安慰。他不用日后的体贴来劝。他只把要求立在《圣经》上，立在那由此应当压在我心头的义务感上。他甚至从没对我说过他爱我；他只一味坚持：上帝所结合的，人间的任何东西都拆不得。到这里之后我给他写过一回信——一封凄凉、冗长、疲惫的信。自那以后，他的来信我只好不复。
