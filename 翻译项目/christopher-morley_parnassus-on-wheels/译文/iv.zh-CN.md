## IV / 四

===Original===
Just out of sight of the farm the road forks, one way running on to Walton where you cross the river by a covered bridge, the other swinging down toward Greenbriar and Port Vigor. Mrs. Collins lives a mile or so up the Walton road, and as I very often run over to see her I thought Andrew would be most likely to look for me there. So, after we had passed through the grove, I took the right-hand turn to Greenbriar. We began the long ascent over Huckleberry Hill and as I smelt the fresh autumn odour of the leaves I chuckled a little.

Mr. Mifflin seemed in a perfect ecstasy of high spirits. “This is certainly grand,” he said. “Lord, I applaud your spunk. Do you think Mr. McGill will give chase?”

“I haven’t an idea,” I said. “Not right away, anyhow. He’s so used to my settled ways that I don’t think he’ll suspect anything till he finds my note. I wonder what kind of story Mrs. McNally will tell!”

===Chinese===
出了农庄，刚瞧不见屋子，大路便分了岔：一条直通沃尔顿（Walton），到那儿过一座廊桥便越了河；另一条蜿蜒而下，通向格林布赖尔（Greenbriar）和波特维戈（Port Vigor）。柯林斯太太家就住在沃尔顿道上约莫一英里外，我平日常跑去看她，所以我想，安德鲁八成会头一个到那儿找我。于是，穿过了那片小树林，我便朝右手边格林布赖尔的岔道拐了过去。我们开始长途爬坡，翻越越橘山（Huckleberry Hill），闻着枝叶间清新的秋天气息，我暗自笑了。

米夫林先生仿佛得意到了陶醉的地步。“这可真是妙极了，”他说，“老天，我真佩服您的胆气。您说麦吉尔先生会追上来吗？”

“我说不上来，”我说，“反正不会马上追。他太习惯我一成不变的日子了，不见着我的字条，怕是起不了疑心。真不知麦克纳利太太会讲出一套什么话来！”

===Original===
“How about putting him off the scent?” he said. “Give me your handkerchief.”

I did so. He hopped nimbly out, ran back down the hill (he was a spry little person in spite of his bald crown), and dropped the handkerchief on the Walton Road about a hundred feet beyond the fork. Then he followed me up the slope.

“There,” he said, grinning like a kid, “that’ll fool him. The Sage of Redfield will undoubtedly follow a false spoor and the criminals will win a good start. But I’m afraid it’s rather easy to follow a craft as unusual as Parnassus.”

===Chinese===
“要不要把他引入歧途？”他说，“把您的手绢给我。”

我递给了他。他灵巧地一纵跳下车，一路小跑回下坡去了（别看他头顶光溜溜的，腿脚可麻利着呢），把手绢丢在岔路口往外约一百英尺的沃尔顿道上，然后又跟上来爬坡。

“好了，”他咧着嘴笑得像个孩子，“这下能蒙他了。雷德菲尔德的圣哲准会顺着这条假踪迹追下去，咱们这对罪犯就赢了个漂亮的开局。不过话说回来，帕纳索斯号这么个与众不同的家伙，人家要跟梢还不容易么。”

===Original===
“Tell me how you manage the thing,” I said. “Do you really make it pay?” We halted at the top of the hill to give Pegasus a breathing space. The terrier lay down in the dust and watched us gravely. Mr. Mifflin pulled out a pipe and begged my permission to smoke.

“It’s rather comical how I first got into it,” he said. “I was a school teacher down in Maryland. I’d been plugging away in a country school for years, on a starvation salary. I was trying to support an invalid mother, and put by something in case of storms. I remember how I used to wonder whether I’d ever be able to wear a suit that wasn’t shabby and have my shoes polished every day. Then my health went back on me. The doctor told me to get into the open air. By and by I got this idea of a travelling bookstore. I had always been a lover of books, and in the days when I boarded out among the farmers I used to read aloud to them. After my mother died I built the wagon to suit my own ideas, bought a stock of books from a big secondhand store in Baltimore, and set out. Parnassus just about saved my life I guess.”

He pushed his faded old cap back on his head and relit his pipe. I clicked to Pegasus and we rumbled gently off over the upland, looking down across the pastures. Distant cow bells sounded *tankle-tonk* among the bushes. Across the slope of the hill I could see the road winding away to Redfield. Somewhere along that road Andrew would be rolling back toward home and roast pork with apple sauce; and here was I, setting out on the first madness of my life without even a qualm.

“跟我讲讲你是怎么操持这营生的，”我说，“当真有钱可赚？”我们在坡顶停下来，好让佩加索斯喘口气。那条梗犬往尘土里一趴，一本正经地望着我们。米夫林先生掏出烟斗，请我准他抽烟。

“我怎么起头的，说来还挺滑稽，”他说，“我从前在马里兰教书，在乡下学校里苦熬了好些年，挣一份饿不死的薪水。我要养活一位多病的母亲，还想攒点钱防个风雨。我还记得，那时候我常琢磨，这辈子能不能穿上一身体面衣裳，皮鞋能不能天天擦得亮亮的。后来我的身体垮了，医生叫我到露天里去。渐渐的，我想出了这个流动书店的主意。我一向是个爱书的人，早年在农户家搭伙的时候，就常给他们读书听。母亲去世后，我照着自己的心思造了这辆车，从巴尔的摩一家大旧书铺进了批书，就上了路。我估摸着，是帕纳索斯号捡回了我这条命。”

他把那顶褪色的旧帽往后脑一推，重新点上烟斗。我朝佩加索斯咂了个响舌，车子便咕隆咕隆地驶过高地，一路俯瞰着山坡下的牧场。远处的牛铃声在灌木丛里“叮啷——咚”地响。隔着山坡，我望得见那条大路蜿蜒通向雷德菲尔德。安德鲁这会儿正在那条路上的什么地方往家赶，赶回去吃烤猪肉配苹果酱；而我呢，平生头一遭撒开疯，心里居然连个咯噔都没打。

===Original===
“Miss McGill,” said the little man, “this rolling pavilion has been wife, doctor, and religion to me for seven years. A month ago I would have scoffed at the thought of leaving her; but somehow it’s come over me I need a change. There’s a book I’ve been yearning to write for a long time, and I need a desk steady under my elbows and a roof over my head. And silly as it seems, I’m crazy to get back to Brooklyn. My brother and I used to live there as kids. Think of walking over the old Bridge at sunset and seeing the towers of Manhattan against a red sky! And those old gray cruisers down in the Navy Yard! You don’t know how tickled I am to sell out. I’ve sold a lot of copies of your brother’s books and I’ve often thought he’d be the man to buy Parnassus if I got tired of her.”

“So he would,” I said. “Just the man. He’d be only too likely to—and go maundering about in this jaunting car and neglect the farm. But tell me about selling books. How much profit do you make out of it? We’ll be passing Mrs. Mason’s farm, by and by, and we might as well sell her something just to make a start.”

“It’s very simple,” he said. “I replenish my stock whenever I go through a big town. There’s always a secondhand bookstore somewhere about, where you can pick up odds and ends. And every now and then I write to a wholesaler in New York for some stuff. When I buy a book I mark in the back just what I paid for it, then I know what I can afford to sell it for. See here.”

“麦吉尔小姐，”小个子说，“这座会滚动的亭子，七年来就是我的妻、我的医生、我的宗教。一个月前，谁要说离开她，我准嗤之以鼻；可不知怎么，我忽然觉着该换换活法了。有一本书，我惦着写好久了，我需要一张胳膊肘搁得稳的书桌，头顶上需要一片屋顶。说来也傻，我就是一门心思想回布鲁克林。我兄弟和我小时候住在那儿。想想日落时分走过那座老桥，望着曼哈顿的塔影衬着满天红霞！还有海军造船厂里那些灰色的老巡洋舰！您不知道我把这营生脱手有多乐。您兄弟的书我卖出去过好多本，我常想，哪天我腻了，他正是接手帕纳索斯号的主儿。”

“可不是，”我说，“正是这么个主儿。他巴不得这样——好坐上这辆游乐车四处闲逛荡，把农庄撂在脑后。不过给我讲讲卖书的事吧。这里头你能挣多少？前头咱们就要经过梅森太太（Mrs. Mason）家的农庄了，正好卖她点什么，开个张。”

He pulled up a book from behind the seat—a copy of *Lorna Doone* it was—and showed me the letters *a m* scrawled in pencil in the back.

“That means that I paid ten cents for this. Now, if you sell it for a quarter you’ve got a safe profit. It costs me about four dollars a week to run Parnassus—generally less. If you clear that much in six days you can afford to lay off on Sundays!”

“How do you know that *a m* stands for ten cents?” I asked.

===Original===
I began to see something of the little man’s idealism in his work. He was a kind of traveling missionary in his way. A hefty talker, too. His eyes were twinkling now and I could see him warming up.

“Lord!” he said, “when you sell a man a book you don’t sell him just twelve ounces of paper and ink and glue—you sell him a whole new life. Love and friendship and humour and ships at sea by night—there’s all heaven and earth in a book, a real book I mean. Jiminy! If I were the baker or the butcher or the broom huckster, people would run to the gate when I came by—just waiting for my stuff. And here I go loaded with everlasting salvation—yes, ma’am, salvation for their little, stunted minds—and it’s hard to make ’em see it. That’s what makes it worth while—I’m doing something that nobody else from Nazareth, Maine, to Walla Walla, Washington, has ever thought of. It’s a new field, but by the bones of Whitman it’s worthwhile. That’s what this country needs—more books!”

He laughed at his own vehemence. “Do you know, it’s comical,” he said. “Even the publishers, the fellows that print the books, can’t see what I’m doing for them. Some of ’em refuse me credit because I sell their books for what they’re worth instead of for the prices they mark on them. They write me letters about price-maintenance—and I write back about merit-maintenance. Publish a good book and I’ll get a good price for it, say I! Sometimes I think the publishers know less about books than anyone else! I guess that’s natural, though. Most school teachers don’t know much about children.”

我开始看出这小个子干这行当里的一点理想主义了。他算是某种云游四方的传教士，也是个健谈的家伙。这会儿他两眼放光，我瞧得出他越说越来劲。

“老天！”他说，“你卖给一个人一本书，卖的可不只是十二盎司的纸、墨水和胶水——你卖给他的是整整一个新生命。爱情、友谊、幽默，还有夜里海上的航船——一本书里有着整个天与地，我指的是一本真正的书。天哪！我要是个面包师、屠夫，或者卖扫帚的小贩，人们听见我路过，准会一路跑到大门口来——就等着我的货呢。而我这一车拉的可是不朽的救恩——没错，太太，是拯救他们那些瘦弱小心智的救恩——偏偏难得让他们开窍。这正是这行当值钱的地方——我干的这件事，从缅因州的拿撒勒到华盛顿州的瓦拉瓦拉，再没有第二个人想到过。这是个新开的园地，可凭惠特曼（Whitman）的骨头起誓，它值得干。这个国家缺的就是这个——更多的书！”

他为自己的慷慨激昂笑了。“您知道吗，说来好笑，”他说，“连出版商——就是印书的那帮家伙——都看不出我在为他们做什么。有几家不肯赊账给我，因为我卖他们的书，是按书本身值多少钱卖，而不是按他们标的定价卖。他们写信跟我谈什么‘维持书价’——我回信跟他们谈‘维持品质’。出一本好书，我自然能卖个好价钱，我说！有时候我想，出版商对书的了解，比谁都少！不过这也自然。大多数教书先生对孩子的了解也多不到哪儿去。”

“The best of it is,” he went on, “I have such a darn good time. Peg and Bock (that’s the dog) and I go loafing along the road on a warm summer day, and by and by we’ll fetch up alongside some boardinghouse and there are the boarders all rocking off their lunch on the veranda. Most of ’em bored to death—nothing good to read, nothing to do but sit and watch the flies buzzing in the sun and the chickens rubbing up and down in the dust. First thing you know I’ll sell half a dozen books that put the love of life into them, and they don’t forget Parnassus in a hurry. Take O. Henry, for instance—there isn’t anybody so doggone sleepy that he won’t enjoy that man’s stories. He understood life, you bet, and he could write it down with all its little twists. I’ve spent an evening reading O. Henry and Wilkie Collins to people and had them buy out all their books I had and clamour for more.”

“What do you do in winter?” I asked—a practical question, as most of mine are.

“最妙的是，”他接着说，“我自个儿也痛快得很。暖和的夏日，佩格、博克（Bock，就是那条狗）和我沿着大路悠悠荡荡，走着走着就傍上一家供膳宿舍，宿客们正在游廊上摇着摇椅消食呢。他们多半都闷得要死——没有好书可读，没事可做，只能干坐着瞧苍蝇在太阳底下嗡嗡转，瞧母鸡在尘土里蹭来蹭去。一转眼的工夫，我就卖出半打书去，把生活的热爱重新灌进他们心窝，他们一时半会儿忘不了帕纳索斯号。就拿欧·亨利来说吧——再瞌睡闷倦的人，读那人的故事也能读得眉开眼笑。他懂得生活，这错不了，还能把它连那些小小曲折一起原样写下来。我好几个晚上给人朗读欧·亨利和威尔基·柯林斯，他们把我带来的书抢购一空，还嚷嚷着要再买。”

“That depends on where I am when bad weather sets in,” said Mr. Mifflin. “Two winters I was down south and managed to keep Parnassus going all through the season. “Not to me,” I said. “He’s known me so long that he thinks of me as a kind of animated bread-baking and cake-mixing machine. I guess he doesn’t put much stock in my judgment in literary matters. But he puts his digestion in my hands without reserve. There’s Mason’s farm over there. I guess we’d better sell them some books—hadn’t we? Just for a starter.”

We turned into the lane that runs up to the Mason farmhouse. Bock trotted on ahead—very stiff on his legs and his tail gently wagging—to interview the mastiff, and Mrs. Mason who was sitting on the porch, peeling potatoes, laid down the pan.
