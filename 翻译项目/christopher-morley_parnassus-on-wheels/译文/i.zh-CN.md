## I / 一

===Original===
I wonder if there isn't a lot of bunkum in higher education? I never found that people who were learned in logarithms and other kinds of poetry were any quicker in washing dishes or darning socks. I've done a good deal of reading when I could, and I don't want to "admit impediments" to the love of books, but I've also seen lots of good, practical folk spoiled by too much fine print. Reading sonnets always gives me hiccups, too.

I never expected to be an author! But I do think there are some amusing things about the story of Andrew and myself and how books broke up our placid life. When John Gutenberg, whose real name (so the Professor says) was John Gooseflesh, borrowed that money to set up his printing press he launched a lot of troubles on the world.

Andrew and I were wonderfully happy on the farm until he became an author. If I could have foreseen all the bother his writings were to cause us, I would certainly have burnt the first manuscript in the kitchen stove.

===Chinese===
我总疑心，高等教育里是不是掺了大量的空话？我从没发现，精通对数以及其他各类诗篇的人，洗起盘子、补起袜子来能比旁人麻利多少。得空的时候我也读过不少书，无意“承认什么障碍”来阻挠我对书籍的爱恋，可我也亲眼见过不少善良务实的人，被太多细字条文给毁了。再说，读十四行诗总叫我打嗝。

我从没想过要当作家！不过我确实觉得，我和安德鲁（Andrew）的故事——书是怎样搅散了我们平静的日子——里头颇有些好玩的情节。想当年约翰·古腾堡（John Gutenberg）借钱开印刷所的时候——据教授说，他的真名其实是约翰·鹅皮（John Gooseflesh）——他就给这世界放出了数不清的祸端。

在安德鲁当上作家之前，我和他在农场上快活得很。倘若我能预见他的那些文字将要给我们惹来多少麻烦，我准会把他第一部手稿塞进厨房炉子里烧个干净。

===Original===
Andrew McGill, the author of those books everyone reads, is my brother. In other words, I am his sister, ten years younger. Years ago Andrew was a business man, but his health failed and, like so many people in the story books, he fled to the country, or, as he called it, to the bosom of Nature. He and I were the only ones left in an unsuccessful family. I was slowly perishing as a conscientious governess in the brownstone region of New York. He rescued me from that and we bought a farm with our combined savings. We became real farmers, up with the sun and to bed with the same. Andrew wore overalls and a soft shirt and grew brown and tough. My hands got red and blue with soapsuds and frost; I never saw a Redfern advertisement from one year's end to another, and my kitchen was a battlefield where I set my teeth and learned to love hard work. Our literature was government agriculture reports, patent medicine almanacs, seedsmen's booklets, and Sears Roebuck catalogues. We subscribed to *Farm and Fireside* and read the serials aloud. Every now and then, for real excitement, we read something stirring in the Old Testament—that cheery book Jeremiah, for instance, of which Andrew was very fond. The farm did actually prosper, after a while; and Andrew used to hang over the pasture bars at sunset, and tell, from the way his pipe burned, just what the weather would be the next day.

As I have said, we were tremendously happy until Andrew got the fatal idea of telling the world how happy we were. I am sorry to have to admit he had always been rather a bookish man. In his college days he had edited the students' magazine, and sometimes he would get discontented with the *Farm and Fireside* serials and pull down his bound volumes of the college paper. He would read me some of his youthful poems and stories and mutter vaguely about writing something himself some day. I was more concerned with sitting hens than with sonnets and I'm bound to say I never took these threats very seriously. I should have been more severe.

Then great-uncle Philip died, and his carload of books came to us. He had been a college professor, and years ago when Andrew was a boy Uncle Philip had been very fond of him—had, in fact, put him through college. We were the only near relatives, and all those books turned up one fine day. That was the beginning of the end, if I had only known it. Andrew had the time of his life building shelves all round our living-room; not content with that he turned the old hen house into a study for himself, put in a stove, and used to sit up there evenings after I had gone to bed. The first thing I knew he called the place Sabine Farm (although it had been known for years as Bog Hollow) because he thought it a literary thing to do. He used to take a book along with him when he drove over to Redfield for supplies; sometimes the wagon would be two hours late coming home, with old Ben loafing along between the shafts and Andrew lost in his book.

===Chinese===
安德鲁·麦吉尔（Andrew McGill）——那些人人都在读的书的作者——是我哥哥。换句话说，我是他妹妹，比他小十岁。多年以前安德鲁是个生意人，后来身体垮了，就像故事书里那么多人一样，逃到了乡下——用他自己的话说，是逃进了大自然（Nature）的怀抱。我们那个不走运的家里，剩下的只有他和我。我那时在纽约的褐石宅区当家庭女教师，兢兢业业，人却一日日枯萎下去。他把我从那境地里搭救出来，我们拿出两人的积蓄合买了一座农场。我们成了地道的庄稼人，日出而起，随日落而息。安德鲁穿工装裤和软领衬衫，晒得又黑又结实。我的手叫肥皂沫和寒霜泡得又红又青；一年到头我见不着一则雷德芬（Redfern）时装广告，我的厨房是一片战场，我在那里咬紧牙关，学会了爱上苦役。我们的精神食粮是政府农业报告、专利药品历书、种子商的小册子，还有西尔斯-罗巴克（Sears Roebuck）百货目录。我们订了《农庄与炉边》（Farm and Fireside），把连载小说大声念出来。隔三差五，为了来点真正的刺激，我们就读一段《旧约》里慷慨激昂的文字——比方说那卷欢畅的《耶利米书》，安德鲁就对它偏爱得很。过了一阵子，农场当真兴旺起来；每到日落，安德鲁总要伏在牧场栏门上，单凭他那烟斗烧得怎么样，把第二天的天气说得一清二楚。

正如我方才所说，我们快活得了不得，直到安德鲁起了那个致命的念头——要向全世界宣告我们有多快活。很遗憾，我不得不承认，他骨子里一直是个书生气的人。念大学的时候他编过学生杂志，有时他对《农庄与炉边》的连载心生不满，就把装订成册的大学校刊搬下来翻。他会给我念几首他少年时代写的诗和故事，含含糊糊地嘀咕着，说哪天自己也要写点什么。我关心的更多是抱窝的母鸡而不是十四行诗，而且我必须说，这些“威胁”我从来没太当过真。我本该更严厉些的。

后来菲利普（Philip）伯祖父去世了，他满满一车书归了我们。他生前是大学教授，多年前安德鲁还是个孩子的时候，菲利普伯父就顶喜欢他——事实上，是他供安德鲁念的大学。我们是仅存的近亲，于是那所有的书便在某个晴朗的日子里运到了。倘若我早知道，那便是末日的开端。安德鲁这辈子从没那么快活过：他绕着起居室四面钉满了书架；还不满足，又把旧鸡舍改成自己的书房，装上炉子，晚上常在我睡下之后还坐在那上头。不知打什么时候起，他管这地方叫萨宾农场（Sabine Farm）——虽然它多年来一直叫泥沼洼（Bog Hollow）——因为他觉得这才够文学味儿。他赶车去雷德菲尔德（Redfield）办货时总要捎上一本书；有时马车迟迟不归，晚了两小时，老本（Ben）在车辕之间慢悠悠地晃，安德鲁整个人钻进书里出不来。

===Original===
I didn't think much of all this, but I'm an easygoing woman and as long as Andrew kept the farm going I had plenty to do on my own hook. Hot bread and coffee, eggs and preserves for breakfast; soup and hot meat, vegetables, dumplings, gravy, brown bread and white, huckleberry pudding, chocolate cake and buttermilk for dinner; muffins, tea, sausage rolls, blackberries and cream, and doughnuts for supper—that's the kind of menu I had been preparing three times a day for years. I hadn't any time to worry about what wasn't my business.

And then one morning I caught Andrew doing up a big, flat parcel for the postman. He looked so sheepish I just had to ask what it was.

"I've written a book," said Andrew, and he showed me the title page:

> **Paradise Regained 

 by 

 Andrew McGill**

===Chinese===
这些事我倒没多想。我是个随和的女人，只要安德鲁把农场撑着，我自己手头的活计就够我忙的。早餐是热面包加咖啡、鸡蛋和果酱；正餐是汤和热肉、蔬菜、面团子、肉汁、黑面包白面包、越橘布丁、巧克力蛋糕和酪乳；晚饭是玛芬、茶、香肠卷、黑莓拌奶油，还有炸面圈——多年来，一日三餐，我操办的就是这样一份菜单。不是分内的事，我可没工夫去操心。

后来有一天早上，我撞见安德鲁在给邮差打一个大大的扁平包裹。他那副羞赧模样，逼得我不能不问一句那是什么。

“我写了一本书。”安德鲁说，还把扉页拿给我看：

> **《复乐园》（Paradise Regained）

 作者

 安德鲁·麦吉尔（Andrew McGill）**

===Original===
Even then I wasn't much worried, because of course I knew no one would print it. But Lord! a month or so later came a letter from a publisher—accepting it! That's the letter Andrew keeps framed above his desk. Just to show how such things sound I'll copy it here:

> Dear Mr. McGill:
> We have read with singular pleasure your manuscript *Paradise Regained*. There is no doubt in our minds that so spirited an account of the joys of sane country living should meet with popular approval, and, with the exception of a few revisions and abbreviations, we would be glad to publish the book practically as it stands. We would like to have it illustrated by Mr. Tortoni, some of whose work you may have seen, and would be glad to know whether he may call upon you in order to acquaint himself with the local colour of your neighbourhood.
> We would be glad to pay you a royalty of 10 percent upon the retail price of the book, and we enclose duplicate contracts for your signature in case this proves satisfactory to you.
> Believe us, etc., etc.,
> Decameron, Jones & Co.

===Chinese===
即便到那时我也没怎么担心，因为我自然知道，不会有人把它印出来的。可是老天爷！大约一个月之后，一家出版社来了信——居然录用了！就是那封信，如今还装在框子里，挂在安德鲁书桌上方。为了让各位见识见识这类信件的腔调，我把它抄在这里：

> 亲爱的麦吉尔先生：
> 我们怀着莫大的愉悦，拜读了您的手稿《复乐园》。我们毫不怀疑，这样一部神采飞扬地记述健全乡居生活之乐的作品，理应博得大众的嘉许；除少量修改与删节之外，我们乐意按原样将书付梓。我们希望延请托尔托尼（Tortoni）先生为本书绘制插图——他的作品您想必见识过——并乐于知晓能否容他登门拜访，领略贵地风光，采撷地方色彩。
> 我们乐意按本书零售价支付您 10% 的版税，并随函附上合同一式两份，敬请署名，如蒙满意。
> 请相信我们，云云，
> 德卡梅隆-琼斯公司（Decameron, Jones & Co.）

===Original===
I have since thought that "Paradise Lost" would have been a better title for that book. It was published in the autumn of 1907, and since that time our life has never been the same. By some mischance the book became the success of the season; it was widely commended as "a gospel of health and sanity" and Andrew received, in almost every mail, offers from publishers and magazine editors who wanted to get hold of his next book. It is almost incredible to what stratagems publishers will descend to influence an author. Andrew had written in *Paradise Regained* of the tramps who visit us, how quaint and appealing some of them are (let me add, how dirty), and how we never turn away anyone who seems worthy. Would you believe that, in the spring after the book was published, a disreputable-looking vagabond with a knapsack, who turned up one day, blarneyed Andrew about his book and stayed overnight, announced himself at breakfast as a leading New York publisher? He had chosen this ruse in order to make Andrew's acquaintance.

You can imagine that it didn't take long for Andrew to become spoiled at this rate! The next year he suddenly disappeared, leaving only a note on the kitchen table, and tramped all over the state for six weeks collecting material for a new book. I had all I could do to keep him from going to New York to talk to editors and people of that sort. Envelopes of newspaper cuttings used to come to him, and he would pore over them when he ought to have been ploughing corn. Luckily the mail man comes along about the middle of the morning when Andrew is out in the fields, so I used to look over the letters before he saw them. After the second book (*Happiness and Hayseed* it was called) was printed, letters from publishers got so thick that I used to put them all in the stove before Andrew saw them—except those from the Decameron Jones people, which sometimes held checks. Literary folk used to turn up now and then to interview Andrew, but generally I managed to head them off.

===Chinese===
后来我常想，那本书若叫《失乐园》倒是更贴切的题名。它出版于 1907 年秋天，打那以后，我们的日子就再没安生过。不知怎么阴差阳错，那本书竟成了当季的畅销之作，被交口称誉为“一部健康与理智的福音书”，安德鲁几乎每班邮件里都能收到出版社和杂志编辑的来函，人人都想攥住他的下一本书。为了左右一位作家，出版商肯使出何等下作的计策，简直令人难以置信。安德鲁在《复乐园》里写到过那些上门来的流浪汉，说他们有的多么古怪又惹人怜（容我补一句，多么脏），还说凡是看着像正经人的，我们从不撵走。您能信吗？书出版后的那个春天，来了个背行囊的流浪汉，一副落魄相，巧舌如簧地奉承安德鲁的书，还留宿了一夜，到第二天早饭桌上才亮明身份——原来是纽约一家头面出版社的人物！他使这一招，就是为了结识安德鲁。

您可以想见，照这个势头，要不了多久安德鲁就叫人给惯坏了！第二年他忽然不见了，只在厨房桌上留了张字条，然后徒步走遍全州，浪荡了六个星期，为一本新书搜罗材料。我费了九牛二虎之力，才拦住他没去纽约找编辑们之流的人物谈话。装着剪报的信封源源不断地寄来，他本该去犁玉米地，却趴在那上头逐字研读。幸而邮差总在上午过半、安德鲁下地的时候来，所以我惯于抢在他之前把信先过一遍。第二本书（书名是《幸福与乡巴佬》〔Happiness and Hayseed〕）印出来之后，出版商的来信多得成灾，我就常常趁安德鲁没瞧见，把它们统统塞进炉子烧掉——只有德卡梅隆-琼斯那班人的信除外，那些信里偶尔夹着支票。文人墨客也不时登门要采访安德鲁，不过一般来说，我总能把他们挡驾回去。

But Andrew got to be less and less of a farmer and more and more of a literary man. He bought a typewriter. He would hang over the pigpen noting down adjectives for the sunset instead of mending the weather vane on the barn which took a slew so that the north wind came from the southwest. He hardly ever looked at the Sears Roebuck catalogues any more, and after Mr. Decameron came to visit us and suggested that Andrew write a book of country poems, the man became simply unbearable.

And all the time I was counting eggs and turning out three meals a day, and running the farm when Andrew got a literary fit and would go off on some vagabond jaunt to collect adventures for a new book. (I wish you could have seen the state he was in when he came back from these trips, hoboing it along the roads without any money or a clean sock to his back. One time he returned with a cough you could hear the other side of the barn, and I had to nurse him for three weeks.) When somebody wrote a little booklet about "The Sage of Redfield" and described me as a "rural Xantippe" and "the domestic balance-wheel that kept the great writer close to the homely realities of life" I made up my mind to give Andrew some of his own medicine. And that's my story.

===Chinese===
可是安德鲁越来越不像个农夫，越来越像个文人了。他买了一台打字机。他会伏在猪圈沿上，搜罗落日的形容词，却不肯去修谷仓上那歪了向的风向标——那玩意儿转偏了一向，弄得北风竟从西南方刮来。西尔斯-罗巴克的目录他几乎再也不翻了，而自从德卡梅隆（Decameron）先生上门做客、撺掇他写一本田园诗集之后，他简直变得叫人忍无可忍。

而这期间，我一直在数鸡蛋、一日三餐照做饭不误；每逢安德鲁文思发作、出门流浪采风、好为一本新书搜罗奇遇，我就得把农场也一并撑起来。（真希望你们能瞧瞧他这些旅行归来时的那副模样：一路像个流浪汉似的，身上没钱，连一双干净袜子都没有。有一回他咳着回来，那咳嗽声隔着谷仓都听得见，我足足护理了他三个星期。）后来什么人写了本小册子，谈“雷德菲尔德的圣哲”，把我形容成“乡下的克桑蒂佩”（Xantippe，苏格拉底之妻，以凶悍闻名）和“一架家用平衡轮，让这位大作家牢牢贴着生活朴素的现实”，我这才打定主意，要让安德鲁也尝尝他自己那套方子的滋味。我的故事，讲的就是这个。
