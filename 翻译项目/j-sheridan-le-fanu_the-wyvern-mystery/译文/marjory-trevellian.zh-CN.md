## LVII Marjory Trevellian / 第五十七章 玛乔丽·特雷维廉
===Original===

In order to throw a light upon the nature of some of the duties of Mr. Archdale, we must convey the reader in spirit, to some little distance.
In the sequestered country, about twelve miles south of Twyford, in a pretty nook formed by a wooded hollow close by the old byroad to Warhampton, stands an antique cottage, with a loft and two little windows peeping through the very steep thatched roof and high narrow gable﻿—gable and wall alike streaked and crossed with those black oak beams which formed the cage into whose interstices our ancestors built their brick and plaster. The steep roof runs out over a little porch which has a bench in one side of it. Another stone bench stands under the lattice window, the woodwork of which casement, as well as the black spars crossed and morticed in the walls, and even the curved brick chimney, look shrunk and warped by time, by which, too, the hatch at the door is rounded and furrowed, and the stone seat and window stones worn into curves and hollows, and such and so venerable is the air of the structure, with its ivy-bound porch, that one might fancy it the very farmhouse in which Anne Hathaway passed her girlhood.
Here dwelt good Mrs. Marjory Trevellian, some fifty years old and upward, with, I think, the kindest face and pleasantest laugh in that part of the country; a widow of many years; not very happy in her marriage, and quite content with her experience of the wedded state; quiet, cheerful, very industrious; with a little farm of three acres, and a cow; spinning sometimes, knitting at others, and when she could, taking in washing, and in all things approving herself diligent, cheerful, and honest.
With this kind, cheery, honest dame lived a little boy, the son of a Mr. Henry﻿—that was all she knew distinctly about his people. She called him her Fairy, and her Prince, and when curious people questioned her closely, she said that his father was a merchant, “unfortunate in business,” as the phrase is; that he was living perhaps in concealment, and in distressed circumstances, or possibly was dead. All she could say for certain was, that she received a very small allowance for maintaining him, which was paid punctually every three months in advance, and that as to the name of the boy, his Christian name was William and his surname Henry, and that she called him her “Prince” or her “Fairy,” and he called her “Granny.”

要把阿奇代尔（Archdale）先生某些差事的性质说个明白，我们须得在想象中把读者带往一小段路之外。
在特怀福德（Twyford）以南约十二英里一处幽僻的乡间，紧挨着通往沃勒姆普顿（Warhampton）的老小路，一座林木环抱的洼地围出个秀美的小角落，其中立着一座古旧的村舍：带一间阁楼，两扇小窗从极陡的茅草顶和高窄的山墙间探出头来——山墙和墙体一样，被那些黑橡木梁纵横刻画；我们的祖先就是拿这些木梁扎成笼架，把砖和灰浆填进格眼里的。陡顶探出，罩住一个小门廊，廊子一侧设着条凳。花格窗下还有一条石凳；那窗子的木框，连同嵌进墙里榫接交叉的黑木条，甚至那弯弯的砖烟囱，都叫岁月浸得缩了、翘了；门上的下格门也磨圆了、起了沟，石凳和窗台石磨出了曲面和凹窝；这房子连同它爬满常春藤的门廊，通身一副德高望重的气派，直叫人疑心，安妮·海瑟薇（Anne Hathaway）度过少女时代的，正是这一座农舍。
这里住着好心的玛乔丽·特雷维廉（Marjory Trevellian）太太，五十开外；依我看，她那张脸、那一串笑声，是那一方乡里顶和善、顶受听的。寡居多年；婚姻里没享着多少福，对已婚生涯的阅历却也心满意足；安静，快活，勤快得很；守着三英亩的小田庄和一头奶牛；时而纺纺线，时而织织补补，得便还替人浆洗；样样事情上，都做得勤恳、爽利、诚实。
同这位和善、爽快、诚实的太太住在一起的，是个小男孩，某位亨利（Henry）先生的儿子——他的家人，她清楚知道的仅止于此。她唤他她的「精灵」，她的「王子」；好奇的人刨根问底，她就说，他父亲是位商人，「生意上折了本」——套话说就是这样；他也许隐姓埋名活着，境况窘迫，也可能已经死了。她能咬定的只有：抚养他的费用她按季预支、分文不差地领着，一笔数目很小的津贴；至于孩子的名字，教名威廉（William），姓亨利；她叫她的小家伙「王子」或「精灵」，他叫她「婆婆」（Granny）。

She idolised this pretty boy, and he loved her with the tenderness which a child bestows upon a loving nurse, something more than filial.
The boy remembers no other home but this, and no other friend but “Granny.” He was now a little past eleven. His life had been solitary, but cheerful. Was there not the pond only thirty yards away from their doorstep, in which he sailed his fleet of ships, made of corks, which old Peter Durdon gave him? He was a cousin of Marjory Trevellian’s, and lived in the village two miles away. He used to call every Sunday and to bring these corks in his pocket, and a bit of such lead as tea is wrapped in to make the keels of their navy. He was dressed in a blue “swallow-tailed” coat with brass buttons; his drab trousers were very short; his stockings faded sky-blue; and his shoes clumsy and clouted, and highly polished. He wore a chestnut wig of a long and lank cut, and his forehead slanted back very much, and his nose came forward, and a perpetual smile expanded his cheeks, which were as red and smooth as a ripe apple. His countenance was not wise, though very good-natured﻿—rather silly, I’m afraid﻿—and I think he took more interest in this sort of shipping than was quite compatible with strength of mind.
As these ships glided with thin paper sails across the pond, while Master Henry watched them in grave absorption, Peter’s raptures expressed themselves in continuous peals of laughter.
These were great occasions in the solitary life of Fairy.
There were a set of big boxwood ninepins﻿—skittles, I suppose, with balls﻿—battered and discoloured﻿—I never knew how they got into the cottage, but they looked a hundred years old if a day. Many a game with these on the smooth patch of sward at the other side of the pond had pleasant old Marjory with her darling.
In its seclusion its life was monastic, but not in its liberty. The boy was, on the whole, very happy.
Looking on honest Marjory as mistress of all she surveyed, it never struck him, that in the points in which her dietary differed from his she was practising a compulsory economy. The article of meat was not often found in her bill of fare.

她把这漂亮娃娃疼得像心尖子；他也爱她，怀着孩子给予慈爱乳母的那种依恋——比孝心还多着几分。
这男孩记不得此外还有别的家，也记不得「婆婆」之外还有别的朋友。他刚过十一岁。日子过得孤单，却快活。离他们门口不过三十码不是有一方池塘么，他的船队就在那儿航行——船是软木塞做的，老彼得·达登（Peter Durdon）给的。他是玛乔丽·特雷维廉的表亲，住在两英里外的村子里。每逢星期天必来，口袋里捫着这些软木塞，还有包茶叶的那种铅皮一小块，给他们的舰队做龙骨。他穿一件铜扣子的蓝「燕尾」外套；黄褐色的裤子短得不能再短；长袜褪成了浅浅的天蓝；鞋子笨重，钉着掌，倒擦得锃亮。他戴一顶栗色假发，又长又塌；额头狠狠向后斜，鼻子使劲向前冲，两颊永远别着一副笑，红润光滑得像只熟苹果。这副面相谈不上聪明，虽说一团和气——恐怕还相当傻——而且我疑心，他对这类航运的兴致，超出了一颗坚强心智所宜有的分量。
这些船张着薄薄的纸帆掠过池面，亨利（Henry）少爷神情严肃、全神贯注地望着；彼得的狂喜则化作一阵接一阵的哄笑。
在精灵孤单的日子里，这些便是盛大的节庆。
屋里还有一套大黄杨木九柱——我想，是连球带柱的九柱戏——磕碰得坑坑洼洼，颜色也斑驳了——它们怎么进的这间村舍，我始终不知；可那模样，说一百岁绝只多不少。池塘那头一片平整草皮上，快活的老玛乔丽同她的心肝，就着这套木柱玩过多少回。
这样的隐居，日子过得像修道院，自由却不像。总的说来，这男孩很快活。
他把老实的玛乔丽看作她眼前一切的主妇，从未想到：饭食上那些同他两样的地方，其实是在强行节省。她的菜单上，肉这一味是不常见的。

But conscientiously she placed the little fellow’s bit of broiled meat before him every day, and told him when he inquired why she had none for herself that she did not like it, and that it did not agree with her, which he accepted as undoubted truths, and wondered and regretted secretly.
On winter evenings their tea was very cosy. A wheaten cake baked on the griddle, a new-laid egg each, and a cup of tea from the many-coloured delf teapot﻿—a good deal burnt on the side next the fire. With the door barred and the window carefully closed, the fire burning cheerfully, and their candle lighting the party﻿—who so happy? And was there not the old *Robinson Crusoe*, with binding black with age, and a frontispiece showing the hero with his grave countenance and beard, his tall cap and goatskin dress, his musket over one shoulder and his umbrella over the other, and recounting his marvellous life in the quaint old type of Queen Anne? And was there not that other literary treasure, the old folio volume of Captain Cook’s, Commodore Anson’s, and other seafaring worthies’ voyages round or up and down the world, with no end of careful old copperplates, showing Pacific islands, curious volcanoes, flotillas of armed canoes, thick-lipped miscreants with rings in their noses and birds’ tails enlivening their foreheads, and long processions of official people, priests, etc., with a small white pocket-handkerchief each by way of dress? But better far than these, which together with her Bible and Prayer Book, constituted Marjory’s library, was that good creature’s inexhaustible collection of fairy tales, received traditionally and recounted viva voce, and prefaced with the rhyme which even at this distance recalls me to the nursery fireside with the far-off tones of a kindly voice that I shall hear no more.

> “Once upon a time there was a king and a queen,

As many have been,

But few I have seen,

Except in pictures!”

可她每天总把小家伙那一小片烤肉端端正正摆在他面前；他问她自己怎么没有，她就说她不爱吃，吃了不合适。他把这些当作千真万确的实话收下，暗暗纳罕，又暗暗难过。
冬天的傍晚，他们的茶点最是惬意。一张烤炉上烙的麦饼，每人一枚新下的蛋，一杯茶——出自那把五彩釉的陶壶，朝着火的那一面燎得相当黑了。门上了闩，窗关得严实，炉火烧得欢，一支蜡烛照着一家人——还有谁比他们更快活？何况不是还有那本老《鲁滨逊漂流记》（Robinson Crusoe）么，封皮黑得上了年纪，卷首插画上，主人公一脸庄严的胡须，高帽、羊皮装，一肩捫着火枪，一肩挂着伞，用安妮女王（Queen Anne）年间那种古雅的铅字，讲述他那奇绝的一生。何况不是还有另一件文学至宝：库克（Cook）船长、安森（Anson）准将同其他海上名流环游世界、上下纵横的航行记，老对开本一厚册，里面没完没了的精细老铜版画——太平洋的岛屿，古怪的火山，成队的武装独木舟，厚嘴唇的歹徒鼻子上穿着环、脑门上插着鸟尾翎，还有一列列官员、祭司之类，每人胯前只系一方小白手帕权当衣着。可远比这些更好的——这些连同她的《圣经》和《公祷书》，就是玛乔丽的全部藏书——是这位好人儿那座取之不竭的童话宝库：口耳相传，随口讲来，开篇必是那支韵语——隔着这么多年月，它还把我领回育儿炉边，领回那副我再也听不到的、慈祥的远调子。

And starting with this little trumpeting and summons to attention﻿—the “oyes-oyes-oyes” and immutable prelude of an ever-varying sequel, good Marjory, the herald of ever new wonders, would tell her tale of dwarfs and castles, of godmother fairies, and malignant enchantresses, brokenhearted princes and persecuted princesses, and enchanted palaces and awful forests, till the hour came for the little fellow to get to his bed and enter the no less wonderful land of dreams.
Another person who contributed to the regular entertainment of the boy was Tom Orange.
Tom Orange called at the cottage sometimes at intervals of three months, sometimes, for perhaps half a year, on the first of every month, and was always made welcome by Marjory Trevellian, and feasted with rashers and whatever else her humble larder afforded, and on going had established a mysterious right to a shilling “tip,” which he always made it a point should be an honourable secret among them.
What might be the nature of his business the little boy neither knew nor cared, but Tom Orange was in the boy’s eyes the ideal and epitome of all that was enchanting, brilliant, and exhilarating.
Tom was somewhat long and lean, with a face also long and always smiling, except when it was making a grimace, an art in which he excelled almost every other blackguard I have heard of. His clothes and hat were seedy, and, for so merry a person, he was wonderfully poor.
Tom Orange’s accomplishments were infinite, he could dance a hornpipe with all the well-known airs and graces of a sailor; he could protrude his mouth till it assumed a shape quite unknown to physiognomists, and with a delicate finger, turning his eyelids inside out, make the pupils of those organs quiver strangely, while he uttered a sound like the call of a jackdaw. He could sing a variety of comic songs, with refrains delivered with a volubility which distanced admiration, and made his very audience breathless, and some of these were relieved with occasional dialogue of matchless character and humour. He could swallow any number of pennies you pleased, and take them all out at different angles of his body; he could put several potatoes under his hat, and withdraw them all without touching either the hat or the potatoes. He could keep three balls always in the air together, and he could balance two chairs upon his chin.

就以这一小段鸣锣唱名——那声「静一静——静一静——」，万变的故事前那雷打不动的引子——开了场，好心的玛乔丽，这位层出不穷的新奇之事的传令官，便讲起她的故事：矮人和城堡，教母仙女和狠毒的妖妇，心碎的王子和受难的公主，着魔的宫殿和可怕的森林，直讲到小家伙该上床、走进同样精彩的梦乡的时辰。
为这男孩的日常欢乐出力的，另有一位：汤姆·奥林奇（Tom Orange）。
汤姆·奥林奇到村舍来串门，有时隔三个月来一回，有时连着约莫半年，每月一号必到；玛乔丽·特雷维廉回回欢迎他，拿咸肉片和粗陋食柜里所有的一切款待他；临走时，他还享有一项神秘的权利——一先令「赏钱」，而且总要把它办成两人之间一桩体面的秘密，这一点他从不含糊。
他的营生究竟是什么性质，小男孩既不知道，也不在意；可在男孩眼里，汤姆·奥林奇是一切迷人、光鲜、叫人畅快之事的化身和总纲。
汤姆身量颅长精瘦，脸也长，永远堆着笑——除非正做鬼脸；这门本事，我听说过的无赖里，几乎没人赶得上他。衣裳帽子都露着穷酸；人这样乐啊，却穷得出奇。
汤姆·奥林奇的本事没有穷尽。他会跳水手号笛舞，水手那套人尽皆知的风姿作派，一样不缺；他能把嘴榱成一种相面先生全然不识的形状；还能用一根灵巧的手指把眼皮翻出来，叫两只瞳仁古怪地颤动，嘴里同时发出寒鸦一般的叫声。他会唱各式各样的滑稽歌，副歌的饶舌快得叫赞叹追不上、叫听众大气透不过来，有几首里还插着几句人物口吻、诙谐无双的对白。你乐意数多少个铜板他都吞得下，还能从身上各个角度一一取出来；他能在帽子底下塞好几只土豆，不碰帽子也不碰土豆，把它们一一变没了；他能叫三只球总在空中不落，还能用下巴顶起两把椅子。

In short, as I have said, his accomplishments were innumerable and extraordinary, and the only wonder was how so universal a genius could possibly possess so few shillings and so many seedy articles of dress.
Tom Orange, too, was great at skittles, and gave his pupil wonderful new lights.
He taught him also how to guard, stop, and strike according to the principles of “the noble art of self-defence.” In fact, it would have been difficult to discover a more fascinating companion and instructor of youth. Possibly it was as well, however, that his visits were so far between, and as brief as fortune ordained them to be. It was no wonder, however, that these visits were looked for by the boy, as the return of the life and excitement of an annual fair might have been by the ingenuous youth of some other rural district.
There was but one point on which Marjory was obliged to impose a prohibition upon the child. It seemed a trifle, but in reality was a gigantic privation.
“No, darling, you mustn’t talk to any other boys, nor play with them, nor go near them; if you do you’ll be took away by your friends, and I’ll never see you again; and what will poor Granny do then without her darling?”
And Granny’s eyes filled with tears, and the boy cried and hugged her passionately, and this little agony gave place to wild affection and a glow of unspeakable delight and happiness, and was celebrated by a hot cake that evening, and new-laid eggs and a great tea, and stories to no end.
And she found her darling that night crying in his sleep, and was sure he was dreaming of leaving the old cottage, and she wakened him with kisses, herself crying.
So these two persons, notwithstanding some disparity of years, were wonderfully happy in one another’s society and if they had each their will, would have fixed things as they were, and neither grown older nor younger, but just gone on living so forever.

总而言之，如我所说，他的本事数不尽、也不同凡响；唯一叫人纳闷的是，这样一位全才，先令怎么会这样少，穷酸的衣物却这样多。
汤姆·奥林奇打九柱戏也是一把好手，给他的小弟子开过好些奇妙的新法门。
他还教他如何按「自卫之高尚艺术」的原理去格挡、封堵和出击。说实话，再找一位比他更迷人的青年伴侣兼教师，怕是难了。不过，他的来访隔得这样久、又这样短，兴倒是件好事。而男孩盼这些来访，也不奇怪——就像别的乡里那些天真少年，盼着一年一度集市的热闹和生气重新回来一样。
只有一件事，玛乔丽不得不对孩子下禁令。看着是桩小事，其实是天大的一份剥夺。
「不行，宝贝，不许同别的男孩子说话，不许同他们玩，也不许凑近他们；你要犯了，你的亲人们就会把你带走，我就再见不着你了；到那时候，婆婆没了心肝，可怎么活呢？」
婆婆眼里涌满泪水，男孩哭着，死死接住她；这一小阵痛苦过后，化成一场发狂的亲热，一团说不出的欢喜与幸福，当晚便烙了热饼庆贺，配上新下的蛋、一顿丰盛茶点，故事讲个没完。
当夜她发现心肝在睡梦里哭，认定他梦见离开老屋，便用吻唤醒他，自己也哭着。
就这样，这一老一小，尽管隔着不少年头，彼此做伴却快活非常；若由他们自己做主，事情就永远这样定住，谁也不老些，谁也不小些，就这么一直过下去，天长地久。
