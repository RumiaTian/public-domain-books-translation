## III / 三

===Original===
It was the third week in August. The weather was sultry; day after day Laura heard the village people telling each other that there was thunder in the air. Every evening they stood in the village street, looking upwards, and the cattle stood waiting in the fields. But the storm delayed. It hid behind the hills, biding its time.

Laura had spent the afternoon in a field, a field of unusual form, for it was triangular. On two sides it was enclosed by woodland, and because of this it was already darkening into a premature twilight, as though it were a room. She had been there for hours. Though it was sultry, she could not sit still. She walked up and down, turning savagely when she came to the edge of the field. Her limbs were tired, and she stumbled over the flints and matted couch-grass. Throughout the long afternoon a stock-dove had cooed in the wood. “Cool, cool, cool,” it said, delighting in its green bower. Now it had ceased, and there was no life in the woods. The sky was covered with a thick uniform haze. No ray of the declining sun broke through it, but the whole heavens were beginning to take on a dull, brassy pallor. The long afternoon was ebbing away, stealthily, impassively, as though it were dying under an anaesthetic.

Laura had not listened to the stock-dove; she had not seen the haze thickening overhead. She walked up and down in despair and rebellion. She walked slowly, for she felt the weight of her chains. Once more they had been fastened upon her. She had worn them for many years, acquiescently, scarcely feeling their weight. Now she felt it. And, with their weight, she felt their familiarity, and the familiarity was worst of all. Titus had seen her starting out. He had cried: “Where are you off to, Aunt Lolly? Wait a minute, and I’ll come too.” She had feigned not to hear him and had walked on. She had not turned her head until she was out of the village, she expected at every moment to hear him come bounding up behind her. Had he done so, she thought she would have turned round and snarled at him. For she wanted, oh! how much she wanted, to be left alone for once. Even when she felt pretty sure that she had escaped she could not profit by her solitude, for Titus’s voice still jangled on her nerves. “Where are you off to, Aunt Lolly? Wait a minute, and I’ll come too.” She heard his very tones, and heard intensely her own silence that had answered him. Too flustered to notice where she was going, she had followed a chance track until she found herself in this field where she had never been before. Here the track ended, and here she stayed.

劳拉没有听那斑鸠；她没有看见头顶的雾霭变浓。她怀着绝望和反叛走来走去。她走得慢，因为她觉出了锁链的分量。它们又一次被扣在了她身上。她戴过它们许多年，顺从地，几乎不觉其重。现在她觉出来了。伴着它们的重量，她觉出了它们的熟稔，而熟稔是最糟的。泰特斯看见她出门，喊道：「洛莉姑姑去哪儿？等一等，我也来。」她装作没听见，径自走了。出了村子她才回头，一路上时时刻刻都等着听见他从后面蹦跳着追上来。他若真追上来，她想，她会转过身去冲他龇牙。因为她想，啊！她多么想，哪怕就一次，一个人待着。等到她相当确定自己逃脱了，她也没法享用这份独处，因为泰特斯的声音还在她神经上叮当作响。「洛莉姑姑去哪儿？等一等，我也来。」她听得出他的确切语调，也强烈地听见了自己以沉默作答。她心慌意乱，顾不上看走到了哪里，顺着一条碰巧的小径，一直走进这块她从没来过的田。小径到这里到了头，她也就留在了这里。

The woods rose up before her like barriers. On the third side of the field was a straggling hedge; along it sprawled a thick bank of burdocks, growing with malignant profusion. It was an unpleasant spot. Bitterly she said to herself: “Well, perhaps he’ll leave me alone here,” and was glad of its unpleasantness. Titus could have all the rest: the green meadows, the hilltops, the beechwoods dark and resonant as the inside of a seashell. He could walk in the greenest meadow and have dominion over it like a bull. He could loll his great body over the hilltops, or rout silence out of the woods. They were hers, they were all hers, but she would give them all up to him and keep only this dismal field, and these coarse weeds growing out of an uncleansed soil. Any terms to be rid of him. But even on these terms she could not be rid of him, for all the afternoon he had been present in her thoughts, and his voice rang in her ears as distinctly as ever: “Wait a minute, and I’ll come with you.” She had not waited; but, nevertheless, he had come.

The pictures, of course, didn’t matter: Titus supposed there were some at the Tate. It was Fuseli the man, Fuseli the sign of his times, etc., that Titus was going to write about. It had been the ambition of his life to write a book about Fuseli, and his first visit to Great Mop convinced him that this was the perfect place to write it in. The secret, Titus said, of writing a good book was to be cut off from access to the reading-room of the British Museum. Laura said a little pettishly that if that were all Titus might have stayed in Bloomsbury, and written his book on Good Fridays. Titus demurred. Suppose he ran out of ink? No! Great Mop was the place. “Tomorrow,” he added, “you must take me around and show me all your footpaths.”

He left his pipe and tobacco pouch on the mantelpiece. They lay there like the orb and sceptre of an usurping monarch. Laura dreamed that night that Fuseli had arrived at Mr. Saunter’s poultry-farm, killed the hens, and laid out the field as a golf-course.

She heard a great deal about Fuseli during the next few days, while she was obediently showing Titus all her footpaths. It was hot, so they walked in the woods. The paths were narrow, there was seldom room for two to walk abreast, so Titus generally went in front, projecting his voice into the silence. She disliked these walks; she felt ashamed of his company; she thought the woods saw her with him and drew back scornfully to let them pass by together.

Titus was more tolerable in the village street. Indeed, at first she was rather proud of her nephew’s success. After a week he knew everybody, and knew them far better than she did. He passed from the bar-parlour of the Lamb and Flag to the rustic woodwork of the rector’s lawn. 「我是拉彭特小姐。您，我想，一定是威洛斯小姐。」

那说话的声音，清亮如小钟，又无色无彩，仿佛时间把它漂洗得只剩骄傲，再无一丝人的感情。搭在劳拉手里的那只手，轻得像鸟爪；一只精致的手套像一层薄膜裹着它，隔着这层膜，劳拉觉出那细细的骨节和棱角分明的戒指。

「很久以前，」拉彭特小姐接着说，「我有幸见过您的叔祖，威洛斯海军准将。」

天哪，劳拉一时昏了头地想，难道叔祖德米特里厄斯是个男巫？因为拉彭特小姐实在女巫气得太完全了，她竟肯屈尊见一位寻常绅士，简直不可能。

显然，拉彭特小姐读得出劳拉的心思。

「在考斯。」她补了一句，叫人安心。

劳拉抬眼作答，可拉彭特小姐已经不见了。她站过的地方，站着卡洛尔小姐，装模作样，扭捏作态，仿佛想僭取前一位的贵妇气派。她脸上蒙着一块点花面纱。认出劳拉，她做出又惊又喜的样子，像蝙蝠似的尖叫了一声，随即也侧身挤开，没入黑暗。

接着，一个她不认识的年轻人走上前来，恭恭敬敬搂住她的腰。她发觉自己被期待着跳舞。她听不见任何音乐，可她尽力跳了，合着他呼吸在她脸颊上的节拍。这支舞很短，她想是自己没能让舞伴满意，因为转了几圈之后他便放开了她，把她撂在树篱边。两人之间一句话也没有。劳拉觉得自己该说点什么，却想不出合适的开场白。夸地板好，总归不像话。

一种熟悉的沮丧开始落到她心上。尽管抱着希望，她还是没能玩得尽兴。看来，连做了女巫，她也注定社交上失败，她的第一个安息日也不会比她的第一场舞会开出更热闹的前景。她想起在萨默塞特跳舞的年代，穿堂风阵阵的会所里的猎舞和郡舞。她怀着最好的心意，也从来没能够享受它们。头一个钟头还凑合，往后便是越来越深的倦怠和无聊；同一位舞伴再跳时，得费劲不说同样的话，还得费劲说点同那些话差不多的；眼皮要管住，哈欠要换成笑；还有那份叫人自惭的意识——除了坐车回家，再没什么可盼的了。回家是愉快的，车程尽头那一顿夜宵是个安慰，终于向真正的饥困投降也是解脱。可这些都是捎带的快活；舞会为之设立的那些欢乐，她一无所知。

她看着舞者们走过，纳闷他们感到而她感到不到的迷醉是什么。是什么让他们半夜三更出来，把纸花环挂上树，在水沟里点一排蜡烛，然后朋友、仇家和不相干者一起，在坑洼的草地上磕磕绊绊地转圈？同樱草联盟的那个致命比较又冒上来了。她不快活，于是怪罪这场招待。可错在她自己，她从来不会玩，她没有安息日应守的那份精神。拉彭特小姐倒是玩得痛快；劳拉看见那顶软帽嗖地掠过。不过，拉彭特小姐在考斯想必也痛快过。

这些丧气的念头被红头发的埃米莉打断了。她从舞伴的臂弯里旋出来，一把抓住劳拉，把她拖回舞里。劳拉喜欢同埃米莉跳；她平日所见的那个在村里游荡的、面色苍白贫血的懒散邋遢的年轻女子，跳起舞来有一种扫荡一切疑虑的热狂。她们越转越快，熔成一体，像两颗互相绕转、在同一场焚毁中熊熊燃烧的太阳。一绺红发散开，拂过劳拉的脸。这一触让她从头到脚发麻。她阖上眼，一头扎进遗忘——有埃米莉做舞伴，她能一直跳到靴跟里的火药烧光为止。唉！这个圆满的结局注定不成，因为她们跳到高潮时，马医乔尔先生把埃米莉抢走了。劳拉睁开眼，看那张苍白的脸在人群里沉没，像月亮沉进云里。

埃米莉大受欢迎，这也难怪。她像一支火炬，被人一个个传下去，每一次转手都抖落更多散发。安息日如今热身热得正好，人人都在蹦跳，连劳拉也是。有一阵子，利克太太还维持着女伴的架势。她会突然出现在劳拉肘边，问她玩得好不好，不等她回答，瞥她一眼就溜走。或者她用含糊的手势一指某个躬身回避的舞伴，男的女的；劳拉便默默伸手，被拉进舞里，过一会儿又被放开，或者被别人接走。

安息日的礼节看来只有一条：什么都别做太久。舞伴来了又去，队形和聚散不断流转。有时舞者们成双成对，有时他们围着某个格外矫健的表演者围圈蹦跳，有时全体手拉手绕田狂奔。拉彭特家小姐们主持的一场非常正式的方阵舞跳到一半，大家突然改玩起狐狸与鹅。尽管格登先生佩着缎带，却没有司仪。一股神秘的冲动仿佛支配着全体。他们像一群椋鸟，盘旋，变阵。

这样跳了一两个钟头，劳拉觉得晕头转向。她趁着这份普遍的不拘礼数，从格登先生的臂弯里挣脱出来——不为另找舞伴，只为溜出去，安安静静坐在树篱里。

她纳闷音乐是打哪儿来的。翻山的时候她听得清清楚楚，一进田就丢了。如今她看着别人，又听见了它。他们走近，它就响些；他们退进黑暗，它便随他们淡去，仿佛声音是从舞者自己身上发出的，嗡嗡的一股气息，悬在他们头顶。那是一种古怪的音乐，一派连绵的、高亢的、无形的声音。有点像热卧室里的蚊子，又有点像远处的打谷机。可除此之外，它还有一点人性的质地，一种铜管乐器打着拍子的金属的呼吸；舞者们手拉手转成一个跳跃的圆圈时，音乐便同他们一起跳跃、捶击，那样像旋转木马的蒸汽风琴，有一瞬间劳拉竟以为他们骑着马和龙，颠上颠下——冠毛耸立的龙头像公鸡的龙，鼻孔血红的高头大马。

The same waves of thought circled outwards, waves of startled thought spreading out on all sides, locking the shadows of familiar things, blurring the steadfast pictures of trees and clouds, circling outward one after the other, each wave more listless, more imperceptible than the last, until the pool was still again.

There might be some questions that even the Devil could not answer. She turned her eyes to him with their question.

Satan had risen to his feet. He picked up the flag basket and the shears, and made ready to go.

“Is it time?” asked Laura.

He nodded, and smiled.

She got up in her turn, and began to shake the dust off her skirt. Then she prodded a hole for the bag which had held the apples, and buried it tidily, smoothing the earth over the hole. This took a little time to do, and when she looked round for Satan, to say goodbye, he was out of sight.

Seeing that he was gone she sat down again, for she wanted to think him over. A pleasant conversation, though she had done most of the talking. The tract of flattened grass at her side showed where he had rested, and there was the rampion flower he had held in his hand. Grass that has been lain upon has always a rather popular bank-holidayish look, and even the Devil’s lair was not exempt from this. It was as though the grass were in league with him, faithfully playing-up to his pose of being a quite everyday phenomenon. Not a blade of grass was singed, not a cloverleaf blasted, and the rampion flower was withering quite naturally; yet he who had sat there was Satan, the author of all evil, whose thoughts were a darkness, whose roots went down into the pit. There was no action too mean for him, no instrument too petty; he would go into a milk-jug to work mischief. And presently he would emerge, imperturbable, inscrutable, enormous with the dignity of natural behaviour and untrammelled self-fulfilment.

To be this﻿—a character truly integral, a perpetual flowering of power and cunning from an undivided will﻿—was enough to constitute the charm and majesty of the Devil. No cloak of terrors was necessary to enlarge that stature, and to suppose him capable of speculation or metaphysic would be like offering to crown him with a few casual straws. Very probably he was quite stupid. When she had asked him about death he had got up and gone away, which looked as if he did not know much more about it than she did herself: indeed, being immortal, it was unlikely that he would know as much. Instead, his mind brooded immovably over the landscape and over the natures of men, an unforgetting and unchoosing mind. That, of course﻿—and she jumped up in her excitement and began to wave her arms﻿—was why he was the Devil, the enemy of souls. His memory was too long, too retentive; there was no appeasing its witness, no hoodwinking it with the present; and that was why at one stage of civilization people said he was the embodiment of all evil, and then a little later on that he didn’t exist.

For a moment Laura thought that she had him: and on the next, as though he had tricked himself out of her grasp, her thoughts were scattered by the sudden consciousness of a sort of jerk in the atmosphere. The sun had gone down, sliding abruptly behind the hills. In that case the bus would have gone too, she might as well hope to catch the one as the other. First Satan, then the sun and the bus﻿—*adieu, mes gens!* With affectionate unconcern she seemed to be waving them farewell, pleased to be left to herself, left to enter into this new independence acknowledged by their departure.

The night was at her disposal. She might walk back to Great Mop and arrive very late: or she might sleep out and not trouble to arrive until tomorrow. Whichever she did Mrs. Leak would not mind. That was one of the advantages of dealing with witches; they do not mind if you are a little odd in your ways, frown if you are late for meals, fret if you are out all night, pry and commiserate when at length you return. Lovely to be with people who prefer their thoughts to yours, lovely to live at your own sweet will, lovely to sleep out all night! She had quite decided, now, to do so. It was an adventure, she had never done such a thing before, and yet it seemed most natural. She would not sleep here: Wickendon was too close. But presently, later on, when she felt inclined to, she would wander off in search of a suitable dry ditch or an accommodating loosened haystack; or wading through last year’s leaves and this year’s fern she would penetrate into a wood and burrow herself a bed. Satan going his rounds might come upon her and smile to see her lying so peaceful and secure in his dangerous keeping. But he would not disturb her. Why should he? The pursuit was over, as far as she was concerned. She could sleep where she pleased, a hind couched in the Devil’s coverts, a witch made free of her Master’s immunity; while he, wakeful and stealthy, was already out after new game. So he would not disturb her. A closer darkness upon her slumber, a deeper voice in the murmuring leaves overhead﻿—that would be all she would know of his undesiring and unjudging gaze, his satisfied but profoundly indifferent ownership.

「你似乎对女巫知道得不少，」撒旦说，「可你本来要说你对我是怎么想的。」

她摇了摇头。

「说下去，」他鼓励道，「你把我比作游侠骑士。那很动听。我相信你还把我比作猎人，一种偷猎的猎人，天黑后在林子里潜行。不及游侠骑士那样中我的虚荣，但恐怕更准确。」

「哦，撒旦！您明明知道我全部的心思，干吗还怂恿我说？」

「我怂恿你说话，不是为了让我知道你的心思，是为了让*你*知道。说下去，劳拉。别犯傻。你怎么想我？」

「我不知道，」她老老实实地说，「我看我并不想。我只是发狂想，打比方。您超出了我，我的念头从您身上飞开去，像离心假说一样。经过了这一场，我更要没主意了，因为我太喜欢您了，我觉得您这样和气、这样体谅。可您显然不可能只是一个慈善机构。不，我只好睁着眼瞎，做您的女巫。」

「你不拿男巫当真，我知道。可你也许会发现他们的观点有启发。既然这是一桩心灵上的难处，何不去请教琼斯先生？」

「可怜的琼斯先生！」劳拉笑起来，「他连自己的灵魂都做不了主。」

「嘘！你忘了他已经把灵魂卖给我了？」

「那您干吗又把它抵押给格登先生？连安息日都不许琼斯先生赴。」

「你有时有点迟钝。就没想过，别人也可能同你一样，嫌安息日不够雅致？」

「说起来，您自己也不赴安息日呀。」

「你怎么知道！别想把我装进你的口袋，劳拉。你不是我唯一的猎获，我也不是人间的君主，会在仆人里宠这个疏那个。一切灵魂都归入我的网。我为这个双关语道歉，可它用得贴切。」

她挨了训，倒不觉得怎么难为情。那么，她读到的魔鬼同仆人之间的融洽关系，是真的了。既然尤凡·麦卡泽安都训过他——急起来，她未尝不可以也训一训。她读到的别的事可能也是真的，她想，那些她至今倾向于不信的事。这样一位随和、仆人里没有偏宠的师父，说不定真去赴安息日，说不定拉得下脸在野餐上吃黑血肠而不失尊严。

「安息日上那个无礼的年轻人，」她说，「我知道那不是您。他是谁？」

「他是那种才华横溢的青年作家之一，」魔鬼答道，「我想泰特斯认识他。他把灵魂卖给我，条件是每个礼拜得有一次，做聚会里无可争议的头号人物。」

「他干吗不为了当大作家卖灵魂呢！那样聚会也白得了。」

「他选了抄近路，你瞧。」

她瞧不出来。可她太高傲，不肯再问，何况撒旦这会儿正朝她微笑，仿佛她是一只宠物羔羊。

「琼斯先生——」

「够了！这个你可以自己去问他，等你上魔鬼学课的时候。」

「您想格登先生能容我同琼斯先生关起门来上针线课吗？他会把脸从窗口探进来，说：『那些产妇还要让人等多久？』或者：『我想知道牧师老爷那堆粪打算怎么处置？』或者：『您大概知道，牛倌的闺女随时会生吧。』然后就把他拽到灌木丛里训一顿。我为那位可怜的老先生心都碎了！」

「琼斯先生，」撒旦端庄地说，「来世自有他的报偿。」

劳拉沉默了。她望着莫尔格雷夫愚楼，自觉带着一种怅惘的神情。可她脑子里一片空白。

「一个微妙的问题，你是说？也许我拿它打趣，是我失于轻薄。」

一只蠓虫落在劳拉手腕上。她一巴掌拍下去。

「死了！」撒旦说。

这个词落进她心里，像一颗石子投进池塘。这个词她听过多少回了，如今又听了一遍。同样的一圈圈念头荡漾开去，受惊的念头的水波向四面漫开，锁住熟悉事物的影子，模糊了树木和云那恒定的图画，一圈接一圈向外荡去，一圈比一圈倦怠，一圈比一圈难辨，直到池水复归平静。

也许有些问题，连魔鬼也答不了。她把带着疑问的眼睛转向他。

撒旦已经站起身。他拾起条编筐和大剪，准备走了。

「到时候了吗？」劳拉问。

他点点头，笑了。

她也站起身，掸裙上的土。然后她戳了个洞，把装过苹果的纸袋埋进去，埋得整整齐齐，把土抹平。这费了一点时间，等她回头找撒旦道别，他已经不见了。

见他已经走了，她重新坐下，因为她要把他好好想一遍。一场愉快的谈话，虽然多半是她在说。身旁那片压平的草地显出他坐过的位置，还有他手里拿过的那枝风铃草。被人躺过的草，总有一点大众银行休假日般的俗艳劲儿，连魔鬼的巢穴也不免于此。仿佛草同他串通一气，忠实地配合他扮演一个寻常现象的姿态。一根草叶也没有焦，一瓣车轴草也没有枯，那枝风铃草凋谢得十分自然；可坐在那里的是撒旦，万恶的肇始者，他的念头是一片黑暗，他的根直扎进深渊。再下贱的事他也没有不肯做的，再微贱的工具他也没有不用的；他会钻进一只奶壶去捣乱。而不久他就会再出来，不动声色，高深莫测，因自然的行为和不受羁绊的自我实现之尊严而显得巨大。

是这样——一个真正整全的性格，一股从一个不可分割的意志里不断开放出来的力量与狡黠——这就足以构成魔鬼的魅力与威严了。不需要恐怖的斗篷来放大那副身量，而设想他能思辨或治形而上学，就像要拿几根随手稻草给他加冕。很可能他相当愚鲁。她问到死，他就站起来走掉了，这看上去像是他对死所知并不比她自己多：的确，他既不死，多半所知还要少些。他的心智倒是不可移易地俯临着风景和众人的天性，一颗不忘也不择的心智。那当然——她兴奋得一跃而起，挥起手臂来——这就是为什么他是魔鬼，是众灵的仇敌。他的记忆太长，太留得住；它的见证安抚不了，现在也蒙骗不了它；所以在文明的一个阶段，人们说他是万恶的化身，稍晚一点，又说他根本不存在。

有一瞬间劳拉以为她抓住他了；下一瞬，仿佛他自己从她掌握里施了个遁法脱身而去，她的念头被一阵大气中的猝然一扯驱散了。太阳落了山，倏地滑到山后去了。既然如此，公共汽车也开走了，这一辆同那一辆，她都一样别想赶上。先是撒旦，然后是太阳和汽车——*别了，我的人们*（adieu, mes gens）！她仿佛带着深情满不在乎地朝它们挥手道别，很高兴被留给她自己，很高兴走进这份被它们的离去所承认的新的独立。

夜归她支配。她可以走回大莫普，到得很晚；也可以睡在外面，不急着在明天之前到家。随她怎样，利克太太都不会介意。同女巫打交道的好处之一就在这里；你举止怪一点，她们不介意；你吃饭迟到，她们不皱眉；你整夜不归，她们不着急；等你终于回去了，她们也不窥探、不假慈悲。同宁愿想自己的心事也不想你的心事的人在一起，多么可爱；随心所欲地过活，多么可爱；整夜睡在露天，多么可爱！她这时完全打定主意这样做了。这是一场历险，她从没干过这样的事，可又显得再自然不过。她不会睡在这儿：威肯登太近。可待一会儿，等她愿意了，她会游荡开去，找一条合适的干水沟，或一个松了捆的干草垛；或者蹚过去年的落叶和今年的蕨草，钻进一片树林，给自己刨一个床。撒旦巡园的时候也许会撞见她，看见她这样安宁、这样稳当地卧在他危险的看管里，微笑起来。可他不会打扰她。他干吗要呢？追猎结束了，就她而言。她爱睡哪儿睡哪儿，一头母鹿卧进魔鬼的树丛，一个享有了师父豁免权的女巫；而他呢，清醒而轻悄，已经出去追新的猎物了。所以他不会打扰她。罩在她睡眠上更近的一层黑暗，头顶上喃喃的叶丛里更深的一个声音——这便是她对他那无所欲、无所断的一瞥，对他那份餍足却彻底冷淡的所有权，所能知道的一切了。
