## XII / 十二

===Original===

Of a Guest Called Waywearer

Now hereafter all went the same way, that from time to time they met on either side the Sundering Flood, save that Osberne came not ever in his fair-dyed raiment, but was mostly clad in russet; but on Elfhild’s birthday he was clad in his best. Otherwise nought befell to tell of. Whiles either of the children were ailing, whiles Elfhild was kept at home by her kinswomen, and so they failed each other, but never by their own will. The one who came to the trysting-place and missed the other was sore grieved, and in special Osberne, whose child’s heart swelled nigh to bursting with sorrow mingled with wrath, and at such times the Sundering Flood seemed to him like the coils of a deadly serpent which was strangling the life out of him, and he would wend home in all despair.

So wore the days through spring and summer and early autumn, and at Wethermel all went smoothly, and the goodman there was better pleased than ever with his new man, who, if he ate two men’s victuals, did three men’s work; as for Osberne, he loved Stephen dearly, and Stephen for his part was forever doing something for his disport, and in two ways in special. For first he was, like Elfhild, stuffed with all kinds of tales and histories, and oft when they were out a-shepherding he would tell these to Osberne daylong; and not unseldom when the tale was underway the lad would cry out: “Fair is thy tale, but I have heard it before, only it is different thus and thus.” And in sooth, he had heard it from Elfhild. The other matter was that Stephen was a smith exceeding deft, and learned the craft to Osberne, so that by the end of the year he bade fair to be a good smith himself. Moreover, whiles would Stephen take a scrap of iron and a little deal of silver, as a silver penny or florin, from out of his hoard, and would fashion it into an ouch or chain or arm-ring, so quaintly and finely that it was a joy to look on. And every one of those things would Stephen give to Osberne with a friendly grin, and Osberne took them with a joyful heart because now he had a new thing to give to Elfhild, and each one he shot across the river unto her the soonest that he might. But whiles, when his heart was full, Osberne would say to the smith: “Thou givest me so much, and doest so well by me, that I know not how ever I am to make it good to thee.” And Stephen would say: “Fear not, master, the time will come when thou mayst do such good to me as shall pay for all at once.”

Now befell tidings on a day of the beginning of October; for the wind, which had been high and blustering all day, grew greater and greater by then candles were lighted in the hall, till it was blowing a great gale from the southwest, which seemed like to lift the house-roof. Then befell a knocking on the house-door, and Stephen went thereto and opened it, and came back with a man all dripping and tousled with the storm. He was a tall man, yellow-haired, and goodly both of face and body, but his face much hidden with a beard untrimmed, and never a shoe had he to his foot: yet was he bold and free of mien despite his poor attire. He carried some long thing under his arm wrapped up in cloth which was bound about with twine and sealed every here and there with yellow wax.

有客名唤行路人（Waywearer）

自此以后，诸事一循旧章：两人时时在分离洪流两岸相会，只是奥斯伯恩不再常穿他那身染色鲜丽的衣裳，多半只穿粗褐布衣；惟独埃尔弗希尔德生日那天，他必换上最好的衣裳。除此之外，别无可述之事。时而这两个孩子中有一个身染小恙，时而埃尔弗希尔德被家中女眷留在家中，两人便彼此错过，却从不曾出于自家本心。到得相会之处而扑了个空的那一个，心里好生悲痛，奥斯伯恩尤其是如此：他那颗孩子的心，被掺着怒气的悲哀胀得几乎迸裂；每逢这种时候，分离洪流在他眼里便好似一条毒蛇的盘卷，正把他性命里的活气绞杀出去，他于是万念俱灰，一路回家。

日子便这样穿过春天、夏天，挨到初秋；韦瑟梅尔诸事顺遂，庄主对他家那个新雇的人比以往更加称意：那人虽说吃两人的饭食，做的却是三人的活计。至于奥斯伯恩，他深爱斯蒂芬；斯蒂芬这一边，也总在寻些事来给他消遣，尤其是两桩。第一桩，他同埃尔弗希尔德一样，满肚子装着各式各样的故事和史话；两人出外牧羊的时候，他往往整日价讲给奥斯伯恩听；而故事讲到半途，那少年也不时（并非罕见地）会喊出声来：“你这故事真好，只是我从前听过了，只不过有这样那样的不同。”当真，他是从埃尔弗希尔德那里听来的。另一桩是：斯蒂芬是个极其灵巧的铁匠，他把这门手艺教给了奥斯伯恩，到得年底，这少年已俨然有望自家也成个好铁匠了。再者，斯蒂芬时或从自家的积蓄里取出一小块铁和一小点儿银子——比如一枚银便士或弗罗林——把它打制成一件饰扣、链子或臂环，打制得那般古雅精巧，看着便是一桩乐事。而每一件这样的物事，斯蒂芬都带着友善的咧嘴一笑送给奥斯伯恩；奥斯伯恩满心欢喜地收下，因为如今他有了新东西可以送给埃尔弗希尔德，而且每一件他都尽早射过河去给她。但时或，待他心满意足，会对那铁匠说：“你给我这么多，又这般善待我，我实在不知何年何月才能补报得了你。”斯蒂芬便道：“别怕，小主人，时候一到，你自能给我做成一件大大的好事，把这一切一并报偿了。”

却说十月初头的一天，出了新的动静：那风整日刮得呼呼作响，到厅堂里点起蜡烛的时分，越刮越大，终于成了西南方向来的一场狂风暴雨，仿佛要把屋顶掀上天去。接着屋门上响起敲声，斯蒂芬走去开门，带回一个教风雨浇得浑身透湿、吹得蓬乱的男子。那是个高个子，一头黄发，脸庞身段都生得堂堂，只是面容教一部未经修剪的须髯遮去了大半，脚上更是连一只鞋也没有：然而尽管衣衫寒碜，他神情气度却昂然洒脱。他腋下夹着一件长长的物事，用布包裹，麻绳捆扎，各处封着黄蜡。

The goodman started up when he came in, and made as if he would have the newcomer put out, and he muttered: “We keep no house for the harbouring of runagates.” Yet he looked at Osberne withal, for he was now grown so masterful that nought was done in the house without him; and the lad stood up straightway and came to the newcomer and bade him welcome from out the storm. Then he took him by the hand and led him up to the hearth, and spake to his grandam: “Goodwife, this our guest has been in rough weather without, and ere he sits down to meat with us, it were well to take him into the inner chamber and wash his feet, and find him dry raiment.” The goodwife looked kindly on the guest and bade him come with her, and he went; but ere his back was turned, Osberne looked on him and caught a glance of his eye, and therewith he was sure that despite his rags and wretchedness this was his friend Steelhead. In a while he came back into the hall, clad and shod as well as might be done in a hurry, and Osberne led him into his own seat at the board, and gave him to drink; and Stephen withal served him with all care, so that he was in an hospitable house, save that the goodman cast somewhat grudging glances on him, but whereas he might not gainsay all the rest of his household, there was little scathe therein.

But when the guest sat down, he took that long bundle and gave it into Osberne’s hands, and said: “Thou art so friendly to a gangrel man, that I make bold to ask this grace of thee also, to wit that thou wilt heed this bundle, and let none other touch it, and give it back to me tomorrow morning ere I depart.” Osberne yeasaid to that, and took the bundle and laid it at his bedhead. And therewith the meat was brought in, and the meal was merry; for now the guest seemed so noble-looking a man and so cheerful of countenance and so debonair, that none save the goodman thought any longer of his rags wherewith he had come into the hall out of the storm. But even the goodman was better with him presently, when he saw that though he ate and drank like a tall man, he needed no such abundance for the filling of his maw as did Stephen.

Ere they began drinking the guest said: “I may as well tell you folks my name, since ye are so good to me, and have not asked for it, and ye must know that I am called Waywearer, and that I wish increase of good unto this house.”

Then the cup went round and they drank late into the night, and when they had drunk the voidee cup, Osberne led the newcomer to the guest-chamber, and kissed him with good night, but made no show of knowing who he was.

那人进得屋来，庄主霍地立起，做出一副要把来客撵出去的架势，嘴里咕哝道：“我们这庄户可不设收容亡命流浪之徒的客房。”但他终究朝奥斯伯恩望了一眼，因为如今这少年在家中日渐一言九鼎，屋里没有他，什么事也办不成；少年当即起身，走到来客跟前，请他自风雨中进屋，向他道了欢迎。随后他执起来客的手，引他到炉边，对祖母说道：“好祖母，咱们这位客人方才在外面遭了恶天气，先别忙让他同我们坐下用饭，最好先领他进里屋，替他洗洗脚，寻一身干爽衣裳。”祖母和颜悦色地看了看客人，请他随她进去，客人便去了；但在他转身之前，奥斯伯恩望住他，恰好接住他瞥来的一道目光，就此心中雪亮：这人虽然衣衫褴褛、狼狈不堪，却是他的朋友钢首。过了一会儿，客人回到厅上，身上穿的、脚上蹬的，都是匆忙中张罗得到的最好模样；奥斯伯恩把他领到自家席上的座位，奉他酒饮；斯蒂芬也在一旁悉心伺候，因此这一家待他可谓宾至如归——只除了庄主时不时朝他投来几分吝惜的眼色；但既然拗不过阖家上下的心意，倒也没多少妨害。

及至客人落座，他取过那件长长的包裹，放到奥斯伯恩手中，说道：“你对一个流浪汉子这般友好，我便斗胆再求你一桩恩典：请你把这包裹看管好，莫让旁人碰它，明日清晨我动身之前，再把它还我。”奥斯伯恩应承下来，接过包裹，安放在自己床头。随即肉食端了上来，席间一派欢洽；原来这时那客人看去已是何等一位高贵的人物，面容开朗，风度翩翩，除了庄主之外，再没有人记得他是披着一身破烂、顶着风雨进得厅来的。就连庄主，不一刻也对他面色和缓了——只因见他虽然像条大汉似的大吃大喝，要把肚皮填满，却远不像斯蒂芬那样需要海量。

开怀畅饮之前，客人说道：“列位既然待我这般好，又始终不曾动问，我不妨索性把自己的名字相告：你们须知，我名唤行路人，并愿这一家福泽日增。”

于是传杯换盏，直喝到夜深；喝过散席的末一杯酒，奥斯伯恩便引这位来客到客房安歇，亲吻道了晚安，却丝毫不露认得他来历的口风。
