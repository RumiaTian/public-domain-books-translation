## II Of Wethermel and the Child Osberne / 二 韦瑟梅尔与孩童奥斯伯恩

===Original===

Draw we nigher now to the heart of our tale, and tell how on the east side of the Sundering Flood was erewhile a stead hight Wethermel: a stead more lonely than most even in that Dale, the last house but one, and that was but a cot, toward the mountains at the head of the Dale. It was not ill set down, for its houses stood beneath a low spreading knoll, the broader side whereof was turned to the southwest, and where by consequence was good increase of corn year by year. The said knoll of Wethermel was amidst of the plain of the Dale a mile from the waterside, and all round about it the pasture was good for kine and horses and sheep all to the water’s lip on the west and halfway up the bent on the east; while towards the crown of the bent was a wood of bushes good for firewood and charcoal, and even beyond the crown of the bent was good sheep-land a long way.

Nevertheless, though its land was fruitful as for that country, yet had Wethermel no great name for luck, and folk who had the choice would liever dwell otherwhere, so that it was hard for the goodman to get men to work there for hire. Many folk deemed that this ill-luck came because the knoll had been of old time a dwelling of the Dwarfs or the Land-wights, and that they grudged it that the children of Adam had supplanted them, and that corn grew on the very roof of their ancient house. But however that might be, there was little thriving there for the most part: and at least it was noted by some, that if there were any good hap, it ever missed one generation, and went not from father to son, but from grandsire to grandson: and even so it was now at the beginning of this tale.

For he who had been master of Wethermel had died a young man, and his wife followed him in a month or two, and there was left in the house but the father and mother of these twain, hale and stout folk, he of fifty winters, she of forty-five; an old woman of seventy, a kinswoman of the house who had fostered the late goodman; and a little lad who had to name Osberne, now twelve winters old, a child strong and bold, tall, bright and beauteous. These four were all the folk of Wethermel, save now and then a hired man who was hard pressed for livelihood would be got to abide there some six months or so. It must be told further that there was no house within ten miles either up or down the water on that side, save the little cot abovesaid nigher to the mountains, and that was four miles upstream; it hight Burcot, and was somewhat kenspeckle. Withal as to those Cloven Motes, as they were called, which were between the folk on either side, they were holden at a stead seven miles below Wethermel. So that in all wise was it a lonely and scantly-manned abode: and because of this every man on the stead must work somewhat hard and long day by day, and even Osberne the little lad must do his share; and up to this time we tell of, his work was chiefly about the houses, or else it was on the knoll, or round about it, scaring fowl from the corn; weeding the acre-ground, or tending the old horses that fed near the garth; or goose-herding at whiles. Forsooth, the two elders, who loved and treasured the little carle exceedingly, were loth to trust him far out of sight because of his bold heart and wilful spirit; and there were perils in the Dale, and in special at that rough and wild end thereof, though they came not from weaponed reivers for the more part, though now and again some desperate outcast from the thicker peopled lands had strayed into it; and there was talk from time to time of outlaws who lay out over the mountain-necks, and might not always do to lack a sheep or a neat or a horse. Other perils more of everyday there were for a young child, as the deep and hurrying stream of the Sundering Flood, and the wolves which haunted the bent and the foothills of the mountains; and ever moreover there was the peril from creatures seldom seen, Dwarfs and Land-wights to wit, who, as all tales told, might be well pleased to have away into their realm so fair a child of the sons of Adam as was this Osberne.

如今让我们走近故事的核心，说一说分离洪流（the Sundering Flood）东岸昔日有座庄园，唤作韦瑟梅尔（Wethermel）：即便在那道谷地里，它也比大多数人家更为冷清——朝谷首群山的方向数去，它是倒数第二户，而最末那一户，也不过是一间茅舍。这庄子坐落得并不算差：房舍立在一片低矮开展的丘冈之下，丘冈较宽的一面朝向西南，因此年年五谷丰登。韦瑟梅尔的这座丘冈正当谷地平原之中，离水边一里；四野牧场丰美，宜于牛马羊群：西面一直吃到水唇，东面吃到半坡；坡顶一带则生着一片灌木林，宜作柴薪、宜烧木炭；便是过了坡顶再往前，还有绵延很远的良牧羊地。

话虽如此，那片土地就当地而论固然丰饶，韦瑟梅尔在运气上的名声却一向不佳，但凡有别处可拣的人，都宁愿住到别处去，因此庄主很难雇到人来此做工。许多人认为，这厄运的根由，在于那座丘冈古时曾是矮人（Dwarfs）或地灵（Land-wights）的居所，他们心怀怨怼，怪亚当的子孙占了他们的地盘，怪五谷偏偏长在他们古宅的屋顶上。但无论真相如何，这庄子上多半是兴旺不起来的：至少有人留意到，那里纵然偶有好运，也总要隔过一代人——福气不从父亲传给儿子，倒从祖父传到孙子。而眼下这故事开篇之时，恰是这般光景。

只因先前做韦瑟梅尔主人的死得年轻，不出一两个月，他的妻子也随他去了；宅中便只剩下这两口的父亲母亲——都是康健壮实的老人家，他五十个冬天，她四十五个；一位七十岁的老妇人，是这家的亲戚，昔日抚育过已故的庄主；还有一个小男孩，名唤奥斯伯恩（Osberne），今年十二个冬天，生得强壮而胆大，高挑、伶俐又俊美。韦瑟梅尔上下便只这四口人，除非偶尔有个为生计所迫的雇工，肯来住上六个月上下。还须补一句：那一岸沿河上下十里之内，再无别家人户，只有上文所说、更近群山的那间小茅舍，在上游四里处；它唤作伯科特（Burcot），模样颇惹眼。至于两岸百姓之间那些名为“裂丘大会”（the Cloven Motes）的集会，则在韦瑟梅尔下游七里的一处地方举行。可见无论怎么看，这都是个冷清而人手单薄的居处：因此庄上人人都得日复一日地苦干久做，连小奥斯伯恩也须尽他一份力；直到我们讲述的这时为止，他的活计多半在房舍左右，不然便是在丘冈上或丘冈四周，从谷地里惊走飞鸟；在耕地上锄草，照看在院垣近旁吃草的老马；偶尔也去放鹅。说实在的，两位老人家把这小家伙疼惜得了不得，却因他胆大心野、任性使气，不肯放心让他离开眼界太远；何况谷地之中自有种种凶险，尤其是在那粗野荒凉的谷尾一端，虽说多半并非来自持械的劫掠者，只是间或有从人烟稠密之地流窜来的亡命之徒误入其间；又不时有人谈起，说有法外之徒露宿在山颈那边的野地里，未必总是肯少掉一只羊、一头牛或一匹马。对年幼的孩子，还有些更为日常的凶险：比如分离洪流那深峻湍急的水流，比如出没于山坡与山麓丘陵间的狼；而更有一桩无处不在的祸患，来自那些难得一见的生灵——矮人与地灵便是——须知种种传闻都说，他们巴不得把奥斯伯恩这样亚当子孙中如此俊美的孩子，掳到他们的国度里去。

Forsooth for the most part the lad kept within bounds, for love’s sake rather than fear, though he wotted well that beating abode bound-breaking; but ye may well wot that this quietness might not always be. And one while amongst others he was missing for long, and when his grandsire sought him he found him at last halfway between grass and water above the fierce swirling stream of the river; for he had clomb down the sheer rock of the bank, which all along the water is fashioned into staves, as it were organ-pipes, but here and there broken by I wot not what mighty power. There then was my lad in an inglenook of the rock, and not able either to go down or come up, till the goodman let a rope down to him and hauled him on to the grass.

Belike he was a little cowed by the peril, and the beating he got for putting his folk in such fear; but though he was somewhat moved by his grandam’s tears and lamentations over him, and no less by the old carline’s bewailing for his days that he would so surely shorten, yet this was not by a many the last time he strayed from the stead away into peril. On a time he was missing again nightlong, but in the morning came into the house blithe and merry, but exceeding hungry, and when the goodman asked him where he had been and bade him whipping-cheer, he said that he cared little if beaten were he, so merry a time he had had; for he had gone a long way up the Dale, and about twilight (this was in mid-May) had fallen in with a merry lad somewhat bigger than himself, who had shown him many merry plays, and at last had brought him to his house, “which is not builded of stone and turf, like to ours,” saith he, “but is in a hole in the rock; and there we wore away the night, and there was no one there but we two, and again he showed me more strange plays, which were wondrous; but some did frighten me.”

Then his grandsire asked him what like those plays were. Said Osberne: “He took a stone and stroked it, and mumbled, and it turned into a mouse, and played with us nought afraid a while; but presently it grew much bigger, till it was bigger than a hare; and great game meseemed that was, till on a sudden it stood on its hind-legs, and lo it was become a little child, and O, but so much littler than I; and then it ran away from us into the dark, squealing the while like a mouse behind the panel, only louder. Well, thereafter, my playmate took a big knife, and said: ‘Now, drudgling, I shall show thee a good game indeed.’ And so he did, for he set the edge of the said knife against his neck, and off came his head; but there came no blood, nor did he tumble down, but took up his head and stuck it on again, and then he stood crowing like our big red cock. Then he said: ‘Poultry, cockerel, now I will do the like by thee.’ And he came to me with the knife; but I was afraid, and gat hold of his hand and had the knife from him; and then I wrestled with him and gave him a fall; but I must needs let him get up again presently, whereas he grew stronger under my hand; then he thrust me from him and laughed exceeding much, and said: ‘Here is a champion come into my house forsooth! Well, I will leave thine head on thy shoulders, for belike I might not be able to stick it on again, which were a pity of thee, for a champion shalt thou verily be in the days to come.’ After this all his play with me was to sit down and bid me hearken; and then he took out a little pipe, and put it to his mouth, and made music out of it, which was both sweet and merry. And then he left that, and fell to telling me tales about the woods where big trees grow, and how his kindred had used to dwell therein, and fashioned most fair things in smith’s work of gold and silver and iron; and all this liked me well; and he said: ‘I tell thee that one day thou shalt have a sword of my father’s father’s fashioning, and that will be an old one, for they both were long-lived.’ And as he spake I deemed that he was not like a child any more, but a little, little old man, white-haired and wrinkle-faced, but without a beard, and his hair shone like glass. And then﻿—I went to sleep, and when I woke up again it was morning, and I looked around and there was no one with me. So I arose and came home to you, and I am safe and sound if thou beat me not, kinsman.”

说实在的，这孩子大体上倒还守着规矩，是出于爱而不是出于怕，虽然他明知犯了界限便有鞭子候着；但你们可想而知，这般安静总不能长久。其中有一回，他又久久不见踪影，祖父出去寻他，末了发现他半悬在草与水之间，脚下便是河中凶猛的漩流；原来他顺着岸边笔直的峭壁爬了下去——那峭壁沿岸被造就成一根根石柱，宛如管风琴的琴管，只是处处被不知何种伟力所折断。我那小家伙就待在一处炉龛般的石凹里，上不去也下不来，直到庄主给他缒下一条绳子，把他拖回草地上。

想来那番凶险，加上害得全家担惊受怕而吃的一顿打，多少叫他有些胆寒；虽说祖母的眼泪与哀哭令他颇为触动，老婆子哭叹他这般胡闹必定折损寿数，也令他同样心下不安；然而要说这是他末一回私自出庄、闯进险地，那可差得远呢。又有一回，他再度彻夜不见踪影，清晨却欢天喜地进了家门，只是饿得厉害；庄主问他去了哪里，又吩咐他预备领鞭子，他却说，玩得这样痛快，挨不挨打都不大放在心上；原来他沿谷地往上走了老远，约莫黄昏时分（其时正当五月中旬）遇见一个比他略大些的快活少年，教他玩了种种有趣的把戏，末了还带他回自己家去——“那房子不像咱们的是石头和草皮垒的，”他说，“是在岩石的一个洞里；我们就在那里把一夜消磨过去，除我们俩再没别人，他又给我看了些更古怪的把戏，真真稀奇得很；只是有几样叫我心里发慌。”

于是祖父问他，那些把戏都是什么样子。奥斯伯恩说：“他拿起一块石头，一边摩挲，一边念念有词，那石头就变成了一只老鼠，同我们玩了一会儿，一点也不怕人；可是不多时它渐渐长大，直到比一只野兔还大；我看这玩意儿有趣极了，直到它猛地用后腿站立起来——瞧啊，它变成了一个小小孩儿，哎呀，比我还要小上好些呢；随后它吱吱叫着从我们身边跑进黑地里去了，那叫声像壁板后头的老鼠，只是响得多。后来呢，我的玩伴取来一把大刀，说：‘喂，小苦力，我这可要给你看一样真格的好把戏了。’他果真做了：他把那刀刃抵在自己颈上，脑袋便应声落下；却不见一点血，他也不曾栽倒，只把脑袋拾起来重新安上，随即站在那儿，学咱们那只大红公鸡喔喔地啼。接着他说：‘小鸡崽儿，小嫩公鸡，如今我可要照样儿对你来一回了。’他便拿着刀朝我走来；我害怕了，抓住他的手，把刀夺了下来；接着我同他角力，把他摔了个跟头；可不一会儿我就只得由他爬起来，因为在我手里他劲儿越来越壮；随后他把我一推，放声大笑，说：‘好啊，我家当真来了位豪杰！也罢，你的脑袋就给你留在肩膀上吧，只怕我未必安得回去，那你可就可惜了——因为你将来必定是位豪杰。’打这以后，他同我玩的只剩一样：坐下叫我侧耳听；他取出一支小笛子，放在嘴边吹起曲调来，又甜美又欢快。后来他连这个也撂下了，改给我讲古：讲那些长着大树的树林，讲他的族人从前如何住在林中，用金、银、铁打出顶精美的物件；这些我都爱听；他还说：‘我告诉你，有朝一日，你会得到一把我父亲的父亲亲手锻的剑——那必是把老剑了，因为他们两位都是长寿之人。’他说话的当儿，我觉得他不再像个孩子，倒是个极小极小的老头儿：白发苍苍，满脸皱纹，却没有胡须，头发亮得像玻璃。再后来——我就睡着了；等我再醒来，已是清晨，四下一望，身边一个人也没有。我便起身回家来了；只要你不打我，亲爷爷，我好端端的在这儿呢。”

Now ye may judge if his fore-elders were not scared by the lad’s tale, for they knew that he had fallen in with one of the Dwarf-kin, and his grandam caught him up and hugged him and kissed him well favouredly; and the carline, whose name was Bridget, followed on the like road; and then she said: “See you, kinsmen, if it be not my doing that the blessed bairn has come back to us. Tell us, sweetheart, what thou hast round thy neck under thy shirt.” Osberne laughed. Said he: “Thou didst hang on me a morsel of parchment with signs drawn thereon, and it is done in a silk bag. Fear not, foster-mother, but that I will wear it yet, since thou makest such to-do over it.”

“Ah! the kind lad thou art, my dear,” said the carline. “I will tell you, kinsmen, that I had that said parchment from our priest, and it is strong neckguard against all evil things, for on it is scored the Holy Rood, and thereon are the names of the three Holy Kings, and other writing withal which I may not read, for it is clerks’ Latin.” And again the two women made much of the little lad, while the goodman stood by grumbling and grunting; but this time did Osberne escape his beating, though he was promised a drubbing which should give him much to think on if he went that way again; and the women prayed and besought him to be obedient to the goodman herein.

But one thing he had not told his kinsfolk, to wit, that the Dwarf had given him for a gift that same knife wherewith he had played the game of heads-off, and a fair sheath went with it, and he had done him to wit that most like luck would go with it. Wherefore little Osberne had the said knife hidden under his raiment, along with the parchment whereon was scored the Holy Rood and the good words of wisdom written.

如今你们可以想见，这孩子的叙述叫他祖辈们多么惊惶，因为他们明白，他遇上的是矮人一族中的一位。祖母一把将他搂起，好好地又抱又亲；那位名叫布里奇特（Bridget）的老婆子也照样搂了他亲了他，然后她说：“你们瞧，亲人们，这蒙福的娃娃能回到我们身边，若不是我的功劳，才怪呢。心肝儿，告诉我们，你衬衫底下、脖子上挂的是什么？”奥斯伯恩笑了。他说：“你在我脖子上挂了一小片羊皮纸，上面画着符记，还拿一只丝袋装着。奶娘放心，我一定还戴着它，既然你为它这样大惊小怪。”

“哎呀！真是个好心的孩子，我的宝贝，”老婆子说。“我告诉你们，亲人们：那片羊皮纸是我从本堂神父那里求来的，是对付一切邪祟的强效护颈符——因为上面刻着圣十字架（the Holy Rood），还写着三位圣王的名字，此外还有些文字，我可念不来，那是教士们的拉丁文。”两个妇人又把小家伙疼宠了一番，庄主却立在一旁，咕哝个不了，哼个不停；不过这一回奥斯伯恩逃过了鞭子，虽然大家许下话来：他若再往那边跑，定要结结实实揍他一顿，好教他多多思量；两个妇人还再三恳求他，在这件事上务要听庄主的话。

但有一件事他没有告诉家人：那矮人把玩“砍脑袋”把戏所用的那把刀，当作礼物送给了他，还配着一只漂亮的刀鞘，并且让他知道，好运多半会随着这刀走。因此小奥斯伯恩把那把刀藏在贴身衣裳底下，同那片刻着圣十字架、写满智慧善言的羊皮纸收在一处。
