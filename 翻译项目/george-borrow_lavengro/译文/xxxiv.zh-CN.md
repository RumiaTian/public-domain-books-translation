===Original===

“What can’t be cured must be endured,” and “it is hard to kick against the pricks.”
At the period to which I have brought my history, I bethought me of the proverbs with which I have headed this chapter, and determined to act up to their spirit. I determined not to fly in the face of the publisher, and to bear﻿—what I could not cure﻿—his arrogance and vanity. At present, at the conclusion of nearly a quarter of a century, I am glad that I came to that determination, which I did my best to carry into effect.
Two or three days after our last interview, the publisher made his appearance in my apartment; he bore two tattered volumes under his arm, which he placed on the table. “I have brought you two volumes of lives, sir,” said he, “which I yesterday found in my garret; you will find them of service for your compilation. As I always wish to behave liberally and encourage talent, especially youthful talent, I shall make no charge for them, though I should be justified in so doing, as you are aware that, by our agreement, you are to provide any books and materials which may be necessary. Have you been in quest of any?”
“No,” said I, “not yet.”
“Then, sir, I would advise you to lose no time in doing so; you must visit all the bookstalls, sir, especially those in the bystreets and blind alleys. It is in such places that you will find the description of literature you are in want of. You must be up and doing, sir; it will not do for an author, especially a young author, to be idle in this town. Tonight you will receive my book of philosophy, and likewise books for the *Review*. And, by the by, sir, it will be as well for you to review my book of philosophy for the *Review*, the other Reviews not having noticed it. Sir, before translating it, I wish you to review my book of philosophy for the *Review*.”
“I shall be happy to do my best, sir.”
“Very good, sir; I should be unreasonable to expect anything beyond a person’s best. And now, sir, if you please, I will conduct you to the future editor of the *Review*. As you are to cooperate, sir, I deem it right to make you acquainted.”
The intended editor was a little old man, who sat in a kind of wooden pavilion in a small garden behind a house in one of the purlieus of the city, composing tunes upon a piano.

「治不好的就得捱着」；还有「踢赶牛棍是自讨苦吃」。
在我把这段经历讲到的那个时期，我想起了本章开篇所引的两句谚语，决意照它们的精神行事。我决意不同出版商对着干，要忍受——既然治不好——他的傲慢与虚荣。如今，将近四分之一个世纪已近尾声，我很庆幸当年下了那个决心，并且尽了最大努力把它付诸实行。
我们上次见面之后过了两三天，出版商出现在我的寓所；他腋下夹着两卷破旧的书，把它们搁在桌上。「我给你带来两卷生平录，先生，」他说，「是昨天在阁楼上翻出来的；对你的编纂会有用处。我一向行事大方，乐于奖掖人才，尤其是青年人才，这两卷便分文不取——虽然按理我尽可以收钱，你知道，咱们讲定的条件里，所需的书籍材料一概归你置办。你可曾去寻过什么？」
「还没有，」我说。
「那么，先生，我劝你即刻着手；你得起遍所有的书摊，先生，尤其是僻街陋巷里的那些。你要的那一类书，正是在那些地方才寻得着。你必须振作勤快，先生；作家、尤其是年轻作家，在这城里游手好闲可不成。今晚你会收到我的哲学著作，还有《评论》要用的书。还有，顺便说一句，先生，你不妨在《评论》上评一评我的哲学著作——别的各家评论都还没理会它呢。先生，趁翻译之前，我要你先在《评论》上评一评我的哲学著作。」
「我乐意尽力而为，先生。」
「很好，先生；指望一个人超出其力所能及，那是不近情理。那么，先生，你若方便，我就领你去见见《评论》未来的主编。既然你们要共事，先生，我认为理当让你们相识。」
这位未来的主编是个小老头，坐在城郊一隅某宅后小花园里一座木造凉亭中，正用一架钢琴作曲。

The walls of the pavilion were covered with fiddles of various sizes and appearances, and a considerable portion of the floor occupied by a pile of books all of one size. The publisher introduced him to me as a gentleman scarcely less eminent in literature than in music, and me to him as an aspirant critic﻿—a young gentleman scarcely less eminent in philosophy than in philology. The conversation consisted entirely of compliments till just before we separated, when the future editor inquired of me whether I had ever read Quintilian; and, on my replying in the negative, expressed his surprise that any gentleman should aspire to become a critic who had never read Quintilian, with the comfortable information, however, that he could supply me with a Quintilian at half-price, that is, a translation made by himself some years previously, of which he had, pointing to the heap on the floor, still a few copies remaining unsold. For some reason or other, perhaps a poor one, I did not purchase the editor’s translation of Quintilian.140
“Sir,” said the publisher, as we were returning from our visit to the editor, “you did right in not purchasing a drug. I am not prepared, sir, to say that Quintilian is a drug, never having seen him; but I am prepared to say that man’s translation is a drug, judging from the heap of rubbish on the floor; besides, sir, you will want any loose money you may have to purchase the description of literature which is required for your compilation.”
The publisher presently paused before the entrance of a very forlorn-looking street. “Sir,” said he, after looking down it with attention, “I should not wonder if in that street you find works connected with the description of literature which is required for your compilation. It is in streets of this description, sir, and blind alleys, where such works are to be found. You had better search that street, sir, whilst I continue my way.”
I searched the street to which the publisher had pointed, and, in the course of the three succeeding days, many others of a similar kind. I did not find the description of literature alluded to by the publisher to be a drug, but, on the contrary, both scarce and dear. I had expended much more than my loose money long before I could procure materials even for the first volume of my compilation.

凉亭的四壁挂满了大大小小、形形色色的提琴，地板上好大一块地方堆着一摞书，开本全然一律。出版商把他介绍给我，说这是一位在文学上的声望不下于音乐的先生；又把我介绍给他，说是一位崭露头角的批评家——一位在哲学上的造诣不下于语言学的年轻先生。谈话自始至终全是客套，直到临别之前，未来的主编才问我可曾读过昆体良；听我答说没有，他表示惊异，说一位先生竟想当批评家而未读过昆体良；不过随即又奉上一句宽慰的话：他能半价供我一册昆体良——也就是他自己几年前译的一个本子，说着指了指地板上那摞书——尚有几册未曾售罄。不知出于什么缘故——许是个不成话的缘故——我没有买那位主编的昆体良译本。140
「先生，」我们从主编那里回来的路上出版商说，「你不买那件滞销货，做得对。昆体良是不是滞销货，我不便妄断，先生，因为我从不曾见过他；可那人的译本是滞销货，这一点我敢断言——只消看看地板上那堆破烂就知道了；况且，先生，你手边的闲钱还得留着购置编纂所需的那一类书呢。」
出版商随即在一条望之颇为寒碜的街口站住了。「先生，」他仔细朝街里望过之后说，「在那条街上寻得着你编纂所需的那一类书，我也不奇怪。正是这一类的街，先生，还有死胡同，才找得着那些书。你最好把那条街搜一搜，先生，我呢，自顾往前去。」
我把出版商指的那条街搜了一遍，随后三天里又搜了许多条相仿的街。出版商提到的那类书，我发现并非滞销货，反倒又稀罕又金贵。还没等我置齐头一卷所需的材料，身上的闲钱早已花得远不止见了底。
