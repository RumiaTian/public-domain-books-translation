### LXXXIII / 八十三
===Original===

It has always struck me that there is something highly poetical about a forge. I am not singular in this opinion: various individuals have assured me that they can never pass by one, even in the midst of a crowded town, without experiencing sensations which they can scarcely define, but which are highly pleasurable. I have a decided penchant for forges, especially rural ones, placed in some quaint, quiet spot﻿—a dingle, for example, which is a poetical place, or at a meeting of four roads, which is still more so; for how many a superstition﻿—and superstition is the soul of poetry﻿—is connected with these crossroads! I love to light upon such a one, especially after nightfall, as everything about a forge tells to most advantage at night; the hammer sounds more solemnly in the stillness; the glowing particles scattered by the strokes sparkle with more effect in the darkness, whilst the sooty visage of the *sastramescro*, half in shadow, and half-illumed by the red and partial blaze of the forge, looks more mysterious and strange. On such occasions I draw in my horse’s rein, and, seated in the saddle, endeavour to associate with the picture before me﻿—in itself a picture of romance﻿—whatever of the wild and wonderful I have read of in books, or have seen with my own eyes in connection with forges.
I believe the life of any blacksmith, especially a rural one, would afford materials for a highly poetical history. I do not speak unadvisedly, having the honour to be free of the forge, and therefore fully competent to give an opinion as to what might be made out of the forge by some dextrous hand. Certainly, the strangest and most entertaining life ever written is that of a blacksmith of the olden north, a certain Volundr,201 or Velint, who lived in woods and thickets, made keen swords, so keen, indeed, that if placed in a running stream, they would fairly divide an object, however slight, which was borne against them by the water, and who eventually married a king’s daughter, by whom he had a son, who was as bold a knight as his father was a cunning blacksmith.

===Chinese===

锻炉，我总觉着它身上有一种顶浓的诗意。持这见解的不止我一人：好些人对我说过，路过锻炉——哪怕是在人烟稠密的镇子当中——他们总要生出一种难以名状、却极其愉快的感觉。我对锻炉有一种斩不断的偏爱，尤其爱乡野的锻炉，坐落在什么古怪清静的角落——譬如一道幽谷，那本是诗意的地方；再不就落在十字路口，那就更有诗意了；因为同这些交叉路口连着的迷信何其多——而迷信正是诗的灵魂！我爱撞见这样一座，尤其天黑以后，因为锻炉的一切到了夜里最见长处：锤声在寂静里响得更庄严；锤下迸散的火星在黑暗里闪得更见效验，而 *sastramescro* 那张烟黑的脸，一半浸在阴影里，一半映着炉火红红的、幽幽的光，显得愈发神秘古怪。逢到这种时候，我便收住缰绳，坐在鞍上，把我书中读到的、亲眼见过的、同锻炉相连的一切蛮荒与奇观，尽力同眼前这幅图画——它本身就是一幅传奇——连到一起。
我相信，任何一个铁匠的一生，尤其是乡野铁匠，都够写一部顶有诗意的历史的材料。这话我不是随口说的：我有幸厕身锻炉行内，因此完全有资格断言，一双巧手能从锻炉里做出什么文章来。当然，有史以来写下的最离奇、最有趣的一部生平，是古时北方一位铁匠的——就是那位沃兰德201，或称韦林特：他住在森林树丛里，打得出锋快的剑——锋快到若把它横在激流里，随水冲来碰到它刃上的东西，再轻它也给你齐齐分成两半；他末了娶了一位国王的女儿，生了一个儿子——父亲是多巧的铁匠，儿子就是多胆的骑士。

I never see a forge at night, when seated on the back of my horse at the bottom of a dark lane, but I somehow or other associate it with the exploits of this extraordinary fellow, with many other extraordinary things, amongst which, as I have hinted before, are particular passages of my own life, one or two of which I shall perhaps relate to the reader.
I never associate Vulcan and his Cyclops with the idea of a forge. These gentry would be the very last people in the world to flit across my mind whilst gazing at the forge from the bottom of the dark lane. The truth is, they are highly unpoetical fellows, as well they may be, connected as they are with Grecian mythology. At the very mention of their names the forge burns dull and dim, as if snowballs had been suddenly flung into it; the only remedy is to ply the bellows, an operation which I now hasten to perform.
I am in the dingle making a horseshoe. Having no other horses on whose hoofs I could exercise my art, I made my first essay on those of my own horse, if that could be called horse which horse was none, being only a pony. Perhaps if I had sought all England, I should scarcely have found an animal more in need of the kind offices of the smith. On three of his feet there were no shoes at all, and on the fourth only a remnant of one, on which account his hoofs were sadly broken and lacerated by his late journeys over the hard and flinty roads. “You belonged to a tinker before,” said I, addressing the animal, “but now you belong to a smith. It is said that the household of the shoemaker invariably go worse shod than that of any other craft. That may be the case of those who make shoes of leather, but it shan’t be said of the household of him who makes shoes of iron; at any rate, it shan’t be said of mine. I tell you what, my *gry*, whilst you continue with me, you shall both be better shod, and better fed, than you were with your last master.”
I am in the dingle making a *petul*; and I must here observe, that whilst I am making a horseshoe, the reader need not be surprised if I speak occasionally in the language of the lord of the horseshoe﻿—Mr. Petulengro. I have for some time past been plying the *peshota*, or bellows, endeavouring to raise up the *yag*, or fire, in my primitive forge.

每当夜里、坐在马背上、在黑巷尽头望见一座锻炉，我总不知怎么就把它同这位非凡汉子的功业联想到一处，还联想到许多别的奇事；其中，如我从前暗示过的，有我自己生平的几段，兴许有一两段我要讲给读者听。
我却从不把伏尔甘和他的独眼巨人们同锻炉联想到一起。从黑巷尽头凝望锻炉时，这班人物是世上最不会掠过我脑海的。实情是，他们是顶顶没有诗意的一伙——同希腊神话沾着亲，也难怪。一提他们的名字，炉火便烧得又钝又暗，仿佛忽然被人丢进了几只雪球；唯一的救法是拉动风箱——这功夫我这就赶着做去。
我正在谷里打一只马蹄铁。再没有旁的马让我施展手艺，我头一遭便在自己那匹马的蹄子上试——倘若那当得起「马」字的话，它本算不得马，只是匹矮马。恐怕找遍全英格兰，也难寻一头更急需铁匠照拂的牲口了。它四只脚里有三只压根没有掌，第四只上只剩残存的一角，因此近来一路硬石路上走下来，蹄子破碎擦伤得不成样子。「你从前跟的是个补锅匠，」我对这牲口说，「如今跟的是个铁匠了。人们说，皮鞋匠家里人的鞋，永远比哪一行人家的都坏。做皮鞋的兴许如此，做铁鞋的可不能叫人这么说；至少不能叫人这么说我的家。告诉你吧，我的 *gry*，只要你跟着我，你脚上的掌、肚子里的料，都要比跟着你前头那位主人的时候强。」
我正在谷里打一只 *petul*；我得在这里说明一句：打马蹄铁的当儿，我若时不时说起马蹄铁之主——彼得伦格罗先生——的那路话，读者不必惊奇。这半天我一直在拉 *peshota*，也就是风箱，想把我那座原始的锻炉里的 *yag*——也就是火——催起来。

The *angar*, or coals, are now burning fiercely, casting forth sparks and long *vagescoe chipes*, or tongues of flame; a small bar of *sastra*, or iron, is lying in the fire, to the length of ten or twelve inches, and so far it is hot, very hot, exceeding hot, brother. And now you see me, *prala*, snatch the bar of iron, and place the heated end of it upon the *covantza*, or anvil, and forthwith I commence *cooring the sastra* as hard as if I had been just engaged by a master at the rate of *dui caulor*, or two shillings a day, brother; and when I have beaten the iron till it is nearly cool, and my arm tired, I place it again in the *angar*, and begin again to rouse the fire with the *pudamengro*, which signifies the blowing thing, and is another and more common word for bellows, and whilst thus employed I sing a gypsy song, the sound of which is wonderfully in unison with the hoarse moaning of the *pudamengro*, and ere the song is finished, the iron is again hot and malleable. Behold, I place it once more on the *covantza*, and recommence hammering; and now I am somewhat at fault; I am in want of assistance; I want you, brother, or someone else, to take the bar out of my hand and support it upon the *covantza*, whilst I, applying a *chinomescro*, or kind of chisel, to the heated iron, cut off with a lusty stroke or two of the *shukaro baro*, or big hammer, as much as is required for the *petul*. But having no one to help me, I go on hammering till I have fairly knocked off as much as I want, and then I place the piece in the fire, and again apply the bellows, and take up the song where I left it off; and when I have finished the song, I take out the iron, but this time with my plaistra, or pincers, and then I recommence hammering, turning the iron round and round with my pincers: and now I bend the iron, and lo, and behold, it has assumed something the outline of a *petul*.
I am not going to enter into further details with respect to the process﻿—it was rather a wearisome one. I had to contend with various disadvantages; my forge was a rude one, my tools might have been better; I was in want of one or two highly necessary implements, but, above all, manual dexterity.

*angar*，也就是煤，此刻烧得正凶，迸着火星，吐着长长的 *vagescoe chipes*，也就是火舌；一小条 *sastra*——铁——躺在火里，烧着的有十到十二英寸；烧着的那一段已经热了，很热，热得不能再热了，老兄。现在你看，*prala*，我一把抽出铁条，把烧热的那一头摃上 *covantza*——铁砧，随即 *cooring the sastra*——打起铁来——那股狠劲，仿佛刚被哪位掌柜的雇下，工钱 *dui caulor*，一天两先令，老兄；待我把铁打到将近冷了、胳膊也打乏了，再把它送回 *angar* 里，又拿 *pudamengro* 去催火——这个词的意思是「吹的东西」，是风箱的另一个、也更常用的叫法——这样干活的时候，我唱起一支吉卜赛歌，那调子同 *pudamengro* 沙哑的哼哧声妙得不可思议地合着拍；歌还没唱完，铁又热了、软了。看哪，我把它再一次放上 *covantza*，重新抡起锤来；这会儿我可有点抓瞎：我缺个帮手；我需要你，老兄，或者随便哪位，从我手里接住铁条，替我把它扶在 *covantza* 上，我好把 *chinomescro*——一种凿子——贴上烧热的铁，抡起 *shukaro baro*——大锤——有力地一两下，敲下打 *petul* 所需的那一截。可没人帮我，我便只管一路锤下去，直到敲够了我所要的长度；随后把那一截放回火里，再拉风箱，再把歌从方才断的地方唱起；歌唱完了，我取出铁来——这回用的是 plaistra，也就是钳子——然后重新开锤，用钳子把铁转来转去：现在我弯铁了；看哪，瞧啊，它已经有了几分 *petul* 的轮廓。
工序的细目我不再多写——那是相当磨人的一桩。我不得不同种种不利相争：锻炉是粗陋的，工具本可以更好；还缺一两件顶要紧的家什；而最缺的，是手上的熟巧。

Though free of the forge, I had not practised the albeytarian art for very many years, never since﻿—but stay, it is not my intention to tell the reader, at least in this place, how and when I became a blacksmith. There was one thing, however, which stood me in good stead in my labour, the same thing which through life has ever been of incalculable utility to me, and has not unfrequently supplied the place of friends, money, and many other things of almost equal importance﻿—iron perseverance, without which all the advantages of time and circumstance are of very little avail in any undertaking. I was determined to make a horseshoe, and a good one, in spite of every obstacle﻿—ay, in spite of *dukkerin*. At the end of four days, during which I had fashioned and refashioned the thing at least fifty times, I had made a *petul* such as no master of the craft need have been ashamed of; with the second shoe I had less difficulty, and, by the time I had made the fourth, I would have scorned to take off my hat to the best smith in Cheshire.
But I had not yet shod my little *gry*; this I proceeded now to do. After having first well pared the hoofs with my *churi*, I applied each *petul* hot, glowing hot to the *pindro*. Oh, how the hoofs hissed; and, oh, the pleasant, pungent odour which diffused itself through the dingle, an odour good for an ailing spirit.
I shod the little horse bravely﻿—merely pricked him once, slightly, with a *cafi*, for doing which, I remember, he kicked me down; I was not disconcerted, however, but, getting up, promised to be more cautious in future; and having finished the operation, I filed the hoof well with the *rin baro*; then dismissed him to graze amongst the trees, and, putting my smaller tools into the *muchtar*, I sat down on my stone, and, supporting my arm upon my knee, leaned my head upon my hand. Heaviness had come over me.

虽说厕身锻炉行内，这打铁的手艺我却多年没有摸过了，打那以后再没有——且慢，我并不打算告诉读者，至少在此地不打算，我怎样、何时当上的铁匠。不过有一件东西在我这劳作里帮了大忙；也正是它，一生于我功不可没，还不时顶了朋友、钱财和许多几乎同等重要之物的作用——铁打的坚忍；没有它，时机与境遇的一切便宜，在任何事业里都所值无几。我打定主意要打出一只马蹄铁，一只好的，不管多少障碍——是的，哪怕 *dukkerin* 挡道。到第四天头上——那东西我打了又改、改了又打，至少五十遍——我打出了一只本行的老师傅也不必脸红的 *petul*；第二只掌就少费了些事；待打到第四只，哪怕柴郡最好的铁匠，我也不屑于脱帽了。
可我这匹小 *gry* 还没钉掌呢；这就动手。先用 *churi* 把蹄子好好削净，再趁热——烧得炽炽地热——把一只只 *petul* 贴上 *pindro*。哦，蹄子嘶嘶地响；哦，那股又香又辛的气味漫过全谷——一味对害病的心神大有益处的气味。
我英勇地给小马钉完了掌——只是有一回，用 *cafi* 把它轻轻扎了一下；我记得，为这一下它把我踢了个跟头；我倒不慌，爬起来，许诺往后更小心些；钉完了活，我用 *rin baro* 把蹄子好好锉了一遭；然后放它到树间吃草去，把小件家伙收进 *muchtar*，在我那块石头上坐下，胳膊支着膝盖，头伏在手上。一阵沉重压上了我。
