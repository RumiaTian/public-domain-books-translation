“Brother,” said Ursula, plucking a dandelion which grew at her feet, “I have always said that a more civil and pleasant-spoken person than yourself can’t be found. I have a great regard for you and your learning, and am willing to do you any pleasure in the way of words or conversation. Mine is not a very happy story, but as you wish to hear it, it is quite at your service. Launcelot Lovell made me an offer, as you call it, and we were married in Roman fashion; that is, we gave each other our right hands, and promised to be true to each other. We lived together two years, travelling sometimes by ourselves, sometimes with our relations; I bore him two children, both of which were stillborn, partly, I believe, from the fatigue I underwent in running about the country telling *dukkerin* when I was not exactly in a state to do so, and partly from the kicks and blows which my husband Launcelot was in the habit of giving me every night, provided I came home with less than five shillings, which it is sometimes impossible to make in the country, provided no fair or merrymaking is going on. At the end of two years my husband, Launcelot, whistled a horse from a farmer’s field, and sold it for forty pounds; and for that horse he was taken, put in prison, tried and condemned to be sent to the other country for life. Two days before he was to be sent away, I got leave to see him in the prison, and in the presence of the turnkey I gave him a thin cake of gingerbread, in which there was a dainty saw which could cut through iron. I then took on wonderfully, turned my eyes inside out, fell down in a seeming fit, and was carried out of the prison. That same night my husband sawed his irons off, cut through the bars of his window, and dropping down a height of fifty feet, lighted on his legs, and came and joined me on a heath where I was camped alone. We were just getting things ready to be off, when we heard people coming, and sure enough they were runners after my husband, Launcelot Lovell; for his escape had been discovered within a quarter of an hour after he had got away.

等了一小会儿我也起身；天已黑了，我想最好还是回谷地去；谷口正遇着彼得伦格罗先生。「怎么样，兄弟，」他说，「你同厄休拉篱笆底下谈了番什么？」
「你想听我们谈什么，就该来同我们并排坐下；你知道我们在哪儿。」
「兄弟，我干的同这差不多：我坐在你们背后了。」
「在篱笆那一边，贾斯珀？」
「篱笆那一边，兄弟。」
「我们的话你全听了？」
「一字不落，兄弟；好一场古怪的谈话。」
「老话说得好，贾斯珀：偷听的听不见自己的好话；厄休拉送你的那个雅号，你多半听见了。」
「你说的雅号若是她骂我撒谎精，听见了，兄弟；她骂得也不大错，我实在未必字字抱定真话；至于我对你，你可抱怨不出多少来。」
「你在厄休拉的事上骗了我，叫我明白她没嫁人。」
「我告诉你的时候她确实没嫁，兄弟——至少没嫁给西尔维斯特；她要嫁他，我也不晓得。我一度以为你对她有几分意思；她呢，一个罗曼尼*chi*对*gorgio*能有几分，她对你也有几分。我半指望听你在篱笆后头向她求爱；如今看来，这世上除了旧词儿同怪故事，你什么都不放在心上。哎呀，把个年轻姑娘约到篱笆底下，同她谈的尽是你那一套；可你到底把你想要的都套出来了——凭你那番老福尔彻同梅丽迪亚娜的花活。你是个刁的，兄弟。」
「这你可说差了，贾斯珀。我不刁。人家若觉得我刁，那是他们自己一身是机巧，见了坦白的品性反而猜不透。你们那些女人实在是奇物，贾斯珀。」
「我没说过她们是古怪的畜生么？兄弟，只要她们牢牢粘着我们，我们罗马人就永远抱成一团。」
「你料她们永远会么，贾斯珀？」
「说不上，兄弟；没有万年不散的东西。罗曼尼*chi*如今还是罗曼尼*chi*，只是不完全是六十年前的样子了。我老婆虽古怪，可不是赫恩太太那一流，兄弟。我看她有点迷法国人、迷法国话。
