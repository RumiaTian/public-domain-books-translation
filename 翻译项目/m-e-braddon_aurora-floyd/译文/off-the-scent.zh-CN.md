It is scarcely necessary to say, that, with the button by Crosby in his pocket, and with the information acquired from Dawson the gardener, stowed away carefully in his mind, Mr. Joseph Grimstone looked with an eye of particular interest upon Steeve Hargraves the “Softy.”
The detective had not come to Doncaster alone. He had brought with him a humble ally and follower, in the shape of the little shabby-looking man who had encountered the “Softy” at the railway station, having received orders to keep a close watch upon Mr. Stephen Hargraves. It was of course a very easy matter to identify the “Softy” in the town of Doncaster, where he had been pretty generally known since his childhood.
Mr. Grimstone had called upon a medical practitioner, and had submitted the button to him for inspection. The stains upon it were indeed that which the detective had supposed﻿—blood; and the surgeon detected a minute morsel of cartilage adhering to the jagged hasp of the button; but the same surgeon declared that this missile could not have been the one used by the murderer of James Conyers. It had not been through the dead man’s body; it had inflicted only a surface wound.
The business which now lay before Mr. Grimstone was the tracing of one or other of the banknotes; and for this purpose he and his ally set to work upon the track of the “Softy,” with a view of discovering all the places which it was his habit to visit. The haunts affected by Mr. Hargraves turned out to be some half-dozen very obscure public-houses; and to each of these Joseph Grimstone went in person.
But he could discover nothing. All his inquiries only elicited the fact that Stephen Hargraves had not been observed to change, or to attempt to change, any banknote whatever. He had paid for all he had had, and spent more than it was usual for him to spend, drinking a good deal harder than had been his habit heretofore; but he had paid in silver, except on one occasion, when he had changed a sovereign. The detective called at the bank; but no person answering the description of Stephen Hargraves had been observed there. The detective endeavoured to discover any friends or companions of the “Softy”; but here again he failed. The half-witted hanger-on of the Mellish stables had never made any friends, being entirely deficient in all social qualities.

It seems as if the minute a man has taken another man’s life, or forged his name, or embezzled his money, his ideas get fixed in one groove, and never can soar higher than Liverpool and the American packet.”
Mr. Chivers listened respectfully to his patron’s communications. He was very well pleased to see the serenity of his employer’s mind gradually returning.
“Now, I’ll tell you what, Tom,” said Mr. Grimstone. “If this chap has given us the slip, why he’s given us the slip, and he’s got a start of us, which we shan’t be able to pick up till half-past ten o’clock tonight, when there’s a train that’ll take us to Liverpool. If he *hasn’t* given us the slip, there’s only one way he can leave Doncaster, and that’s by this station; so you stay here patient and quiet till you see me, or hear from me. If he is in Doncaster, I’m jiggered if I don’t find him.”
With which powerful asseveration Mr. Grimstone walked away, leaving his scout to keep watch for the possible coming of the “Softy.”

他找乘警和搬运工挨个问：见没见过一个像「孱头」的人——白脸，驼背，穿灯芯绒裤、粗棉布褂；甚至钻进售票员的屋里，问了同样的问题。
没有；谁也没见过斯蒂芬·哈格雷夫斯先生。倒有两三个人，听侦探一形容便认出了他，反问：先生打听的是不是梅利什庄园的马房里那位。格里姆斯通先生对这一问支支吾吾。我们知道，保密是他办事的本则。
「他兴许有法子把这些人全甩了，」他推心置腹地对忠心而丧气的帮手说。「他兴许谁的眼皮底下都溜过去了。钱在他身上，我几乎拿得稳；他打的主意，就是奔利物浦。他昨天打听火车，就是明证。我如今可以打电报，叫利物浦截他——假定他真把咱们全甩了、奔那边去了——只要我肯放旁人入局；可我不肯。赢要独赢，输也独输。他也可能另耍一套，走运河船——赶集人用的那种——奔赫尔，再溜去汉堡，诸如此类；可那不大像——这路人走道儿向来一条。仿佛一个人一旦要了另一条命、冒了人家的名、或挪了人家的钱，他的念头就钉进一条槽里，再也高不出利物浦和美利加邮船。」
奇弗斯先生恭恭敬敬听东家的训示。眼看东家的心境渐渐回平，他大为高兴。
「我告诉你怎么办，汤姆，」格里姆斯通先生说。「这家伙若是真甩了咱们，那他甩了就甩了，他占了咱们的先；这先，咱们到今晚十点半才追得回——有一班车送咱们去利物浦。他若没甩掉，离唐卡斯特的路就剩一条：就是这个车站；所以你守在这里，安安稳稳，等见着我，或接我的信。他只要还在唐卡斯特，我拼了命也把他寻出来。」
撂下这句斩钉截铁的誓言，格里姆斯通先生走了，留下他的望风，守候「孱头」可能的光临。
