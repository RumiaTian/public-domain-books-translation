===Original===

During the battles of the Somme in 1916, and afterward in periods of progress and retreat over the abominable fields, the city of Amiens was the capital of the British army. When the battles began in July of that year it was only a short distance away from the fighting-lines; near enough to hear the incessant roar of gunfire on the French front and ours, and near enough to get, by motorcar or lorry, in less than thirty minutes, to places where men were being killed or maimed or blinded in the routine of the day’s work. One went out past Amiens station and across a little stone bridge which afterward, in the enemy’s advance of 1918, became the mark for German high velocities along the road to Querrieux, where Rawlinson had his headquarters of the Fourth Army in an old château with pleasant meadows round it and a stream meandering through fields of buttercups in summertime. Beyond the dusty village of Querrieux with its white cottages, from which the plaster fell off in blotches as the war went on, we went along the straight highroad to Albert, through the long and straggling village of Lahoussoye, where Scottish soldiers in reserve lounged about among frowsy peasant women and played solemn games with “the bairns”; and so, past camps and hutments on each side of the road, to the ugly redbrick town where the Golden Virgin hung head downward from the broken tower of the church with her Babe outstretched above the fields of death as though as a peace-offering to this world at war.

One could be killed any day in Albert. I saw men blown to bits there the day after the battles of the Somme began. It was in the road that turned to the right, past the square to go to Méaulte and on to Fricourt. There was a tide of gun transport swirling down the road, bringing up new ammunition for the guns that were firing without a pause over Fricourt and Mametz. The high scream of a shell came through a blue sky and ended on its downward note with a sharp crash. For a few minutes the transport column was held up while a mass of raw flesh which a second before had been two living men and their horses was cleared out of the way. Then the gun wagons went at a harder pace down the road, raising a cloud of white dust out of which I heard the curses of the drivers, swearing in a foul way to disguise their fear.

一九一六年索姆河各战役期间，以及后来在那片可憎的原野上进退的时期，亚眠城是英国军队的都城。那年七月战役打响时，它离战线只有短短一段；近得听得见法军和我们前线不停歇的炮吼，近得坐汽车或卡车不到三十分钟就能到那些地方——人们正在那里按当天的例行工作被杀死、致残、打瞎。人出亚眠车站，过一座小石桥——后来一九一八年敌军推进时，那桥成了德国高速弹沿去克雷约的公路的标靶——罗林森的第四集团军司令部就设在克雷约一座老城堡里，四周宜人的草甸，夏天一条溪流蜿蜒穿过毛茛的田野。过了尘土的克雷约村——白村舍，随着战争拖长，墙皮一块块剥落——我们沿笔直的大路去阿尔贝，穿过拉乌索耶那个又长又散的村子，苏格兰预备兵在那里同邋遢的村妇闲逛，同「小娃儿」们玩郑重的游戏；就这样，过路两边的营地和棚屋，到那座丑陋的红砖城——金圣母头朝下悬在教堂断塔上，怀里的圣婴伸向死亡的田野，仿佛献给这个交战世界的一件和平祭。

在阿尔贝，人随便哪天都可能死。索姆河战役打响的第二天，我就在那里看见人被炸成碎块。就在右拐去梅奥尔特再去弗里库尔的那条路上。一股炮兵运输的潮水顺路卷过，给正对弗里库尔和马梅茨不停轰击的火炮送新弹药。一声炮弹的高啸穿过蓝天，落调一收，一声脆炸。运输队停了几分钟，把一团生肉清出道来——一秒钟前那还是两个活人和他们的马。随后炮车加紧赶路，卷起一团白尘，尘里我听见车夫们的咒骂，骂得脏，遮他们的怕。

I went through Albert many scores of times to the battlefields beyond, and watched its process of disintegration through those years, until it was nothing but a wild scrap heap of red brick and twisted iron, and, in the last phase, even the Golden Virgin and her Babe, which had seemed to escape all shellfire by miraculous powers, lay buried beneath a mass of masonry. Beyond were the battlefields of the Somme where every yard of ground is part of the great graveyard of our youth.

我几十次穿过阿尔贝去那边的战场，看着它那些年里的碎解过程，直到它只剩红砖和拧铁的野堆，最后阶段，连凭着神力似乎躲过了一切炮火的金圣母和她的圣婴，也埋进了一大堆砌体底下。再往前，就是索姆河的战场，那里每一码地都是我们青春那片大墓园的一部分。

So Amiens, as I have said, was not far away from the red heart of war, and was clear enough to the lines to be crowded always with officers and men who came out between one battle and another, and by “lorry-jumping” could reach this city for a few hours of civilized life, according to their views of civilization. To these men﻿—boys, mostly﻿—who had been living in lousy ditches under hell fire, Amiens was Paradise, with little hells for those who liked them. There were hotels in which they could go get a bath, if they waited long enough or had the luck to be early on the list. There were streets of shops with plate-glass windows unbroken, shining, beautiful. There were well-dressed women walking about, with kind eyes, and children as dainty, some of them, as in High Street, Kensington, or Prince’s Street, Edinburgh. Young officers, who had plenty of money to spend﻿—because there was no chance of spending money between a row of blasted trees and a ditch in which bits of dead men were plastered into the parapet﻿—invaded the shops and bought fancy soaps, razors, hair-oil, stationery, pocketbooks, knives, flash-lamps, top-boots (at a fabulous price), khaki shirts and collars, gramophone records, and the latest set of Kirchner prints. It was the delight of spending, rather than the joy of possessing, which made them go from one shop to another in search of things they could carry back to the line﻿—that and the lure of girls behind the counters, laughing, bright-eyed girls who understood their execrable French, even English spoken with a Glasgow accent, and were pleased to flirt for five minutes with any group of young fighting-men﻿—who broke into roars of laughter at the gallantry of some Don Juan among them with the gift of audacity, and paid outrageous prices for the privilege of stammering out some foolish sentiment in broken French, blushing to the roots of their hair (though captains and heroes) at their own temerity with a girl who, in another five minutes, would play the same part in the same scene with a different group of boys.

于是，如我说过，亚眠离战争的红色心脏不远，离战线又够空，总是挤满两仗之间出来的官兵，靠「跳卡车」几个钟头就摸到这座城，过一把文明生活——按他们对文明的理解。对这些人——多半是男孩——在地狱之火下的虱窝沟里住了好久的人来说，亚眠是天堂，还附带给喜欢者的几处小地狱。有旅馆，等得够久或运气好排在名单前头，就能洗上个澡。有整街的铺子，大玻璃橱窗不破，亮晶晶，美。有衣着一新的女人走来，眼睛和善，孩子精致，有的不输肯辛顿的高街或爱丁堡的王子街。年轻军官们钱多得花不完——因为在一排烧秃的树和一条把死人碎肉糊进胸墙的沟之间，没有花钱的机会——涌进了那些铺子，买香皂、剃刀、发油、文具、皮夹、小刀、手电、长筒靴（天价）、卡其衬衫和领子、唱片，还有最新一套基希纳版画。让他们挨店挨家跑的，是花的快乐，不是有的快乐——还有柜台后姑娘们的诱惑：爱笑、眼睛发亮，听得懂他们那糟透的法语，连格拉斯哥腔的英语也行，乐意同任何一小伙年轻作战人调情五分钟——小伙们为他们中间某个有胆量的唐璜的献殷情哄然大笑，为结巴着用破碎法语说傻话的特权付出离谱的价，脸红到发根（虽然身为上尉和英雄）——为自己的唐突害臊，而那姑娘五分钟后就将在同一幕戏里同另一伙男孩演同一个角色。

I used to marvel at the patience of these girls. How bored they must have been with all this flirtation, which led to nothing except, perhaps, the purchase of a bit of soap at twice its proper price! They knew that these boys would leave to go back to the trenches in a few hours and that some of them would certainly be dead in a few days. There could be no romantic episode, save of a transient kind, between them and these good-looking lads in whose eyes there were desire and hunger, because to them the plainest girl was Womanhood, the sweet, gentle, and feminine side of life, as opposed to the cruelty, brutality, and ugliness of war and death. The shopgirls of Amiens had no illusions. They had lived too long in war not to know the realities. They knew the risks of transient love and they were not taking them﻿—unless conditions were very favorable. They attended strictly to business and hoped to make a lot of money in the shop, and were, I think, mostly good girls﻿—as virtuous as life in wartime may let girls be﻿—wise beyond their years, and with pity behind their laughter for these soldiers who tried to touch their hands over the counters, knowing that many of them were doomed to die for France and England. They had their own lovers﻿—boys in blue somewhere between Vaux-sur-Somme and Hartmanns-weilerkopf﻿—and apart from occasional intimacies with English officers quartered in Amiens for long spells, left the traffic of passion to other women who walked the streets.

我常惊叹这些姑娘的耐心。这调情多叫她们腻啊——它通不到任何地方，顶多按双倍价卖出一块香皂！她们知道这些男孩几小时后就回战壕，其中一些人几天内必死。她们同这些漂亮小伙子之间不会有什么罗曼史，除了转瞬那一种，因为对他们，最平常的姑娘也是女人——生活里甜蜜、温柔、女性的一面，对着战争与死亡的残忍、粗暴和丑。亚眠的店娘们没有幻想。她们在战争里活得太久，懂现实。她们知道露水爱的风险，不冒——除非条件非常有利。她们严守生意，指望在店里挣一大笔；我想她们多半是好姑娘——战时的生活许可姑娘们怎么贞，她们就怎么贞——比她们的年纪懂事得多，笑声后面藏着可怜，可怜这些隔着柜台想碰她们手的士兵——知道他们中许多注定为法兰西和英格兰去死。她们有自己的爱人——蓝色军装的男孩，在索姆河畔沃和哈特曼斯韦勒峰之间的什么地方——除了同长驻亚眠的英国军官偶尔的亲近，把激情的买卖留给街上走的女人。

The Street of the Three Pebbles﻿—la rue des Trois Cailloux﻿—which goes up from the station through the heart of Amiens, was the crowded highway. Here were the best shops﻿—the hairdresser, at the left-hand side, where all day long officers down from the line came in to have elaborate luxury in the way of close crops with *friction d’eau de quinine*, shampooing, singeing, oiling, not because of vanity, but because of the joyous sense of cleanliness and perfume after the filth and stench of life in the desolate fields; then the booksellers’ (Madame Carpentier *et fille*) on the right-hand side, which was not only the rendezvous of the miscellaneous crowd buying stationery and *La Vie Parisienne*, but of the intellectuals who spoke good French and bought good books and liked ten minutes’ chat with the mother and daughter. (Madame was an Alsatian lady with vivid memories of 1870, when, as a child, she had first learned to hate Germans.) She hated them now with a fresh, vital hatred, and would have seen her own son dead a hundred times﻿—he was a soldier in Saloniki﻿—rather than that France should make a compromise peace with the enemy. She had been in Amiens, as I was, on a dreadful night of August of 1914, when the French army passed through in retreat from Bapaume, and she and the people of her city knew for the first time that the Germans were close upon them. She stood in the crowd as I did﻿—in the darkness, watching that French column pass with their transport, and their wounded lying on the baggage wagons, men of many regiments mixed up, the light of the street lamps shining on the casques of cuirassiers with their long horsehair tails, leading their stumbling horses, and foot soldiers, hunched under their packs, marching silently with dragging steps. Once in a while one of the soldiers left the ranks and came on to the sidewalk, whispering to a group of dark shadows. The crowds watched silently, in a curious, dreadful silence, as though stunned. A woman near me spoke in a low voice, and said, “*Nous sommes perdus!*” Those were the only words I heard or remembered.

三石街——从车站上去、穿过亚眠心脏的那条——是拥挤的大道。这里有好铺子——左边理发店，从线上下来的军官全天拥进去，享受精致奢侈的贴头皮剪，加奎宁水摩擦、香洗、火燎、上油——不为虚荣，为的是荒凉田野生活的污秽恶臭之后，那种干净和香气的快活感；右边书铺（卡庞捷太太和女儿），它不但是买文具和《巴黎生活》的杂色人群的会合点，也是说法语的好、买好书的知识分子的——他们喜欢同母女俩聊十分钟。（太太是位阿尔萨斯淑女，对一八七〇年记忆鲜明，那时她还是孩子，头一回学会恨德国人。）如今她恨他们，恨得新鲜而有生气，宁愿自己的儿子死一百回——他在萨洛尼卡当兵——也不愿法兰西同敌人妥协媾和。一九一四年八月那个可怕的夜晚，她同我一样在亚眠，法军从巴波姆溃退经过，她和她城里的人头一回知道德国人近在身后。她同我一样站在人群里——黑暗中，看那支法国纵队带着辎重过，伤者躺在行李车上，许多团的人混着，街灯的光照在胸甲骑兵的盔上，马鬃垂着，牵着踉跄的马，步兵背着包弓着身，拖着步子默默行军。时不时有个士兵出列，走上人行道，对一群黑影低语。人群静静看着，一种古怪而可怕的静，像被打懵了。我身旁一个女人低声说：「Nous sommes perdus（我们完了）！」那是我听见或记得的唯一的话。

That night in the station of Amiens the boys of a new class were being hurried away in truck trains, and while their army was in retreat sang “La Marseillaise,” as though victory were in their hearts. Next day the German army under von Kluck entered Amiens, and ten days afterward passed through it on the way to Paris. Madame Carpentier told me of the first terror of the people when the field-gray men came down the Street of the Three Pebbles and entered their shops. A boy selling oranges fainted when a German stretched out his hand to buy some. Women hid behind their counters when German boots stamped into their shops. But Madame Carpentier was not afraid. She knew the Germans and their language. She spoke frank words to German officers, who saluted her respectfully enough. “You will never get to Paris﻿ ﻿… France and England will be too strong for you﻿ ﻿… Germany will be destroyed before this war ends.” They laughed at her and said: “We shall be in Paris in a week from now. Have you a little diary, Madame?” Madame Carpentier was haughty with them. Some women of Amiens﻿—poor drabs﻿—did not show any haughtiness, nor any pride, with the enemy who crowded into the city on their way toward Paris. A girl told me that she was looking through the window of a house that faced the Place de la Gare, and saw a number of German soldiers dancing round a piano-organ which was playing to them. They were dancing with women of the town, who were laughing and screeching in the embrace of big, blond Germans. The girl who was watching was only a schoolgirl then. She knew very little of the evil of life, but enough to know that there was something in this scene degrading to womanhood and to France. She turned from the window and flung herself on her bed and wept bitterly﻿ ﻿…

那天夜里，亚眠车站上，新一届的男孩们正被塞进敞车急急运走，他们的军队在溃退中，他们唱着《马赛曲》，仿佛胜利在胸。第二天，冯·克卢克麾下的德军进了亚眠，十天后取道它去巴黎。卡庞捷太太对我讲过灰野战服的人顺着三石街下来、走进铺子时，人们最初的恐怖。一个卖橙子的男孩，一个德国人伸手要买时，晕倒了。德国靴子踏进铺子时，女人们躲到柜台后。可卡庞捷太太不怕。她认得德国人，会他们的话。她对德国军官说直话，军官们对她倒相当恭敬。「你们到不了巴黎……法兰西和英格兰对你们太强了……这场战争结束前德国就会被毁掉。」他们笑她，说：「一周后我们就在巴黎了。您有小日历册么，太太？」卡庞捷太太对他们高傲。亚眠有些女人——可怜的邋遢货——对涌进城、取道去巴黎的敌人，没有显出高傲，也没有显出自尊。一个姑娘告诉我，她从面朝车站广场的一扇窗里望出去，看见一群德国兵围着一架替他们演奏的手摇风琴跳舞。同他们跳的是城里的野鸡，在大个子金发德国人的怀抱里笑着尖叫。看的那个姑娘当时只是个女学生。人生的恶她懂得很少，可已够她知道这一幕里有辱没女人、辱没法兰西的东西。她从窗前转过身，扑在床上，痛苦地哭了……

I used to call in at the bookshop for a chat now and then with Madame and Mademoiselle Carpentier, while a crowd of officers came in and out. Madame was always merry and bright in spite of her denunciations of the “*Sale Boches﻿—les brigands, les bandits!*” and Mademoiselle put my knowledge of French to a severe but pleasant test. She spoke with alarming rapidity, her words tumbling over one another in a cascade of volubility delightful to hear but difficult to follow. She had a strong mind﻿—masterly in her methods of business﻿—so that she could serve six customers at once and make each one think that her attention was entirely devoted to his needs﻿—and a very shrewd and critical idea of military strategy and organization. She had but a poor opinion of British generals and generalship, although a wholehearted admiration for the gallantry of British officers and men; and she had an intimate knowledge of our preparations, plans, failures, and losses. French liaison-officers confided to her the secrets of the British army; and English officers trusted her with many revelations of things “in the wind.” But Mademoiselle Carpentier had discretion and loyalty and did not repeat these things to people who had no right to know. She would have been far more efficient as a staff officer than many of the young gentlemen with red tabs on their tunics who came into the shop, flipping beautiful top-boots with riding-crops, sitting on the counter, and turning over the pages of *La Vie* for the latest convention in ladies’ legs.

Mademoiselle was a serious musician, so her mother told me, but her musical studies were seriously interrupted by business and air raids, which one day ceased in Amiens altogether after a night of horror, when hundreds of houses were smashed to dust and many people killed, and the Germans brought their guns close to the city﻿—close enough to scatter high velocities about its streets﻿—and the population came up out of their cellars, shaken by the terror of the night, and fled. I passed the bookshop where Mademoiselle was locking up the door of this house which had escaped by greater luck than its neighbors. She turned as I passed and raised her hand with a grave gesture of resignation and courage. “*Ils ne passeront pas!*” she said. It was the spirit of the courage of French womanhood which spoke in those words.

我常去书铺，同卡庞捷太太和小姐聊一阵，军官们进进出出。太太永远轻快乐观，尽管骂着「Sale Boches（脏波希）——强盗！土匪！」，小姐则把我的法语架在严厉而愉快的火上烤。她说得吓人地快，词一个绊一个，滔滔的瀑布，好听，难追。她头脑强干——生意手法大师级——能同时招呼六个客人，让每个都觉得她的注意力全归他——对军事战略和组织又有一套精明挑剔的见解。她对英国将军和将道评价不高，虽然对英国官兵的英勇全心钦佩；对我们的准备、计划、失败和损失，她了如指掌。法国联络军官向她吐英军的秘密；英国军官们把许多「风闻」托付给她。可卡庞捷小姐有分寸有忠心，不把这些讲给无权知道的人。她当参谋军官，比许多进店来的、军衣上缀红领章、拿马鞭抽着漂亮长筒靴、坐上柜台、翻《巴黎生活》看女士大腿最新款式的年轻绅士强得多。

小姐是位严肃的音乐家——她母亲告诉我——可她的音乐学业被生意和空袭严重打断；有一天空袭在亚眠彻底停了，在恐怖的一夜之后——几百座房屋砸成灰，许多人死，德国人把炮拖到城边——近得高速弹撒进街道——人们从地窖里上来，被夜的恐怖震着，逃了。我经过书铺，小姐正锁门——这座宅子比邻居们运气好，逃过了。我经过时她转过身，抬起手，一个庄重的听任与勇气的手势。「Ils ne passeront pas（他们过不去）！」她说。那是法兰西女性勇气的精神，借着那几个字说话。

That was in the last phase of the war, but the Street of the Three Pebbles had been tramped up and down for two years before then by the British armies on the Somme, with the French on their right. I was never tired of watching those crowds and getting into the midst of them, and studying their types. All the types of young English manhood came down this street, and some of their faces showed the strain and agony of war, especially toward the end of the Somme battles, after four months or more of slaughter. I saw boys with a kind of hunted look in their eyes; and Death was the hunter. They stared into the shop windows in a dazed way, or strode along with packs on their backs, looking neither to the right nor to the left, and white, haggard faces, as expressionless as masks. Tomorrow or the next day, perhaps, the Hunter would track them down. Other English officers showed no sign at all of apprehension or lack of nerve-control, although the psychologist would have detected disorder of soul in the rather deliberate note of hilarity with which they greeted their friends, in gusts of laughter, for no apparent cause, at “Charlie’s bar,” where they would drink three cocktails apiece on an empty stomach, and in their tendency to tell tales of horror as things that were very funny.
