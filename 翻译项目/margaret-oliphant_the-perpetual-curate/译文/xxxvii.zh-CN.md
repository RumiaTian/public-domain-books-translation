The little assembly which met in the vestry of Carlingford Church to inquire into the conduct of the Perpetual Curate, had so many different interests in hands when it dispersed, and so much to do, that it is difficult for the narrator of this history to decide which thread should be taken up first. ===Original===

The conclusion of this letter gave Lucy a good deal of trouble. Her honest heart was so moved with gratitude and admiration that she had nearly called herself “affectionately” Mr. Wentworth’s. Why should not she? “He has acted like a brother to us,” Lucy said to herself; and then she paused to inquire whether his conduct had indeed arisen from brotherly motives solely. Then, when she had begun to write “faithfully” instead, a further difficulty occurred to her. Not thus lightly and unsolicited could she call herself “faithful,” for did not the word mean everything that words could convey in any human relationship? When she had concluded it at last, and satisfied her scruples by the formula above, she laid the letter before her sister. This event terminated the active operations of the day in the dwelling of the Wodehouses. Their brother had not asked to see them, had not interrupted them as yet in their retreat upstairs, where they were sedulously waited upon by the entire household. When Miss Wodehouse’s agitation was over, she too began to collect together her books and personalities, and they ended by a long consultation where they were to go and what they were to do, during the course of which the elder sister exhibited with a certain shy pride that little photograph of the new rectory, in which there was one window embowered in foliage, which the bride had already concluded was to be Lucy’s room. Lucy yielded during this sisterly conference to sympathetic thoughts even of Mr. Proctor. The two women were alone in the world. They were still so near the grave and the deathbed that chance words spoken without thought from time to time awakened in both the ready tears. Now and then they each paused to consider with a sob what *he* would have liked best. They knew very little of what was going on outside at the moment when they were occupied with those simple calculations. What was to become of them, as people say﻿—what money they were to have, or means of living﻿—neither was much occupied in thinking of. They had each other; they had, besides, one a novel and timid middle-aged confidence, the other an illimitable youthful faith in one man in the world. Even Lucy, whose mind and thoughts were more individual than her sister’s, wanted little else at that moment to make her happy with a tender tremulous consolation in the midst of her grief.

这封信的落款叫露西费了好一番踌躇。她那颗诚实的心为感激与倾慕鼓荡着，几乎写下了「深情依恋您的」。为什么不呢？「他待我们像一位兄长。」露西自忖；随即又停下来盘问自己：他的种种行事，当真纯出于兄长之心吗？后来她改笔要写「忠实的」，又碰到一层新的难处。这样轻率地、又是在无人相求之下，她怎能自称「忠实」呢——须知这个词，在人间一切关系里，把言语能传达的意思都说尽了吗？待信终于收尾、又用上面那套格式把良心安抚平了，她才把信摆到姐姐面前。这件事，就此终结了伍德豪斯家这一天里种种活动。她们的哥哥没有求见她们，也还没有上楼搅扰她们的幽居——全家上下都在殷勤伺候着。伍德豪斯小姐激动一过，也开始收拾起自己的书和随身零碎；末了是长长一场商议：去哪里，做什么。商议中间，姐姐带着几分羞怯的得意，出示了那张新教区长宅的小照片，其中有一扇窗掩在绿叶里——新娘子已经认定，那是露西的房间。在这场姊妹的商谈里，露西一时连普罗克特先生也起了同情之念。这两个女人在世上孤零零的。她们离坟墓和临终的病榻还这样近，以致偶尔一句无心的话，都会随时唤出两人的热泪。她们不时各自停下来，抽一口气，想想*他*最喜欢什么。至于外面此刻的种种，她们埋头于这些简单的计较，知道得极少。人家会怎样——她们会有多少钱、靠什么过活——两个人都很少想到。她们有彼此；此外，一个怀着新生的、怯生生的中年之信，一个怀着对世上一个男人无穷无尽的少女之信。连心思个性都比姐姐强的露西，此刻也差不多什么都不缺了——在哀伤的正中，有一样温柔的、颤巍巍的安慰，便足够她幸福。
