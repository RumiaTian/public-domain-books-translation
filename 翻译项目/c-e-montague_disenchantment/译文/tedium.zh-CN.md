## IV Tedium / 四 沉闷

### I / 一

===Original===

A book may be bad and yet tell you much. Lately I came across such a book. It is surely one of the crossest books ever written. Its author fought in France, in the ranks, for a good many months of the war. He must have been one of the men who make sergeants grey﻿—a “proper lawyer,” as Regulars call the type which a cotton district labels as “self-acting mules.”

I seem to know that man. He was a volunteer, but he would not enlist until conscription came in, because of some precious doctrine he had about younger men without families. When he did join his first act was to ask to speak to the colonel. He was aggrieved because army doctors would not act, when he desired it, except as such. When anyone checked him he felt an ardent thirst to “explain,” and the explanation was always that he who had checked was wrong. In the field he kept a diary and sternly would he note on its recording page that tea one day﻿—nay, on more than one﻿—was served “very late indeed.” Heinous!

The continued existence of war is precarious. More than the League of Nations menaces its future. For it depends, at the last, on the infrequency of “proper lawyers.” Armies can now be made, and moved about when made, only because the plain man who keeps the world going round does not stick up for the last ounce of his rights, or stick out for the joys of having the last word, so dourly as these. Even to keep up a game with so modest an element of voluntaryism about it as penal justice you have to have some little effort of cooperation all round. If your convicts will not even eat the whole thing begins crumbling. The “suffragettes” showed us that. A pioneer still earlier, an Indian coolie, proved it in a Fijian gaol. Were every soldier like this diarist war would have to be dropped, not because men were too good, but because they were too prickly.

===Chinese===

一本书可能很坏，却仍能告诉你许多东西。近来我就碰到了这样一本书。它想必是有史以来写得最暴躁的书之一。其作者在法国当兵，在行伍中熬过了战争的好些个月份。他必是那种能把军士们熬白头发的人——正规军（Regulars）把这一类型叫作「十足的讼师」（proper lawyer），而棉业地区则给他们贴上「自动骡子」（self-acting mules）的标签。

我仿佛认得这个人。他本是志愿投效者，却迟迟不肯入伍，直到征兵制（conscription）颁行才去当兵，因为他怀有一套宝贵的教义，认为该先轮到那些没有家室的更年轻的人。他一入伍，头一个举动便是请求面见团长。他愤愤不平，因为军医们不肯在他想要的时候便听凭差遣，只肯按军医自己的身份行事。每当有人拦他，他便燃起一股热切的干渴，非要「解释」不可，而解释的结论永远是：拦他的人错了。在战地他记日记，会在那记录的页面上铁面无私地记下：某日的茶——不，不止一日的茶——送得「实在是晚」。罪大恶极！

战争之得以继续存在，本就岌岌可危。比国际联盟（League of Nations）更厉害地威胁着它前途的，另有其物。因为归根结底，战争系于「十足的讼师」们之不多见。如今之所以还组建得起军队、组建之后还调得动，只是因为那位推着世界转个不停的普通人，并不像这一类人那样，死硬地坚持自己权利的最后一盎司，也不那样死死咬住「非说最后一句话不可」的乐趣。就连刑罚司法这样一门自愿成分已颇为微薄的游戏，要维持下去，也得人人四面八方通力合作那么一小点。倘若连你的苦役犯都绝食了，整座建筑便开始崩塌。「妇女参政论者」（suffragettes）们已经向我们示范过这一点。更早的一位先驱，一个印度苦力，在斐济（Fiji）的一座监狱里证实了它。倘若每个士兵都像这位日记作者一样，战争就只好作罢——不是因为人太好，而是因为他们刺头得过了头。

### II / 二

===Original===

And yet the book told something which no other book has yet succeeded in telling you. Wordy, cantankerous, dull, repeating itself like a decimal, padded with cheap political “thoughts” gathered from old “stunts” in bad papers﻿—still, it came nearer than any other to showing you the way trench warfare struck a mind and soul quite commonplace in everything except a double dose of native sourness. Here was nothing of M. Barbusse’s doctrinaire fire to make the author pervert or exaggerate. No thrill of drastic passion, not even the passionate self-pity of Dickens describing his childhood as Copperfield’s, stirred the plodding and crabbed narrative. The writer seemed too peevish to be at the pains to beautify or exalt. And so his account of the bungled attack in which he took part is extraordinarily true to all that the commonplace man found to be left in almost any attack when once all the picturesque fluff filling the current literary pictures of it were found not to be there﻿—the touch of bathos; the supposed heroic moment only seeming a bit of a “dud,” a miscarriage; the hugger-mugger element of confusion; the baffling way that the real thing did not so often give men obvious gallant things to do as irritating puzzles to solve, muddles to liquidate at short notice; the queer flashes of revelation, in contact with individual enemies, of the bottomless falsity of the cheaper kind of current war psychology.

Advances, however, were far from being the staple of warfare. They caused the most losses, but still they did less than the years of less sensational routine to make what changes were made by the war in the minds of the men in the ranks. And here our pettish author found the congenial theme for his own acrid, accurate method. His trivial reiterations succeed, in the end, in piling up in the reader’s mind an image of that old trench life as the sum of innumerable dreary units of irksome fatigue. This was the normal life of the infantry private in France. For N.C.O.’s it was lightened by the immunity of their rank from fatigue work in the technical sense. For the officer it was much further lightened by better quarters and the servant system. For most of his time the average private was tired. Fairly often he was so tired as no man at home ever is in the common run of his work.

===Chinese===

然而这本书讲出了一样东西，那是任何别的书都还没能成功地讲给你的。冗长，乖戾，乏味，像循环小数一般自我重复，塞满了从蹩脚报纸的旧「噱头」里捡来的廉价政治「思想」——即便如此，它仍比任何别的书都更接近于让你看清：堑壕战是怎样打击了一副头脑与灵魂——这副头脑与灵魂在一切方面都平庸无奇，唯独天生的酸劲比常人多上一倍。书中没有巴比塞先生（M. Barbusse）那种教条主义的火气，足以诱使作者歪曲或夸大。没有凌厉激情的震颤，甚至也没有狄更斯借科波菲尔（Copperfield）之笔描写自己童年时那种火热的自怜，来搅动这部步履滞重、脾气乖张的记叙。这位作者似乎火气太盛，懒得劳神去美化或拔高。于是，他对他亲身经历的那场打糟了的进攻的记述，格外真切地呈现了平凡人在几乎任何一场进攻中终于发现剩下来的东西——前提是，当人们发现，把当时文学图景填塞得满满当当的种种如画绒絮全都不在场之后——那一下由崇高跌落的突降（bathos）；那个想当然的英雄时刻，看上去只是一枚「哑弹」，一场失手；那乱成一团的乌烟瘴气；还有那令人困惑之处：真家伙交给人的，往往不是堂堂正正的英勇事业，而是叫人恼火的谜题，是要在仓促之间料理干净的乱局；还有同个别敌兵接触时种种怪异的灵光一闪，照出时下那类廉价战争心理学的无底虚假。

然而，进攻远非战争的主食。进攻造成的损失最重，但比起那些年头里不甚耸动的日常勤务来，它们在行伍士兵心里促成的改变——战争于士兵内心造成的一切改变——反倒更少。而正是在这里，我们这位易怒的作者为他那套尖刻而精确的笔法找到了投缘的题材。他那些琐屑的重复，最终成功地在读者心里堆起一幅图景：那旧日的堑壕生活，乃是无穷多个令人生厌的烦重苦役单元之和。这便是法国土地上步兵列兵的正常生活。对军士（N.C.O.）们而言，他们的军阶豁免了严格意义上的苦役，日子轻省了一层。对军官而言，更好的住处与勤务兵制度又使之轻省得多。普通列兵的大部分时间都在疲惫之中。他还常常疲惫到那样的地步——本土上任何人在寻常工作的常规进程里，是从来不曾疲惫到那里的。

===Original===

If a company’s trench strength was low and sentry-posts abounded more than usual in its sector a man might, for eight days running, get no more than one hour off duty at any one time, day or night. If enemy guns were active many of these hours off guard duty might have to be spent on trench repair. After one of these bad times in trenches a company or platoon would sometimes come out on to the road behind the communication trench like a flock of over-driven sheep. The weakest ones would fall out and drop here and there along the road, not as a rule fainting, but in the state of a horse deadbeat, to whom any amount of thrashing seems preferable to going on. Men would come out lightheaded with fatigue, and ramble away to the men next them about some great time which they had had, or meant to have, at home. Or a man would march all right till the road fetched a bend, and then he would march straight on into the ditch in his sleep. Upon a greasy road with a heavy camber I have seen a used-up man get the illusion, on a night-march back to billets, that he was walking on a round, smooth, horizontal pole or convex plank above some fearsome sort of gulf. He would struggle hard to recover imaginary losses of footing, pant and sweat and scrape desperately sideways with his feet like a frightened young horse new to harness when it leans in against the pole, with its feet skidding outwards on the setts. Down he would go, time after time, in the mud, each time as unable to rise of himself, under the weight of his pack and equipment, as any medieval knight unhorsed and held down by the weight of his armour. Hauled up again to his feet, to be driven along like one of the spent cab-horses in Naples just strong enough to move when up, but not to rise, he would in another five minutes be agonizing again on the greasy pole of his delirium.

The querulist of the book took it hard, I remember, that more kind words did not come to the men. He saw his own lot very clearly, but not so clearly the lot of those other unfortunates who had to put the job through. A man who finds himself in charge of a spent horse at night, in a place where there may be no safe waiting till dawn, must do something. Ten to one he will flog or kick the horse into moving. He may feel that he, not the horse, is the beast; but still he will do it. So, too, will he bully and curse exhausted men into safety. That was what happened. Every decent N.C.O. and company officer﻿—and far the larger part were decent﻿—did what they could to humour and “buck” the bad cases through the pangs of endurance. Some would reach the journey’s end carrying whole faggots of rifles. Some would put by their own daily rations of rum to ginger beaten men through the last mile. But there would come times when only hard driving seemed to be left. *Bella, horrida bella!*

倘若一个连队的堑壕兵力不足，而其防区里的哨位又比平常更密，那么一个人可能一连八天，无论昼夜，每次下哨都得不到多于一个钟头的休息。倘若敌炮活跃，这些离哨的钟头里有许多还得花在修补战壕上。熬过这样一段糟糕的堑壕时日之后，一个连队或一个排有时会走出交通壕（communication trench），上了后面的公路，像一群被赶过了头的羊。最虚弱的人会掉队，零零落落地倒在路边，通常并不是晕倒，而是处在一匹力竭的马的境地——对这样的马，怎么鞭打似乎都比继续前行可取。人们会带着疲惫到头昏的脑子走出来，向身旁的人语无伦次地絮叨着他们在国内曾度过的、或打算要度过的什么好时光。也有的人一路走得挺好，直到公路拐了弯，便在睡梦里照直走进路沟里去。在一条油腻泛光、路拱厚重的路上，我见过一个精疲力尽的人在夜行军回宿营地的途中生出错觉，以为自己正走在一根浑圆光滑的水平杆上、或一块凸面板上，下方是某种可怕的深渊。他会拼命挣扎，去夺回想象中失掉的立足处，喘气，流汗，两只脚绝望地朝旁边刨蹭，活像一匹初次上套、惊惶失措的年轻马——那马斜倚进辕杆，蹄子在石块路面上直向外打滑。他会一次又一次栽进泥里，每一次都在背包与全副装具的重量下，像任何一个被挑落马下、又被盔甲压住的中世纪骑士那样，自己再也起不来。被拽着重新架到脚上，像那不勒斯（Naples）那些拉客车的疲马——站起来了才挪得动、卧下去便起不来——那样被驱赶着往前走，不出五分钟，他又会在他那谵妄的油腻滑杆上苦苦挣扎起来。

我记得，书中那位满腹冤情的诉苦者对一件事耿耿于怀：温存话到士兵们这里来得太少。他把自己那份苦楚看得清清楚楚，对另外那些倒霉蛋的苦楚却看得不那么清楚——那些人必得把这趟差事押送到头。一个人若在夜里发觉自己管着一匹力竭的马，又身处一个未必能安然等到天亮的地方，就必须有所动作。十有八九，他会鞭打或脚踢那匹马，逼它挪步。他也许会觉得，是他自己、而不是那匹马，才是畜生；但他还是会干。同样，他也会连吓带咒，把精疲力尽的人们轰进安全地带。当时发生的就是如此。每一位正派的军士和连队军官——而他们绝大多数是正派的——都尽力哄着、鼓着那些垮得厉害的人，熬过忍耐的剧痛。有的人到达旅程终点时，肩上扛着一整捆步枪。有的人省下自己每日的朗姆酒配给，好在最后一里路上给被打垮的人提提气。但总有些时候，剩下的似乎只有狠命的驱赶。*Bella, horrida bella!*（战争，可怕的战争！）

### III / 三

===Original===

Suppose those first eight days in the front and support trenches to be the beginning of a divisional tour of sixteen days’ duty in the line. For four days now the weary men would be in reserve, under enemy fire, but not in trenches; probably in the cellars of ruined houses. But these were not times of rest. Each day or night every man would make one or more journeys back to the trenches that they had left carrying some load of food, water, or munitions up to the three companies in trenches, or perhaps leading a pack-mule over land to some point near the front line, under cover of night. Even to lead a laden mule in the dark over waste ground confusingly wired and trenched is work; to get him back on to his feet when fallen and wriggling, in wild consternation, among a tangle of old barbed wire may be quite hard work.

In intervals between these journeys most men would lie in the straw in the cellars or hobble weakly about the outside of the premises, looking as boys sometimes do when stiff with many hearty hacks sustained in a hard game of football, with a chill after it. They crawled in and out of their billets like late autumn bees, feebly scraping the eight days’ plating of mud off their clothes and cleaning their jackknives after meals with the languor of the elders in the Bible to whom the grasshopper was a burden. A few robust spirits, armed with craft and subtlety more fully than the rest, would strike out, whenever released, for some “just-a-minute,” or estaminet, not too far off, nor yet too near, and there lie perdus, lest the Company Orderly Sergeant warn them for some new liturgy. This defensive policy did not lighten the work of their brethren.

After four days of their labours as sumpter mules, or muleteers, the company would plod back for another four days of duty in trenches, come out yet more universally tired at their end, and drift back to rest-billets, out of ordinary shellfire, for their sixteen days or so of “divisional rest.” Here their work was really lighter, but still it was work and not rest. It did not wholly wind up in most of the men the spring that had run down while they were in the line. And then the division would go again into the line, and the old cycle be worked through once more. So most of the privates were tired the whole of the time; sometimes to the point of torment, sometimes much less, but always more or less tired.

===Chinese===

且把在前沿堑壕与支援堑壕里度过的头八天，当作一个师十六天阵地值勤轮回的开端。此后四天，疲惫的人们将充作预备队，仍在敌军炮火之下，只是不在堑壕里；多半待在倾颓房屋的地窖中。但这些并不是休憩的时光。每个昼或夜，人人都得回到他们撤离的那些堑壕跑上一趟或几趟，扛着食物、水或弹药，送上堑壕里的那三个连去；或者趁着夜色的掩护，牵一头驮骡越野走到前线附近的某个地点。黑夜里牵着满载的骡子，走过那片铁丝网与堑壕纵横交错、令人晕头转向的荒地，这本身就是活计；待它跌倒了，在一团陈年带刺铁丝中间惊得乱蹬乱扭，要把它重新弄站起来，那更是相当吃重的活计。

在这些往返的间隙，大多数人躺在地窖的稻草里，或者有气无力地在宅子外头一瘸一拐地蹭来蹭去，那模样像极了踢完一场硬仗似的足球赛、结结实实挨了许多狠铲、赛后又着了凉的男孩们，浑身僵痛。他们爬进爬出宿营地，像晚秋的蜜蜂，有气无力地刮掉衣裳上那八天镀上去的一层泥，饭后清理随身小刀时，带着圣经里那些长老们的倦怠——对那些长老，蚱蜢已成了重担。少数精壮的角色，凭着比旁人装备得更充足的狡黠与心机，一得释放便直奔某家「一分钟小店」（just-a-minute），也就是法国乡间小酒馆（estaminet），路程不远不近，到了便潜伏不出，免得连值班军士（Company Orderly Sergeant）点了名，把他们派上什么新的日课。这一防御性方针，并没有减轻他们弟兄们的活计。

当满四天驮骡、或满四天赶骡人之后，连队会再步履沉重地回去当四天堑壕值勤，期满出来时更是人人都疲，然后漂回寻常炮火之外的休整宿营地去，过他们那十六天上下的「师级休整」。在那里，活计确实轻了，但仍是活计，不是休息。它并不能把大多数人在阵地里走空了的那根发条，重新上满。然后这个师便再度开进阵地，旧的轮回再从头经历一遍。于是大多数列兵自始至终都疲着；有时疲到苦刑的地步，有时轻得多，但总是或多或少地疲着。

### IV / 四

===Original===

Many, of course, lost health and drifted “down the line,” as it was called, to the base, where work might be light, but much of the company rather more blighting than any work to the spirit. Hither, to all the divisional base depots and into the ultimate dust-hole or sink that was called “Base Details,” there gravitated most of the walking wreckage and wastage, physical and moral, of active warfare: convalescent, sick and wounded from hospital, men found too old or too young for trench work, broken-nerved men smuggled out of the way before disaster should come, and malingerers triumphant and chuckling, or only semi-successful, suspect, and tediously overacting.

There was the good man fretting and raging to get back to his friends and the fight, away from this tainted backwater in which the swelling flotillas of the unfit and the unwilling were left to rot at their moorings. There was the pallid and bent London clerk, faintly disguised in khaki but too blind to fight, now working furiously fifteen hours each day of his seven-day week in the orderly room﻿—no Sunday here, no Saturday afternoon﻿—for pure love of international right. There was the dugout, the Grenadier Guards sergeant-major of sixty, the handsome and melancholy old boy, a Victorian survivor into our little vulgar age, with a careful and dignified manner and mighty memories of a radiant past in London, when all parades, for a good-conduct-man well up in his drill, were over by half-past ten in the morning and he had a permanent midnight pass into barracks and so could act as a super at one of the theatres every night except when doing a guard, and see life and move among genius and beauty, making good money. Oh, yes, he had acted with Irving and Booth, and lived the life, and heard the chimes at midnight.
