### II / 二
===Original===

They had been travelling for a week, and had quite definitely decided that they had nothing whatever in common. As they stood there, lost and desolate on the grimy platform of the Finland station, this same thought must have been paramount in their minds: “Thank God we shan’t have to talk to one another any longer. Whatever else may happen in this strange place that at least we’re spared.” They were probably quite unconscious of the contrast they presented, unconscious because, at this time, young Bohun never, I should imagine, visualised himself as anything more definite than absolutely “right,” and Lawrence simply never thought about himself at all. But they were perfectly aware of their mutual dissatisfaction, although they were of course absolutely polite. I heard of it afterwards from both sides, and I will say quite frankly that my sympathy was all with Lawrence. Young Bohun can have been no fun as a travelling companion at that time. If you had looked at him there standing on the Finland station platform and staring haughtily about for porters you must have thought him the most self-satisfied of mortals. “That fellow wants kicking,” you would have said. Good-looking, thin, tall, large black eyes, black eyelashes, clean and neat and “right” at the end of his journey as he had been at the beginning of it, just foreign-looking enough with his black hair and pallor to make him interesting﻿—he was certainly arresting. But it was the self-satisfaction that would have struck anyone. And he had reason; he was at that very moment experiencing the most triumphant moment of his life.
He was only twenty-three, and was already as it seemed to the youthfully limited circle of his vision, famous. Before the war he had been, as he quite frankly admitted to myself and all his friends, nothing but ambitious. “Of course I edited the *Granta* for a year,” he would say, “and I don’t think I did it badly.﻿ ﻿… But that wasn’t very much.”
No, it really wasn’t a great deal, and we couldn’t tell him that it was. He had always intended, however, to be a great man; the *Granta* was simply a stepping-stone. He was already, during his second year at Cambridge, casting about as to the best way to penetrate, swiftly and securely, the fastnesses of London journalism. Then the war came, and he had an impulse of perfectly honest and selfless patriotism﻿ ﻿…

他们已经同行了一个星期，并且十分明确地断定彼此毫无共同之处。此刻他们站在芬兰站肮脏的月台上，迷茫而凄凉，心里想必都转着同一个念头：“感谢上帝，咱们再不用彼此说话了。这个古怪地方还要出什么乱子随它去，至少这一样是免了。”他们多半全然不觉得自己合起来是多么鲜明的一对——不觉得，因为那时的博亨，我想，从没把自己想象成比绝对“正确”更具体的东西，而劳伦斯则压根不想自己。可他们对彼此的不满心里透亮，虽然礼数上自然一丝不差。这事我后来从两边都听说了，坦白讲，我的同情全在劳伦斯一边。那时的小博亨做旅伴，准不是个乐子。你只要看见他站在芬兰站的月台上，傲然四顾找搬运夫，一定当他是最自满的凡人。“那家伙欠踹，”你会这样说。英俊，瘦高，黑色的大眼睛，黑睫毛，一路下来同启程时一样干净、齐整、“正确”，黑头发加苍白脸，那点外国味恰到好处地叫人觉得有趣——他确实是惹眼的。可谁都会先挨上那股自满。他有他的理由：此刻他正经历着一生最得胜的时刻。
他只有二十三岁，而且，以他那年轻人有限的视野看，已经成名。战前，正如他对我、对所有朋友坦白承认的，他一无所有，只有野心。“当然，我编过一年《格兰塔》，”他会说，“而且我不认为编得坏。……可那也不算什么。”
是，那确实算不得什么，我们也照实不能说它算什么。然而他一向打定主意要做个大人物；《格兰塔》不过一块垫脚石。在剑桥的第二年，他已经在盘算怎样又快又稳地打进伦敦新闻界的堡垒。随后战争来了，他起了一阵完全诚实、无私的爱国冲动……

, not quite selfless perhaps, because he certainly saw himself as a mighty hero, winning V.C.’s and saving forlorn hopes, finally received by his native village under an archway of flags and mottoes (the local postmaster, who had never treated him very properly, would make the speech of welcome). The reality did him some good, but not very much, because when he had been in France only a fortnight he was gassed and sent home with a weak heart. His heart remained weak, which made him interesting to women and allowed time for his poetry. He was given an easy post in the Foreign Office and, in the autumn of 1916 he published *Discipline: Sonnets and Poems*. This appeared at a very fortunate moment, when the more serious of British idealists were searching for signs of a general improvement, through the stress of war, of poor humanity.﻿ ﻿… “Thank God, there are our young poets,” they said.
The little book had excellent notices in the papers, and one poem in especial “How God spoke to Jones at Breakfast-time” was selected for especial praise because of its admirable realism and force. One paper said that the British breakfast-table lived in that poem “in all its tiniest most insignificant details,” as no breakfast-table, save possibly that of Major Pendennis at the beginning of *Pendennis* has lived before. One paper said, “Mr. Bohun merits that much-abused word ‘genius.’ ”
The young author carried these notices about with him and I have seen them all. But there was more than this. Bohun had been for the last four years cultivating Russian. He had been led into this through a real, genuine interest. He read the novelists and set himself to learn the Russian language. That, as anyone who has tried it will know is no easy business, but Henry Bohun was no fool, and the Russian refugee who taught him was no fool. After Henry’s return from France he continued his lessons, and by the spring of 1916 he could read easily, write fairly, and speak atrociously. He then adopted Russia, an easy thing to do, because his supposed mastery of the language gave him a tremendous advantage over his friends. “I assure you that’s not so,” he would say. “You can’t judge Chekhov till you’ve read him in the original. Wait till you can read him in Russian.” “No, I don’t think the Russian characters are like that,” he would declare.

也许不十分无私，因为他分明看见自己是个大英雄，赢得维多利亚十字勋章，挽回绝望的战局，末了由家乡的父老在旗子和标语的拱门下迎接（一直没把他当回事的本地邮政局长将致欢迎辞）。现实给了他一点好处，但不多，因为他到法国才两个星期就中了毒气，心脏带着毛病被送回了家。他的心脏一直没好利索，这叫他在女人眼里有趣起来，也给他的诗留出了工夫。他在外交部得了个清闲职位，1916年秋天出版了《纪律：十四行诗与诗歌》。它出的时机极好，英国理想主义者中比较严肃的那一派，正苦于战事，四处搜寻人性普遍好转的迹象。……“感谢上帝，还有我们的青年诗人，”他们说。
这本小书在报上得了极好的评论，特别有一首《上帝如何在早餐时对琼斯说话》，因它那可赞的写实和力度被挑出来特别表扬。一家报纸说，英国的早餐桌活在那首诗里，“连最细小、最不起眼的细节都全了”，自古没有哪张早餐桌这样活过，也许《潘登尼斯》开头潘登尼斯少校那张除外。另一家报纸说：“博亨先生当得起那个用滥了的词——‘天才’。”
年轻的作者把这些评论随身带着，我全都看过。还不止这些。博亨四年来一直在修俄文。领他进门的，是一份真真切切的兴趣。他读小说家们，下决心学俄语。谁试过谁知道，那不是桩容易事，可亨利·博亨不是傻子，教他的那位俄国流亡者也不是。亨利从法国回来后继续上课，到1916年春，他读已经轻松，写还过得去，说得一塌糊涂。于是他收养了俄国，这容易得很，因为他那想象中的语言优势叫他大大压过朋友们一头。“我向你保证不是那样的，”他会说，“契诃夫你没读原文就评不了。等你读得懂俄文再说。”“不，我认为俄国人不是那个样子，”他会宣布。

“It’s a queer thing, but you’d almost think I had some Russian blood in me﻿ ﻿… I sympathise so.” He followed closely the books that emphasised the more sentimental side of the Russian character, being of course grossly sentimental himself at heart. He saw Russia glittering with fire and colour, and Russians, large, warm, and simple, willing to be patronised, eagerly confessing their sins, rushing forward to make him happy, entertaining him forever and ever with a free and glorious hospitality.
“I really think I do understand Russia,” he would say modestly. He said it to me when he had been in Russia two days.
Then, in addition to the success of his poems and the general interest that he himself aroused the final ambition of his young heart was realised. The Foreign Office decided to send him to Petrograd to help in the great work of British propaganda.
He sailed from Newcastle on December 2, 1916.﻿ ﻿…

“怪事，你几乎要以为我身上有俄国血统……我太同情他们了。”他紧追那些渲染俄国人性格中伤感一面的书，自己骨子里当然也是伤感得一塌糊涂。他看见俄国火光与彩色闪亮，俄国人高大、热心、单纯，乐意受人抬举，急切地忏悔自己的罪，抢着来叫他高兴，用自由而豪爽的殷勤招待他，永世无穷。
“我真觉得我懂俄国，”他会谦虚地说。到俄国的第三天，他就对我说了这句话。
随后，除了诗集的成功和他本人引起的普遍兴趣，他这颗年轻的心最后一个抱负也实现了。外交部决定派他去彼得格勒，协助英国宣传的大业。
他于1916年12月2日自纽卡斯尔启航。……
