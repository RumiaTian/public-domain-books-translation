### VI / 六
===Original===

I must speak, for a moment, of myself. Throughout the autumn and winter of 1914 and the spring and summer of 1915 I was with the Russian Red Cross on the Polish and Galician fronts. During the summer and early autumn of 1915 I shared with the Ninth Army the retreat through Galicia. Never very strong physically, owing to a lameness of the left hip from which I have suffered from birth, the difficulties of the retreat and the loss of my two greatest friends gave opportunities to my arch-enemy Sciatica to do what he wished with me, and in October 1915 I was forced to leave the Front and return to Petrograd. I was an invalid throughout the whole of that winter, and only gradually during the spring of 1916 was able to pull myself back to an old shadow of my former vigour and energy. I saw that I would never be good for the Front again, but I minded that the less now in that the events of the summer of 1915 had left me without heart or desire, the merest spectator of life, passive and, I cynically believed, indifferent. I was nothing to anyone, nor was anyone anything to me. The desire of my heart had slipped like a laughing ghost away from my ken﻿—men of my slow warmth and cautious suspicion do not easily admit a new guest.﻿ ﻿…
Moreover during this spring of 1916 Petrograd, against my knowledge, wove webs about my feet. I had never shared the common belief that Moscow was the only town in Russia. I had always known that Petrograd had its own grace and beauty, but it was not until, sore and sick at heart, lonely and bitter against fate, haunted always by the face and laughter of one whom I would never see again, I wandered about the canals and quays and deserted byways of the city that I began to understand its spirit. I took, to the derision of my few friends, two tumbledown rooms on Pilot’s Island, at the far end of Ekateringofsky Prospect. Here amongst tangled grass, old, deserted boats, stranded, ruined cottages and abraided piers, I hung above the sea.

我得说一会儿我自己。1914年秋冬和1915年春夏，我随俄国红十字会待在波兰和加利西亚前线。1915年夏末初秋，我又同第九集团军一道经历了加利西亚大撤退。我身体向来不强，先天左胯的残疾让我吃了一辈子苦，撤退的艰辛加上两位至友的丧生，给我的死敌坐骨神经痛大开了方便之门，它爱拿我怎样就怎样，1915年10月我被迫离开前线，回到彼得格勒。那一整个冬天我是个病人，直到1916年春才慢慢拽回来几分旧日精力的影子。我明白自己再上也顶不了用了，可我也不那么在意了，因为1915年夏天的那些事已把我掏得没了心气、没了欲望，只剩一个生活的旁观者，被动的，而且——我犬儒地相信——冷漠的。我对谁都不是什么，谁对我也不是什么。我心中的愿望像个笑着的鬼影，溜出了我的视野——我这种温吞而多疑的人，是不轻易再放新客进门的。……
再说，1916年这个春天，彼得格勒背着我，在我脚下结起了网。我从不信莫斯科是俄国唯一的城那种通说。我一向知道彼得格勒自有它的风致和美，可直到我心痛身虚、孤单而怨命、永远被一张再不能见的脸和一阵笑声纠缠着，在这座城的运河、河岸和僻巷里乱走时，我才开始懂它的魂。我不顾寥寥几个朋友的嗤笑，在皮洛特岛上、叶卡捷琳戈夫斯基大街的尽头，赁下了两间破屋。这里，乱草丛中，废旧的老船，搁浅的破屋，剥蚀的木桩之间，我悬在海的上空。

Not indeed the sea of my Glebeshire memories; this was a sluggish, tideless sea, but in the winter one sheet of ice, stretching far beyond the barrier of the eye, catching into its frosted heart every colour of the sky and air, the lights of the town, the lamps of imprisoned barges, the moon, the sun, the stars, the purple sunsets, and the strange, mysterious lights that flash from the shadows of the hovering snow-clouds. My rooms were desolate perhaps, bare boards with holes, an old cracked mirror, a stove, a bookcase, a photograph, and a sketch of Rafiel Cove. My friends looked and shivered; I, staring from my window on to the entrance into the waterways of the city, felt that any magic might come out of that strange desolation and silence. A shadow like the sweeping of the wing of a great bird would hover above the ice; a bell from some boat would ring, then the church bells of the city would answer it; the shadow would pass and the moon would rise, deep gold, and lie hard and sharp against the thick, impending air; the shadow would pass and the stars come out, breaking with an almost audible crackle through the stuff of the sky﻿ ﻿… and only five minutes away the shop-lights were glittering, the *isvostchicks* crying to clear the road, the tram-bells clanging, the boys shouting the news. Around and about me marvellous silence.﻿ ﻿…
In the early autumn of 1916 I met at a dinner-party Nicolai Leontievitch Markovitch. In the course of a conversation I informed him that I had been for a year with the Ninth Army in Galicia, and he then asked me whether I had met his wife’s uncle Alexei Petrovitch Semyonov, who was also with the Ninth Army. It happened that I had known Alexei Petrovitch very well and the sound of his name brought back to me so vividly events and persons with whom we had both been connected that I had difficulty in controlling my sudden emotion. Markovitch invited me to his house. He lived, he told me, with his wife in a flat in the Anglisky Prospect; his sister-in-law and another of his wife’s uncles, a brother of Alexei Petrovitch, also lived with them. I said that I would be very glad to come.
It is impossible to describe how deeply, in the days that followed, I struggled against the attraction that this invitation presented to me.

自然不是我记忆里格勒本郡的海；这是一片滞缓的、无潮的海，可一到冬天便是一整幅冰，铺到目力的界桩之外，把它霜结的心窝里收进天空和空气的每一种颜色，全城的灯火，被困驳船的灯，月亮，太阳，星星，紫色的落日，还有从盘旋雪云的暗影里闪出的那些古怪、神秘的光。我的房间也许荒凉，光地板带着窟窿，一面裂纹的旧镜子，一只炉子，一只书架，一张照片和一幅拉菲尔小湾的素描。朋友们看了打寒噤；我却望着窗外这座城市水路的入海口，觉得任何魔法都可能从那奇怪的荒凉和寂静里出来。一道影子，像大鸟掠过的翅，会悬在冰上；哪条船上一口钟响，城里的教堂钟便应它；影子过去，月亮升起来，深金色，又硬又利地贴在浓重的、低垂的空气上；影子过去，星星出来，几乎带着听得见的脆响，挣破天的布面……而五分钟外，铺子的灯正亮，马车夫喊着开道，电车铃铛响，孩子们喊着号外。我的四周，是奇妙的寂静。……
1916年初秋，我在一次晚宴上认识了尼古拉·列昂季那维奇·马尔科维奇。谈话间我告诉他，我随第九集团军在加利西亚待过一年，他于是问我有没有见过他妻子的舅舅阿列克谢·彼得罗维奇·谢苗诺夫，那人也在第九集团军。恰好我同阿列克谢·彼得罗维奇熟得很，他的名字一响，把我们两人都连着的人和事便栩栩地涌回来，我竟一时按捺不住突来的激动。马尔科维奇邀我去他家。他说他同妻子住在英国大街的一套公寓里；他妻子的妹妹、还有他妻子的另一位舅舅，阿列克谢·彼得罗维奇的弟弟，也同住。我说我很乐意去。
随后那些日子里，我怎样同这份邀请的吸引力搏斗，深到什么地步，没法形容。

I had succeeded during all these months in avoiding any contact with the incidents or characters of the preceding year. I had written no letters and had received none; I had resolutely avoided meeting any members of my old Atriad when they came to the town.
But now I succumbed. Perhaps something of my old vitality and curiosity was already creeping back into my bones, perhaps time was already dimming my memories﻿—at any rate, on an evening early in October I paid my call. Alexei Petrovitch was not present; he was on the Galician front, in Tarnople. I found Markovitch, his wife Vera Michailovna, his sister-in-law Nina Michailovna, his wife’s uncle Ivan Petrovitch and a young man Boris Nicolaievitch Grogoff. Markovitch himself was a thin, loose, untidy man with pale yellow hair thinning on top, a ragged, pale beard, a nose with a tendency to redden at any sudden insult or unkind word and an expression perpetually anxious.
Vera Michailovna on the other hand was a fine young woman, and it must have been the first thought of all who met them as to why she had married him. She gave an impression of great strength; her figure tall and her bosom full, her dark eyes large and clear. She had black hair, a vast quantity of it, piled upon her head. Her face was finely moulded, her lips strong, red, sharply marked. She looked like a woman who had already made up her mind upon all things in life and could face them all. Her expression was often stern and almost insolently scornful, but also she could be tender, and her heart would shine from her eyes. She moved slowly and gracefully, and quite without self-consciousness.
A strange contrast was her sister, Nina Michailovna, a girl still, it seemed, in childhood, pretty, with brown hair, laughing eyes, and a trembling mouth that seemed ever on the edge of laughter. Her body was soft and plump; she had lovely hands, of which she was obviously very proud. Vera dressed sternly, often in black, with a soft white collar, almost like a nurse or nun. Nina was always in gay colours; she wore clothes, as it seemed to me, in very bad taste, colours clashing, strange bows and ribbons and lace that had nothing to do with the dress to which they were attached. She was always eating sweets, laughed a great deal, had a shrill piercing voice, and was never still. Ivan Petrovitch, the uncle, was very different from my Semyonov.

这几个月里，我成功地避开了上一年的一切人事。信我没写，也没收到；老伙伴们进城来，我一概躲开不见。
可这一回我屈服了。兴许我那点旧日的活力和好奇已经悄悄回到骨头里，兴许时间已经在把我的回忆磨淡——总之，十月初的一个傍晚，我登门造访了。阿列克谢·彼得罗维奇不在；他在加利西亚前线，在塔尔诺波尔。我见到马尔科维奇、他的妻子薇拉·米哈伊洛夫娜、他妻子的妹妹妮娜·米哈伊洛夫娜、他妻子的舅舅伊万·彼得罗维奇，还有一位年轻人，鲍里斯·尼古拉那维奇·格罗戈夫。马尔科维奇本人瘦弱、邋遢，淡黄的头发顶上见稀，一部参差的浅色胡子，一个受一点突然的冒犯或恶话就要红起来的鼻子，一脸常驻的焦急。
薇拉·米哈伊洛夫娜正相反，是位出众的少妇，见了他们俩的人，头一个念头想必都是：她怎么会嫁给他。她给人一种极有力量的印象；身量高，胸脯丰满，深色的眼睛又大又亮。她一头黑发，多得很，堆在头顶。脸型端正，嘴唇厚实、红润、轮廓分明。她看上去像一个对生活里的一切都已拿定主意、而且样样对付得了的女人。她的神情时常严厉，几乎近于放肆的轻蔑，可她也能温柔，那时她的心就从眼睛里照出来。她行动缓慢而优美，全无自我意识。
同她形成古怪对照的是她妹妹妮娜·米哈伊洛夫娜，一个仿佛还停在童年里的姑娘，俊俏，棕色头发，爱笑的眼睛，一张永远像要笑出声的、哆嗦着的嘴。她身子软而丰腴；一双手很好看，她显然也极为之得意。薇拉穿得素严，常穿黑，配软白领，几乎像护士或修女。妮娜永远一身艳色；她的衣裳照我看品位极坏，颜色打架，古怪的蝴蝶结、缎带和花边，同它们所附的裙子毫不相干。她总在吃糖，笑个不停，嗓音又尖又亮，一刻不得安生。舅舅伊万·彼得罗维奇同我那位谢苗诺夫则是另一路。

He was short, fat, and dressed with great neatness and taste. He had a short black moustache, a head nearly bald, and a round chubby face with small smiling eyes. He was a *chinovnik*, and held his position in some Government office with great pride and solemnity. It was his chief aim, I found, to be considered cosmopolitan, and when he discovered the feeble quality of my French he insisted in speaking always to me in his strange confused English, a language quite of his own, with sudden startling phrases which he had “snatched” as he expressed it from Shakespeare and the Bible. He was the kindest soul alive, and all he asked was that he should be left alone and that no one should quarrel with him. He confided to me that he hated quarrels, and that it was an eternal sorrow to him that the Russian people should enjoy so greatly that pastime. I discovered that he was terrified of his brother, Alexei, and at that I was not surprised. His weakness was that he was inpenetrably stupid, and it was quite impossible to make him understand anything that was not immediately in line with his own experiences﻿—unusual obtuseness in a Russian. He was vain about his clothes, especially about his shoes, which he had always made in London; he was sentimental and very easily hurt.
Very different again was the young man Boris Nicolaievitch Grogoff. No relation of the family, he seemed to spend most of his time in the Markovitch flat. A handsome young man, strongly built, with a head of untidy curly yellow hair, blue eyes, high cheek bones, long hands with which he was forever gesticulating. Grogoff was an internationalist Socialist and expressed his opinions at the top of his voice whenever he could find an occasion. He would sit for hours staring moodily at the floor, or glaring fiercely upon the company. Then suddenly he would burst out, walking about, flinging up his arms, shouting. I saw at once that Markovitch did not like him and that he despised Markovitch. He did not seem to me a very wise young man, but I liked his energy, his kindness, sudden generosities, and honesty. I could not see his reason for being so much in this company.
During the autumn of 1916 I spent more and more time with the Markovitches. I cannot tell you what was exactly the reason. Vera Michailovna perhaps, although let no one imagine that I fell in love with her or ever thought of doing so.

他矮胖，衣裳穿得极其整洁考究。黑黑的小胡子，脑袋几乎秃顶，一张圆滚滚的胖脸，笑眯眯的小眼睛。他是公务员，以极大的骄傲和庄严守着他的政府差事。我发现他的头号志愿是让人当他见多识广的世界公民，等他发现我那法语不顶用，他就坚持永远用他那套稀奇古怪、混杂不清的英语同我说话——那完全是他自造的一门语言，时不时蹦出几句他——用他的话说——从莎士比亚和《圣经》里“捞”来的惊人之语。他是天底下最好心的灵魂，全部所求无非别搅扰他、别同他吵。他向我吐露，他恨吵架，俄国人民那么爱这门消遣，是他永恒的悲哀。我发现他怕他哥哥阿列克谢，这我不奇怪。他的短处是愚钝得刀枪不入，凡是不能同他自身经验直接挂钩的事，就休想叫他明白——这在俄国人里是少见的心钝。他讲究衣裳，尤其讲究鞋，他的鞋永远在伦敦定做；他伤感，极易受伤。
再一个截然不同的是年轻人鲍里斯·尼古拉那维奇·格罗戈夫。他不是这家人的亲戚，工夫倒像大半耗在马尔科维奇家。俊朗的青年，身板结实，一头乱蓬蓬的黄卷发，蓝眼睛，高颧骨，一双长手永远在比划。格罗戈夫是国际派社会党人，一逮着机会就放开最大嗓门发表主张。他会几个钟头阴沉沉瞪着地板，或恶狠狠扫视满座。随后忽然爆发，来回走，抡胳膊，喊。我立刻看出马尔科维奇不喜欢他，他也看不起马尔科维奇。我看他不算个多聪明的青年，可我喜欢他的那股劲、他的和气、突如其来的慷慨和诚实。我看不出他凭什么老泡在这一伙里。
1916年整个秋天，我在马尔科维奇家消磨的时光越来越多。我说不出确切的缘故。兴许是薇拉·米哈伊洛夫娜吧——尽管谁也别以为我爱上了她，或起过那样的念头。

No, my time for that was over. But I felt from the first that she was a fine, understanding creature, that she sympathised with me without pitying me, that she would be a good and loyal friend, and that I, on my side could give her comprehension and fidelity. They made me feel at home with them; there had been as yet no house in Petrograd whither I could go easily and without ceremony, which I could leave at any moment that I wished. Soon they did not notice whether I were there or no; they continued their ordinary lives and Nina, to whom I was old, plain, and feeble, treated me with a friendly indifference that did not hurt as it might have done in England. Boris Grogoff patronised and laughed at me, but would give me anything in the way of help, property, or opinions, did I need it. I was in fact by Christmas time a member of the family. They nicknamed me “Durdles,” after many jokes about my surname and reminiscences of *Edwin Drood* (my Russian name was Ivan Andreievitch). We had merry times in spite of the troubles and distresses now crowding upon Russia.
And now I come to the first of the links in my story. It was with this family that Henry Bohun was to lodge.

不，我的那个年代过去了。可我头一回就觉出，她是个出色的、善解人的生灵，她同情我而不可怜我，她会是一位忠实的挚友，而我这一边，也能给她理解和忠诚。他们叫我在他们跟前不拘形迹；那时彼得格勒还没有第二户人家，我能这样随来随去、要走就走。很快他们就不留意我在不在；他们照过他们的日子，妮娜——在她眼里我又老、又丑、又弱——拿一种友好的淡然待我，那淡然若在英格兰是会伤人的，在这儿却不。鲍里斯·格罗戈夫抬举我、笑话我，可我需要时，人力、财物、主意，他什么都肯给。事实上，到圣诞前后，我已是这一家的人了。他们给我起了绰号“杜杜”，先拿我的姓氏打了一堆趣，又想起《埃德温·德鲁德之谜》（我的俄国名字是伊万·安德烈耶维奇）。尽管烦恼和苦难这时正朝俄国蜂拥而来，我们过的是快活日子。
现在，我来到我故事的第一个环扣。亨利·博亨要寄宿的，正是这一家。
