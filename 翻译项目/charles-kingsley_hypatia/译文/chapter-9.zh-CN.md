## IX / IX

### The Snapping of the Bow / 弓弦的崩断

===Original===
Cyril heard Philammon's story and Hypatia's message with a quiet smile, and then dismissed the youth to an afternoon of labour in the city, commanding him to mention no word of what had happened, and to come to him that evening and receive his order when he should have had time to think over the matter. So forth Philammon went with his companions, through lanes and alleys hideous with filth and poverty, compulsory idleness and native sin. Fearfully real and practical it all was; but he saw it all dimly as in a dream. Before his eyes one face was shining; in his ears one silvery voice was ringing. … "He is a monk, and knows no better. …" True! And how should he know better? How could he tell how much more there was to know, in that great new universe, in such a cranny whereof his life had till now been past? He had heard but one side already. What if there were two sides? Had he not a right—that is, was it not proper, fair, prudent, that he should hear both, and then judge?

Cyril had hardly, perhaps, done wisely for the youth in sending him out about the practical drudgery of benevolence, before deciding for him what was his duty with regard to Hypatia's invitation. He had not calculated on the new thoughts which were tormenting the young monk; perhaps they would have been unintelligible to him had he known of them. Cyril had been bred up under the most stern dogmatic training, in those vast monastic establishments, which had arisen amid the neighbouring saltpetre quarries of Nitria, where thousands toiled in voluntary poverty and starvation at vast bakeries, dyeries, brick-fields, tailors' shops, carpenters' yards, and expended the profits of their labour, not on themselves, for they had need of nothing, but on churches, hospitals, and alms. Educated in that world of practical industrial production as well as of religious exercise, which by its proximity to the great city accustomed monks to that world which they despised; entangled from boyhood in the intrigues of his fierce and ambitious uncle Theophilus, Cyril had succeeded him in the patriarchate of Alexandria without having felt a doubt, and stood free to throw his fiery energy and clear practical intellect into the cause of the Church without scruple, even, where necessary, without pity. How could such a man sympathise with the poor boy of twenty, suddenly dragged forth from the quiet cavern-shadow of the Laura into the full blaze and roar of the world's noonday? He, too, was cloister-bred. But the busy and fanatic atmosphere of Nitria, where every nerve of soul and body was kept on a lifelong artificial strain, without rest, without simplicity, without human affection, was utterly antipodal to the government of the remote and needy, though no less industrious commonwealths of Coenobites, who dotted the lonely mountain-glens, far up into the heart of the Nubian desert. In such a one Philammon had received, from a venerable man, a mother's sympathy as well as a father's care; and now he yearned for the encouragement of a gentle voice, for the greeting of a kindly eye, and was lonely and sick at heart. … And still Hypatia's voice haunted his ears, like a strain of music, and would not die away. That lofty enthusiasm, so sweet and modest in its grandeur—that tone of pity—in one so lovely it could not be called contempt—for the many; that delicious phantom of being an elect spirit, unlike the crowd. … "And am I altogether like the crowd?" said Philammon to himself, as he staggered along under the weight of a groaning fever-patient. "Can there be found no fitter work for me than this, which any porter from the quay might do as well? Am I not somewhat wasted on such toil as this? Have I not an intellect, a taste, a reason? I could appreciate what she said.—Why should not my faculties be educated? Why am I only to be shut out from knowledge? There is a Christian Gnosis as well as a heathen one. What was permissible to Clement"—he had nearly said to Origen, but checked himself on the edge of heresy—"is surely lawful for me! Is not my very craving for knowledge a sign that I am capable of it? Surely my sphere is the study rather than the street!"

区利罗（Cyril）静静地微笑着，听菲拉蒙（Philammon）讲了事情的经过，也收下了希帕蒂娅（Hypatia）的口信，随后便打发这少年去城里做一下午的劳作，嘱咐他对所发生的事只字不提，傍晚再到他那里去领命——他要等有了斟酌的时间再作决断。于是菲拉蒙便跟着同伴们出去了，穿过一条条污秽贫穷的小巷与弄堂，那里满是被迫的游手好闲和与生俱来的罪孽。这一切可怕地真实、可怕地切近；可他却像在梦中一样，看什么都模模糊糊。他眼前只有一张脸在发光；他耳中只有一个银铃般的声音在回响。……「他是个修士，不懂得更好的。」是啊！他又怎能懂得更好的呢？他怎知道，在那个崭新的浩瀚宇宙里——他此前的半生便是在它某个偏僻的角落度过的——还有多少是他所不知道的呢？他至今只听过一面之词。倘若还有另一面呢？他难道没有权利——也就是说，让他兼听两面、然后再作判断，岂不是妥当的、公允的、审慎的么？

区利罗在替这少年决断他对希帕蒂娅邀约该尽何等本分之前，先打发他出去干些慈善的粗活，这做法恐怕未必明智。他没有料到那些正折磨着这年轻修士的新念头；就算知道，恐怕也难以理解。区利罗是在最严苛的教条训练下调教出来的，那是在尼特拉（Nitria）附近的硝石采石场间兴起的庞大修院群落里，那里有数以千计的人自愿守贫挨饿，在巨大的面包房、染坊、砖窑、裁缝铺、木匠作坊里劳作，而把劳作所得的利润，不花在自己身上——因为他们一无所需——而是花在教会、医院和施舍上。他既在那兼有实际工业生产与宗教操行的世界里受过教育——那地方紧邻大城，使修士们习于他们所鄙视的那个世俗世界；又自幼卷入他那位凶狠而野心勃勃的叔父提奥菲鲁斯（Theophilus）的种种阴谋——区利罗便这样毫无疑虑地继其叔之后登上了亚历山大城的宗主教之位，得以毫无顾忌、必要时甚至毫无怜悯地，将他炽烈的精力与清明务实的才智投入教会的事业。这样的人，又怎能体恤一个二十岁的可怜少年，突然被从劳拉那幽静的洞穴阴影里拖出来，抛入这世界正午的烈焰与喧嚣之中呢？他自己也是在修院里长大的。可尼特拉那忙碌而狂热的空气——在那里，灵魂与肉体的每一根神经都被终身置于一种人为的紧张之中，没有安息，没有纯朴，没有人的情爱——与那些遥远而贫苦、却同样勤勉的独居修士团体（Coenobites）的治理之道，截然相反；那些团体星罗棋布地散落在孤独的山间幽谷里，远至努比亚（Nubian）沙漠腹地。菲拉蒙所来的，正是那样一个团体，他从一位可敬的长者那里，既受了慈母般的体恤，也得着严父般的照拂；而此刻，他渴望一个温柔声音的鼓励，渴望一道和善目光的致意，他孤独，他心里发闷。……而希帕蒂娅的声音仍如一缕乐音，萦绕在他耳畔，挥之不去。那崇高的热忱，在其壮美之中如此甘美而谦逊；那怜悯的声调——出自如此美丽的人口中，便算不得轻蔑——对芸芸众生的怜悯；那甘美的幻影：他是个蒙选的灵，与芸芸众生不同。……「我当真与他们全然相同么？」菲拉蒙背负着一个呻吟的热病病人、踉跄前行时，自言自语道。「难道就找不出比这更适合我的活计么？这活儿码头上的任何一个苦力都能干得一样好。我干这种苦工，岂不是有些浪费？我难道没有理智、没有趣味、没有理性么？她说的那些话，我是能够领会的。——为什么我的心智就不该受到栽培？为什么唯独我要被拒于知识之外？既有异教的灵知（Gnosis），也有基督教的灵知啊。对克莱门特（Clement）所许可的——」他险些说出对奥利金（Origen）所许可的，却在异端的边缘硬生生收住了口——「对我自然也是合法的！我对知识的这份渴慕本身，岂不正是我能承受它的凭据么？我的天地分明是书房，而不是街头！」

And then his fellow-labourers—he could not deny it to himself—began to grow less venerable in his eyes. Let him try as he might to forget the old priest's grumblings and detractions, the fact was before him. The men were coarse, fierce, noisy … so different from her! Their talk seemed mere gossip—scandalous too, and hard-judging, most of it; about that man's private ambition, and that woman's proud looks; and who had stayed for the Eucharist the Sunday before, and who had gone out after the sermon; and how the majority who did not stay could possibly dare to go, and how the minority who did not go could possibly dare to stay. … Endless suspicions, sneers, complaints … what did they care for the eternal glories and the beatific vision? Their one test for all men and things, from the patriarch to the prefect, seemed to be—did he or it advance the cause of the Church?—which Philammon soon discovered to mean their own cause, their influence, their self-glorification. And the poor boy, as his faculty for faultfinding quickened under the influence of theirs, seemed to see under the humble stock-phrases in which they talked of their labours of love, and the future reward of their present humiliations, a deep and hardly-bidden pride, a faith in their own infallibility, a contemptuous impatience of every man, however venerable, who differed from their party on any, the slightest, matter. They spoke with sneers of Augustine's Latinising tendencies, and with open execrations of Chrysostom, as the vilest and most impious of schismatics; and, for aught Philammon knew, they were right enough. But when they talked of wars and desolation past and impending, without a word of pity for the slain and ruined, as a just judgment of Heaven upon heretics and heathens; when they argued over the awful struggle for power which, as he gathered from their words, was even then pending between the Emperor and the Count of Africa, as if it contained but one question of interest to them—would Cyril, and they as his bodyguard, gain or lose power in Alexandria? and lastly, when at some mention of Orestes, and of Hypatia as his counsellor, they broke out into open imprecations of God's curse, and comforted themselves with the prospect of everlasting torment for both; he shuddered and asked himself involuntarily—were these the ministers of a Gospel?—were these the fruits of Christ's Spirit? … And a whisper thrilled through the inmost depth of his soul—"Is there a Gospel? Is there a Spirit of Christ? Would not their fruits be different from these?"

Faint, and low, and distant, was that whisper, like the mutter of an earthquake miles below the soil. And yet, like the earthquake-roll, it had in that one moment jarred every belief, and hope, and memory of his being each a hair's-breadth from its place. … Only one hair's-breadth. But that was enough; his whole inward and outward world changed shape, and cracked at every joint. What if it were to fall in pieces? His brain reeled with the thought. He doubted his own identity. The very light of heaven had altered its hue. Was the firm ground on which he stood after all no solid reality, but a fragile shell which covered—what?

The nightmare vanished, and he breathed once more. What a strange dream! The sun and the exertion must have made him giddy. He would forget all about it.

于是他的同伴们——他无法对自己否认——在他眼中开始显得不那么可敬了。任他怎样努力忘却那老神父的牢骚与毁谤，事实毕竟摆在那里。这些人粗鄙、凶悍、聒噪……与她是何等不同！他们的谈话似乎不过是些闲言碎语——而且大多是在搬弄是非、苛责论断：论到某人的私心野心，某妇人的傲慢神色；论到上个主日谁留下来领了圣餐（Eucharist）、谁听了讲道便走了出去；又论到那些未曾留下的大多数怎敢擅自离去，而那些未曾离去的少数又怎敢擅自留下。……无尽的猜疑、讥诮、怨言……他们哪里在乎那永恒的荣耀与那荣福的异象（beatific vision）呢？他们衡量一切人事的唯一尺子——从宗主教到总督（prefect）——似乎是：他或它是否促进了教会的事业？——而菲拉蒙很快便发现，那无非是他们自己的事业、他们的影响力、他们自我荣耀的工具。这可怜的少年，在他人的影响下，自己找岔的本事也跟着灵敏起来，他似乎看透了：在他们用以谈论自己爱心劳作、谈论自己今日屈辱将来必得赏赐的那一套谦卑套话底下，藏着一种根深蒂固、难以抑制的骄傲，一种对自己绝无谬误的信心，一种对凡与本党有丝毫分歧之人——无论他多么可敬——所抱的轻蔑的不耐。他们带着讥诮谈论奥古斯丁（Augustine）的拉丁化倾向，又公然咒诅屈梭多模（Chrysostom），视其为最卑劣、最不敬的分裂派；就菲拉蒙所知，他们也许并不算错。可当他们谈论已成过往的与将要临到的战乱与荒芜，对那些被杀被灭之人竟无半句怜悯，只当作上天对异端与异教徒的公义审判；当他们议论着——据他听话中之意——当时正在皇帝与非洲伯爵（the Count of Africa）之间酝酿的那场争夺权力的可怕斗争，仿佛其中对他们唯有唯一值得关心的问题：区利罗、以及身为他扈从的他们，在亚历山大城里是得势还是失势？最后，当他们一提到俄瑞斯忒斯（Orestes）、提到希帕蒂娅是他的谋臣，便公然祈求上帝的咒诅降在他们身上，又以二人将受永刑的前景来自慰——这时他战栗起来，不由自主地问自己：这些人，难道就是那福音的执事么？——这些，难道就是基督之灵（Christ's Spirit）所结的果子么？……有一个低语，颤栗着掠过他灵魂的最深处——「果真有福音么？果真有基督之灵么？它的果子，岂不该与这些全然不同么？」

那低语，又微弱，又低沉，又遥远，仿佛在地下数英里深处隆隆作响的地震。然而，正如地震的滚动，它就在那一瞬间，把他每一样信念、每一样盼望、每一样记忆，都从原处震开了一发之差。……只是一发之差。可这便够了；他整个内在与外在的世界都变了形，处处接缝都裂开了口。倘若它整个坍塌下来，又将如何？一念及此，他脑中一阵眩晕。他怀疑起自己的本相来。连天上的光，也变了颜色。他所站立的那块实地，难道终究不是什么坚固的现实，而不过是一层薄薄的脆壳，其下覆盖着——什么？

那场噩梦消散了，他重新喘过气来。好一场怪梦！必是日头和劳顿叫他的头发了晕。他要把这一切统统忘掉。

Weary with labour, and still wearier with thought, he returned that evening, longing and yet dreading to be permitted to speak with Hypatia. He half hoped at moments that Cyril might think him too weak for it; and the next, all his pride and daring, not to say his faith and hope, spurred him on. Might he but face the terrible enchantress, and rebuke her to her face! And yet so lovely, so noble as she looked! Could he speak to her, except in tones of gentle warning, pity, counsel, entreaty? Might he not convert her—save her? Glorious thought! to win such a soul to the true cause! To be able to show, as the firstfruits of his mission, the very champion of heathendom! It was worth while to have lived only to do that; and having done it, to die.

The archbishop's lodgings, when he entered them, were in a state of ferment even greater than usual. Groups of monks, priests, parabolani, and citizens rich and poor, were banging about the courtyard, talking earnestly and angrily. A large party of monks fresh from Nitria, with ragged hair and beards, and the peculiar expression of countenance which fanatics of all creeds acquire, fierce and yet abject, self-conscious and yet ungoverned, silly and yet sly, with features coarsened and degraded by continual fasting and self-torture, prudishly shrouded from head to heel in their long ragged gowns, were gesticulating wildly and loudly, and calling on their more peaceable companions, in no measured terms, to revenge some insult offered to the Church.

"What is all this about, reverend sir?" asked Philammon of Peter the Reader, who made his appearance at that moment in the quadrangle, walking with great strides, like the soul of Agamemnon across the meads of Asphodel, and apparently beside himself with rage.

"Ah! you here? You may go tomorrow, young fool! The patriarch can't talk to you. Why should he? Some people have a great deal too much notice taken of them, in my opinion. Yes; you may go. If your head is not turned already, you may go and get it turned tomorrow. We shall see whether he who exalts himself is not abased, before all is over!" And he was striding away, when Philammon, at the risk of an explosion, stopped him.

"His holiness commanded me to see him, sir, before—"

Peter turned on him in a fury. "Fool! will you dare to intrude your fantastical dreams on him at such a moment as this?"

"He commanded me to see him," said Philammon, with the true soldierlike discipline of a monk; "and see him I will in spite of any man. I believe in my heart you wish to keep me from his counsels and his blessing."

Peter looked at him for a moment with a right wicked expression, and then, to the youth's astonishment, struck him full in the face, and yelled for help.

If the blow had been given by Pambo in the Laura a week before, Philammon would have borne it. But from that man, and coming unexpectedly as the finishing stroke to all his disappointment and disgust, it was intolerable; and in an instant Peter's long legs were sprawling on the pavement, while he bellowed like a bull for all the monks in Nitria.
