## VIII / VIII

The Kaaba of Solitude / 孤寂之克尔白

===Original===
Disappointed, distraught, diseased—worsted by the Jesuits, excommunicated, crossed in love—but with an eternal glint of sunshine in his breast to open and light up new paths before him, Khalid, after the fatal episode, makes away from Baalbek. He suddenly disappears. But where he lays his staff, where he spends his months of solitude, neither Shakib nor our old friend the sandomancer can say. Somewhither he still is, indeed; for though he fell in a swoon as he saw Najma on her caparisoned palfrey and the decorated Excellency coming up along side of her, he was revived soon after and persuaded to return home. But on the following morning, our Scribe tells us, coming up to the booth, he finds neither Khalid there, nor any of his few worldly belongings. We, however, have formed a theory of our own, based on certain of his writings in the K.L. MS., about his mysterious levitation; and we believe he is now somewhither whittling arrows for a coming combat. In the Lebanon mountains perhaps. But we must not dog him like the Jesuits. Rather let us reverence the privacy of man, the sacredness of his religious retreat. For no matter where he is in the flesh, we are metaphysically certain of his existence. And instead of filling up this Chapter with the bitter bickerings of life and the wickedness and machination of those in power, let us consecrate it to the divine peace and beauty of Nature. Of a number of Chapters in the Book of Khalid on this subject, we choose the one entitled, My Native Terraces, or Spring in Syria, symbolising the natural succession to Khalid’s Winter of destiny. In it are signal manifestations of the triumph of the soul over the diseases and adversities and sorrows of mortal life. Indeed, here is an example of faith and power and love which we reckon sublime.

失望，烦乱，抱病——被耶稣会士（Jesuits）所败，遭绝罚，爱情又受挫——然而胸中却永有一点不灭的阳光，为他开辟、照亮新的前路；那场致命的变故之后，哈立德离开了巴勒贝克（Baalbek）。他忽然销声匿迹。然而他把行杖搁在了何处，他那数月的孤寂又是在哪里打发的，沙基布说不上来，我们那位老朋友沙占师（sandomancer）也说不上来。他确乎仍在于某处；因为虽说他在看见娜吉玛（Najma）骑着那匹披挂华美的鞍马、那位勋章满胸的阁下挨到她身旁之时，当场昏厥倒地，可不久便被救醒，并被劝回了家。然而翌日清晨——据我们的抄写人所言——他走到那间茅棚一看，既不见哈立德在那里，也不见他那寥寥几件随身之物。不过，关于他这一桩神秘的「飞升」，我们依据赫迪夫图书馆手稿（K.L. MS.）中他的若干文字，自成了一套说法；我们相信，他此刻正在某处，为一场即将到来的争斗削制箭矢。或许就在黎巴嫩（Lebanon）群山之中。但我们不可像耶稣会士那样跟踪盯梢他。与其如此，不如去敬重一个人的隐私、敬重他宗教退修的圣洁。因为无论他的肉身身在何方，我们在形而上学的意义上确知他的存在。本章我们不愿再充斥人生的尖酸龃龉、当权者的邪恶与诡谋，而宁愿把它奉献给大自然那神圣的宁静与美。在《哈立德之书》论此主题的若干章节中，我们选取题为《我乡的梯田，或叙利亚之春》的一章，象征着哈立德命运的严冬之后自然的更迭。其中有灵魂战胜尘世疾病的灾厄与悲愁的显著见证。诚然，这里有一例信心、力量与爱，我们奉之为崇高。

“The inhabitants of my terraces and terrace walls,” we translate, “dressed in their Sunday best, are in the doorways lounging or peeping idly through their windows. And why not? It is Spring, and to these delicate, sweet little creatures, Spring is the one Sunday of the year. Have they not hugged the damp, dark earth long enough? Hidden from the wrath of Winter, have they not squatted patiently round the primitive, smokeless fire of the mystic depths? And now, the rain having partly extinguished the inner, hidden flame, they come out to bask in the sun, and drink deeply of the ambrosial air. They come, almost slain with thirst, to the Mother Fountain. They come out to worship at the shrine of the sweet-souled, God-absorbed Rabia of Attar. In their bright, glowing faces what a delectable message from the under world of romance and enchantment! Their lips are red with the kisses of love, in whose alembics, intangible, unseen, the dark and damp of the earth are translated into warmth and colour and shade. Ay, these dear little children, unfolding their soft green scrolls and reading aloud such odes on Modesty and Beauty, are as inspiring as the star-crowned night. And every chink in my terrace walls seems to breathe a message of sweetness and light and love.

“Know you not the anecdote about the enchanting Goddess Rabia, as related by Attar in his *Biographies of Sufi Mystics and Saints*? Here it is. Rabia was asked if she hated the devil, and she replied, ‘No.’ Asked again why, she said, ‘Being absorbed in love, I have no time to hate.’ Now, all the inhabitants of my terraces and fields seem to echo this sublime sentiment of their Goddess. The air and sunshine, nay, the very rocks are imbued with it. See, how the fissures in the boulders yonder seem to sympathise with the gaps in the terrace walls: the cyclamen leaves in the one are salaaming the cyclamen flowers in the other. O, these terraces would have delighted the heart of the American naturalist Thoreau. He could not have desired stone walls with more gaps in them. But mind you, these are not dark, ugly, hollow, hopeless chinks. Behind every one of them lurks a mystery. Far back in the niches I can see the busts of the poets who wrote the poems which these beautiful wild flowers are reading to me. Yes, the authors are dead, and what I behold now are the flowers of their amours. These are the offspring of their embraces, the crystallised dew of their love. Yes, this one single, simple act of love brings forth an infinite variety of flowers to celebrate the death of the finite outward shape and the eternal essence of life perennial. In complete surrender lies the divineness of things eternal. This is the keynote of the Oriental mystic poets. And I incline to the belief that they of all bards have sung best the song of love. In rambling through the fields with these beautiful children of the terraces, I know not what draws me to al-Farid, the one erotic-mystic poet of Arabia, whose interminable rhymes have a perennial charm. Perhaps such lines as these—

‘All that is fair is fairer when she rises,

					All that is sweet is sweeter when she is here;

					And every form of beauty she surprises

					With one brief word she whispers in its ear:

‘Thy wondrous charms, O let them not deceive thee;

					They are but borrowed from her for a while;

					Thine outward guise and loveliness would grieve thee,

					If in thine inmost soul she did not smile.

‘All colours, forms, into each other merging,

					Are woven on her Loom of Unity;

					For she alone is One in All diverging,

					And she alone is absolute and free.’

“Now, I will bring you to a scene most curiously suggestive. Behold that little knot of daisies pressing around the alone anemone beneath the spreading leaves of the colocasia. Here is a rout at the Countess Casiacole’s, and these are the debutantes crowding around the Celebrity of the day. But would they do so if they were sensible of their own worth, if they knew that their idol, flaunting the crimson crown of popularity, had no more, and perhaps less, of the pure essence of life than any of them? But let Celebrity stand there and enjoy her hour; tomorrow the Ploughman will come.

「我那梯田与梯田石墙上的居民们，」我们迻译道，「穿着他们最讲究的礼拜日盛装，倚在门口闲坐，或从窗子里懒懒地向外窥望。这有何不可呢？春日已至，对这些娇嫩甘美的小生灵而言，春天便是一年里唯一的礼拜日。他们拥抱那潮湿幽暗的泥土，难道还不够长久么？为躲避冬日的烈怒，他们耐着性子蹲踞在那幽秘深处的原始无烟之火周围，难道还不够久么？如今，雨水已将那内里隐藏的火焰灭去了一半，他们便出来晒晒太阳，痛饮那芳醇的空气。他们几乎渴得要死地来到那「母亲之泉」（Mother Fountain）。他们出来，在阿塔尔（Attar）笔下那位灵魂甘美、全心归主的女神拉比娅（Rabia）的神龛前朝拜。他们光彩焕发的脸庞上，传递着何等动人的消息——来自那浪漫与迷人的地下世界！他们的双唇因爱的亲吻而殷红；在那无形的、看不见的爱之蒸馏器里，大地的阴暗与潮湿被转化成了温暖、色彩与光影。是啊，这些亲爱的小孩儿们，展开他们柔软的绿色卷轴，高声诵读着关于谦逊与美的颂歌，其鼓舞人心，一如那群星为冠的夜。而我梯田石墙上的每一条裂缝，似乎都在呼吸着一条甘美、光明与爱的消息。

「你们可知道那段关于迷人的女神拉比娅的轶事么？那是阿塔尔在他的《苏菲神秘主义者与圣徒列传》（*Biographies of Sufi Mystics and Saints*）里所讲述的。便是这一段：有人问拉比娅是否恨魔鬼，她答道：『不恨。』又问她为何不恨，她说：『我既已沉醉于爱，便无暇去恨。』如今，我那梯田与田野上的一切居民，似乎都在回应他们女神这句崇高的情思。空气与阳光——不，连岩石本身——都浸透了它。看哪，那边巨石上的裂缝，似乎正与我梯田石墙上的缺口同病相怜：这一处的仙客来叶子，正向着那一处的仙客来花朵行色兰（salaam）之礼。啊，这些梯田定能叫美国博物学家梭罗（Thoreau）满心欢喜。他再不能指望有比这缺口更多的石墙了。可是请留意，这些都不是阴暗、丑陋、空洞、无望的裂缝。它们每一个的背后都潜藏着一个秘密。在那些壁龛的深处，我能看见那些诗人的半身像——正是他们写下了这些美丽的野花此刻正为我诵读的诗篇。是的，作者们已经死了，而我此刻所看见的，是他们风月之恋的花。这些是他们拥抱的子嗣，是他们爱之甘露的结晶。是的，这单独而简单的一桩爱的举动，便生出无穷无尽的花朵，来庆贺那有限的、外在形相的死亡，以及生命那永恒不灭的本质。在彻底的降服之中，方有永恒之物的神性。这便是东方神秘诗人的主调。而我倾向于相信，在所有歌者之中，正是他们把爱之歌唱得最美。与这些梯田的美丽孩子们一道漫游于田野之间，我不知是什么把我引向了法里德（al-Farid）——阿拉伯唯一一位「欲爱与神秘」交融的诗人，他那连绵不绝的韵脚有一种四季常新的魅力。或许是这般诗句——

『一切美者，因她升起而愈美，

　　　　一切甘甜，因她在场而愈甘；
　　　　而她所遇见的每一种美的形相，
　　　　她只消一句低语，便附在其耳旁：

『「啊，莫让你那奇妙的魅力欺骗了你；
　　　　它们不过是向她暂借了片刻；
　　　　你那外在的仪容与可爱反将令你悲伤，
　　　　若她在你灵魂的最深处不曾微笑。」

『一切色彩、形相，彼此交融，
　　　　都织在她那「太一之机」（Loom of Unity）上；
　　　　因为在一切分殊之中，唯她是一，
　　　　唯她绝对，唯她自由。』

「如今，我要带你去看一处最为奇巧、发人深省的景象。看那一小簇雏菊，挤在芋头（colocasia）舒展的叶子底下、围着那株孤零零的银莲花。这是卡西亚科伯爵夫人（Countess Casiacole）府上的一场盛会，这些便是簇拥着当日名媛的初入社交界的新人。可是，倘若它们自觉身价，倘若它们知道它们那位偶像——那炫耀着人缘之朱红冠冕的——其生命之纯粹精华并不比它们之中任何一个更多、甚至更少，它们还会如此簇拥么？可是且让那位名媛站在那里、享受她的时辰罢；明天，那犁田人就要来了。

===Original===
“The sage, with its spikes of greyish blue flowers, its fibrous, velvety leaves, its strong, pungent perfume, which is neither squandered nor repressed, is the stoic of my native terraces. It responds generously to the personal touch, and serves the Lebanonese, rich and poor alike, with a little luxury. Ay, who of us, wandering on foreign strands, does not remember the warm foot-bath, perfumed with sage leaves, his mother used to give him before going to bed? Our dear mothers!”—And here, Khalid goes in raptures and tears about his sorry experience in Baalbek and the anguish and sorrow of his poor mother. “But while I stand,” he continues, “let me be like the sage, a live-oak among shrubs, indifferent as the oak or pine to the winds and storms. And as the sun is setting, find you no solace in the thought, O Khalid, that some angel herb-gatherer will preserve the perfume in your leaves, to refresh therewith in other worlds your dear poor mother?

“My native terraces are rich with faith and love, luxuriant with the life divine and the wondrous symbols thereof. And the grass here is not cut and trimmed as in the artificial gardens and the cold dull lawns of city folk, whose love for Nature is either an experiment, a sport, a business, or a fad. ‘A dilettantism in Nature is barren and unworthy,’ says Emerson. But of all the lovers of Nature, the children are the least dilettanteish. And every day here I see a proof of this. Behold them wading to their knees in that lusty grass, hunting the classic lotus with which to deck their olive branches for the high mass and ceremony of Palm Sunday. But alas, my lusty grass and my beautiful wild flowers do not enjoy the morning of Spring. Here, the ploughman comes, carrying his long plough and goad on his shoulder, and with him his wife lugging the yoke and his boy leading the oxen. Alas, the sun shall not set on these bright, glowing, green terraces, whose walls are very ramparts of flowers. There, the boy with his scythe is paving the way for his father’s plough; the grass is mowed and given to the oxen as a bribe to do the ugly business. And all for the sake of the ugly mulberries, which are cultivated for the ugly silkworms. Come, let us to the heath, where the hiss of the scythe and the ‘ho-back’ and ‘oho’ of the ploughman are not heard.

“But let us swing from the road. Come, the hedges of Nature are not as impassable as the hedges of man. Through these scrub oaks and wild pears, between this tangle of thickets, over the clematis and blackberry bush—and here we are under the pines, the lofty and majestic pines. How different are these natural hedges, growing in wild disorder, from the ugly cactus fences with which my neighbours choose to shut in their homes, and even their souls. But my business now is not with them. There are my friends the children again gathering the pine-needles of last summer for lighting the fire of the silkworm nursery. And down that narrow footpath, meandering around the boulders and disappearing among the thickets, see what big loads of brushwood are moving towards us. Beneath them my swarthy and hardy peasants are plodding up the hill asweat and athirst. When I first descended to the wadi, one such load of brushwood emerging suddenly from behind a cliff surprised and frightened me. But soon I was reminded of the moving forest in *Macbeth*. The man bowed beneath the load was hidden from view, and the boy directly behind was sweating under a load as big as that of his father. ‘*Awafy!*’ (Allah give you strength), I said, greeting them. ‘And increase of health to you,’ they replied. I then asked the boy how far down do they have to go for their brushwood, and laying down his load on a stone to rest, he points below, saying, ‘Here, near the river.’ But this ‘Here, near the river’ is more than four hours’ walk from the village.—Allah preserve you in your strength, my Brothers. And they pass along, plodding slowly under their overshadowing burdens. A hard-hearted Naturalist, who goes so deep into Nature as to be far from the vital core even as the dilettante, might not have any sympathy to throw away on such occasions. But of what good is the love of Nature that consists only in classification and dissection? I carry no notebook with me when I go down the wadi or out into the fields. I am content if I bring back a few impressions of some reassuring instance of faith, a few pictures, and an armful of wild flowers and odoriferous shrubs. Let the learned manual maker concern himself with the facts; he is content with jotting down in his notebook the names and lineage of every insect and every herb.

“But Man? What is he to these scientific Naturalists? If they meet a stranger on the road, they pass him by, their eyes intent on the breviary of Nature, somewhat after the fashion of my priests, who are fond of praying in the open-air at sundown. No, I do not have to prove to my Brothers that my love of Nature is but second to my love of life. I am interested in my fellow men as in my fellow trees and flowers. ‘The beauty of Nature,’ Emerson again, ‘must always seem unreal and mocking until the landscape has human figures, that are as good as itself.’ And ’tis well, if they are but half as good. To me, the discovery of a woodman in the wadi were as pleasing as the discovery of a woodchuck or a woodswallow or a woodbine. For in the soul of the woodman is a song, I muse, as sweet as the rhythmic strains of the goldfinch, if it could be evoked. But the soul plodding up the hill under its heavy overshadowing burden, what breath has it left for song? The man bowed beneath the load, the soul bowed beneath the man! Alas, I seem to behold but moving burdens in my country. And yet, my swarthy and shrunken, but firm-fibred people plod along, content, patient, meek; and when they reach the summit of the hill with their crushing burdens, they still have breath enough to troll a favourite ditty or serenade the night.

‘I come to thee, O Night,

					I’m at thy feet;

					I can not see, O Night,

					But thy breath is sweet.’

“And so is the breath of the pines. Here, the air is surcharged with perfume. In it floats the aromatic soul of many a flower. But the perfume-soul of the pines seems to tower over all others, just as its material shape lifts its artistic head over the oak, the cercis, and the terabinth. And though tall and stately, my native pines are not forbidding. They are so pruned that the snags serve as a most convenient ladder. Such was my pleasure mounting for the green cones, the salted piñons of which are delicious. But I confess they seem to stick in the stomach as the pitch of the cones sticks on the hands. This, however, though it remains for days, works no evil; but the piñons in the stomach, and the stomach on the nerves—that is a different question.

“The only pines I have seen in the United States are those in front of Emerson’s house in Concord; but compared with my native trees, they are scrubby and mean. These pine parasols under which I lay me, forgiving and forgetting, are fit for the gods. And although closely planted, they grow and flourish without much ado. I have seen spots not exceeding a few hundred square feet holding over thirty trees, and withal stout and lusty and towering. Indeed, the floor of the Tent seems too narrow at times for its crowded guests; but beneath the surface there is room for every root, and over it, the sky is broad enough for all.

“Ah, the bewildering vistas through the variegated pillars, taking in a strip of sea here, a mountain peak there, have an air of enchantment from which no human formula can release a pilgrim-soul. They remind me—no; they can not remind me of anything more imposing. But when I was visiting the great Mosques of Cairo I was reminded of them. Yes, the pine forests are the great mosques of Nature. And for art-lovers, what perennial beauty of an antique art is here. These majestic pillars arched with foliage, propping a light-green ceiling, from which cones hang in pairs and in clusters, and through which curiously shaped clouds can be seen moving in a cerulean sky; and at night, instead of the clouds, the stars—the distant, twinkling, white and blue stars—what to these are the decorations in the ancient mosques? There, the baroques, the arabesques, the colourings gorgeous, are dead, at least inanimate; here, they palpitate with life. The moving, swelling, flaming, flowing life is mystically interwoven in the evergreen ceiling and the stately colonnades. Ay, even the horizon yonder, with its planets and constellations rising and setting ever, is a part of the ceiling decoration.

“Here in this grand Mosque of Nature, I read my own Koran. I, Khalid, a Bedouin in the desert of life, a vagabond on the highway of thought, I come to this glorious Mosque, the only place of worship open to me, to heal my broken soul in the perfumed atmosphere of its celestial vistas. The mihrabs here are not in this direction nor in that. But whereso one turns there are niches in which the living spirit of Allah is ever present. Here, then, I prostrate me and read a few Chapters of **my** Holy Book. After which I resign myself to my eternal Mother and the soft western breezes lull me asleep. Yea, and even like my poor brother Muslim sleeping on his hair-mat in a dark corner of his airy Mosque, I dream my dream of contentment and resignation and love.

“See the ploughman strutting home, his goad in his hand, his plough on his shoulder, as if he had done his duty. Allah be praised, the flowers in the terrace-walls are secure. That is why, I believe, my American brother Thoreau liked walls with many gaps in them. The sweet wild daughters of Spring can live therein their natural life without being molested by the scythe or the plough. Allah be praised a hundred times and one.”

===Chinese===
「鼠尾草，以其灰蓝色花穗、纤维质的天鹅绒般叶片、其浓烈辛锐而既不挥霍也不压抑的香气，是我乡梯田上的斯多葛派哲人。它对人的抚摸慷慨回应，并以一点小小的奢华，服务于黎巴嫩人——无论贫富。是啊，我们之中漂泊异乡的人，谁不记得母亲在就寝前为咱们备的那盆漂着鼠尾草叶的温热足浴呢？我们亲爱的母亲们！」——说到此处，哈立德为他自己在巴勒贝克那不堪回首的遭遇、为他那可怜母亲的痛苦与悲伤，一时神驰泪下。「然而我既站立着，」他续道，「便让我像那鼠尾草罢，做灌木中的常青橡，如橡如松，对风雨无动于衷。且趁夕阳西下，想一想这层意思，哈立德啊，岂不令人宽慰：某位采草天使将把你叶中的香气保存下来，好在别的世界里，以此为你那亲爱而可怜的母亲提神？

「我乡的梯田，丰饶于信心与爱，繁茂于神圣的生命及其奇妙象征。这里的草，并不像那些城里人的人工花园与冷清呆板的草坪那样地被修剪裁割——那些城里人对自然的所谓爱，不过是一场试验、一种消遣、一桩买卖、或一时的怪癖。『对自然抱着半吊子的态度，是贫瘠而可鄙的，』爱默生（Emerson）如是说。然而在一切自然的爱好者之中，孩子们最少半吊子气。这一点我每天在这里都看得到明证。看他们没膝地蹚过那片茁壮的青草，寻觅那古典的莲花，用来装饰他们的橄榄枝，好迎接棕枝主日（Palm Sunday）那盛大的弥撒与典礼。然而可叹，我那茁壮的青草、我那美丽的野花，却享受不到春日的早晨。这里，犁田人来了，肩上扛着长犁与刺棍，身旁是他那扛着轭的妻子，还有他那个牵着牛的孩子。可叹，那阳光不会落在这片光彩焕发的绿色梯田之上——它们的石墙简直是用花筑成的城墙。那边，那男孩挥着他的镰刀，为他父亲的犁开路；青草被割倒，喂给了牛，作为它们去干那桩丑活的贿赂。而这一切，全是为了那丑陋的桑葚，而桑葚又是为那丑陋的蚕而种的。来罢，让我们到那荒原上去，那里听不到镰刀的嘶响，也听不到犁田人『嗬—驾』『哦—嗬』的吆喝。

「可让我们从这条道上荡开去罢。来，自然的藩篱并不像人为的藩篱那样不可逾越。穿过这些矮橡与野梨，钻过这片纠结的灌木丛，越过这些铁线莲与黑莓丛——我们便到了松林之下，到了那些高耸而庄严的松树之下。这些野生野长、乱中生序的天然藩篱，与我的邻居们用以圈禁他们的家园、甚至圈禁他们的灵魂的那种丑陋的仙人掌篱笆，有何等不同。但我此刻要说的不是他们。那边又有我的小朋友们，在捡拾去年夏天的松针，好为蚕房生火。而沿着那条窄窄的、绕过巨石、隐没于灌木丛中的小径，看哪，有好大一捆一捆的柴薪正朝我们这边移动。它们底下，是我那些黝黑而强健的农人，汗流浃背、口渴难当地向山上跋涉。我头一次下到那旱谷（wadi）时，有那样一大捆柴薪忽然从一座悬崖背后转出来，倒把我吓了一跳。但随即我便想起了《麦克白》（*Macbeth*）里那座移动的森林。那压在重担之下、弓着身子的汉子完全看不见，紧随其后的男孩，所背的担子与他父亲的一般大，也是大汗淋漓。『*Awafy!*（真主赐你力量！）』我这样向他们招呼道。『愿你康健日增。』他们答道。我于是问那男孩，他们要下到多远的地方才能打到柴；他把担子搁在一块石头上歇息，朝下面一指，说：『这儿，靠河那块儿。』然而这『这儿，靠河那块儿』，离村子足有四个多钟头的脚程。——真主在你们的力量中保佑你们，我的兄弟们。他们就这样缓缓地，在笼罩头顶的重担之下，向前跋涉而去。一位铁石心肠的博物学家，钻研自然钻得那样深，以至于远离了生机之核——与那半吊子并无二致——在这种场合或许并无同情可掷。但那种只由分类与解剖所构成的对自然的爱，又有何益处？我下旱谷或下田野时，并不携带笔记本。我若能带回几则关于某种令人宽慰的信心实例的印象、几幅图景、以及一抱野花与芳香的灌木，便已知足。让那位博学的「手册编纂者」去操心那些事实罢；他满足于在笔记本里草草记下每一只虫、每一株草的名字与谱系。

「可是人呢？在这些科学的博物学家眼中，人又算什么？倘若他们在路上遇见一个陌生人，他们径自走过，眼睛死盯着他们那本大自然的日课经——倒颇有几分像我的那些神父，他们喜欢在日落时的露天之下祈祷。不，我无须向我那班兄弟们证明：我对自然之爱，仅次于我对生命之爱。我对同类的兴趣，一如我对同类树木花朵的兴趣。『自然之美，』又是爱默生之言，『在风景里有了与之同样美好的人物之前，必定始终显得虚幻而嘲弄。』而倘若它们能有那一半的好，也就很好了。于我而言，在旱谷里发现一个樵夫，其乐正与发现一只旱獭、一只林燕、或一株忍冬相同。因为我冥想着，在那樵夫的灵魂里，藏着一支歌，倘若能被唤起，其甜美一如金雀那富有节奏的鸣啭。然而那在沉重的、笼罩头顶的重担之下向山上跋涉的灵魂，哪里还有气息留给了歌唱？那弓身于重担之下的，是人；而弓身于此人之下的，是灵魂！可叹，在我的国度里，我所见的似乎只是会移动的重担。然而我那些黝黑干瘦、却筋骨坚韧的人民，仍这般地跋涉向前——知足、忍耐、柔顺；待他们扛着那压死人的重担攀上山巅之时，他们竟还有足够的气息，高歌一支心爱的小调，或向黑夜献上一支小夜曲——

『我来到你这里，啊，黑夜，
　　　　我在你脚前；
　　　　我看不见，啊，黑夜，
　　　　但你的气息甘甜。』

「而松树的气息也是如此。此处，空气中饱含着香气。许多花的芳香之魂漂浮其中。然而那松树的香魂，似乎凌驾于众香之上，正如同它那有形之躯，把它的艺术之头，昂然地举在橡树、紫荆（cercis）与笃薷香（terabinth）之上。尽管我乡的松树高大庄严，却并不拒人。它们被修剪得那些残桩恰好成了一架最为便利的梯子。我当年之乐，便是攀缘其上去摘那绿色的松果，那盐渍的松子可谓美味。但我得承认，它们似乎粘在胃里，一如松果上的松脂粘在手上。然而这后者，虽能存留数日，却并无大碍；至于前者——松子之于胃，胃之于神经——那便另当别论了。

「我在合众国所见过的唯一的松树，乃是康科德（Concord）爱默生宅前的那几棵；但与我乡的树相比，它们既矮小又寒酸。我如今所卧的这些松树华盖之下，宽恕着、忘却着，它们简直配得上诸神。它们虽种得密，却无须怎样照料便生长繁茂。我见过不过几百平方英尺的一块地，竟能容下三十余株，且株株粗壮、茁壮、高耸。诚然，这座「帐篷」之地有时对其济济的宾客而言似乎过于狭窄；然而地面之下，每一条根都有它的余地，而地面之上，那片天空也足够为众株所共。

「啊，那些透过斑斓立柱望出去的令人目眩的远景——这边一带海，那边一座山峰——有一种迷人的气息，没有任何人为的程式能将一个朝圣者的灵魂从中解脱。它们令我想起——不；它们不能令我再想起任何更为恢宏之物了。然而，当我在开罗（Cairo）参观那些伟大的清真寺（Mosques）之时，我却由它们想起了那些松林。是的，松林便是大自然的大清真寺。对艺术爱好者而言，此处有一种古朴艺术的不朽之美。这些被繁叶拱成穹顶的庄严立柱，撑起一片浅绿色的藻井，松果成双成簇地自其上垂下，透过它能望见奇形怪状的云朵在湛蓝的天空中移动；而到了夜间，代云而起的，是群星——那遥远的、闪烁的、白色与蓝色的群星——古清真寺里的种种装饰，比起它们来又算得了什么呢？在那里，那些怪诞饰、阿拉伯式花纹、华丽的色彩，是死的，至少是无生命的；在这里，它们却因生命而悸动。那流动的、涨涌的、燃烧的、奔涌的生命，神秘地交织在那常绿的穹顶与庄严的柱廊之中。是啊，连那边那道地平线，连同其上升下落不息的行星与星座，也是这片藻井装饰的一部分。

「在这座大自然的大清真寺里，我诵读我自己的《古兰经》（Koran）。我，哈立德，生命沙漠中的一个贝都因人，思想大道上的一个流浪者，我来到这座光荣的清真寺——这是向我敞开的唯一的礼拜之所——好让它那天界远景的芬芳气息，治愈我破碎的灵魂。这里的米哈拉布（mihrabs，即壁龛）不在此一方向，也不在彼一方向。然而无论转向何方，都有真主（Allah）的活灵长存的壁龛。那么，就在这里，我俯伏叩拜，诵读几章**我自己的**圣典。之后，我把自己交托给我那永恒的母亲，让柔和的西风将我哄入梦乡。是啊，便如我那位可怜的穆斯林兄弟，在他那通风的清真寺的一处幽暗角落里，睡在他的鬃毛垫子上，我也做起我的梦——一场知足、顺从与爱的梦。

「看那犁田人昂首阔步地归家，一手执刺棍，一肩扛长犁，仿佛已尽了他的本分。赞美真主，梯田石墙里的花算是保住了。我想，这便是我那位美国兄弟梭罗之所以喜欢缺口多的石墙的缘故了。春日那些甘甜的野女儿，可以在其中过它们天然的生活，免遭镰刀与犁的侵扰。赞美真主，一百次又一遍。」
