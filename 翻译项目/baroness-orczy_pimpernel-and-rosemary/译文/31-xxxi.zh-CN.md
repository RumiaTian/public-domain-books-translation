## XXXI / 三十一
===Original===

Rosemary did not see Elza again that day. Just before dinner Rosa came with a short scribbled note from her. “Maurus is very restless,” it said; “I don’t like to leave him. Will you and dear Lord Tarkington forgive me if I don’t join you at dinner?”
The evening was dreary. Jasper said very little, and Rosemary felt thoroughly out of tune with him; he had a meek air about him that irritated her. Hers was not a nature to sympathize with remorse, and Jasper’s manner gave the idea that he regretted having forced her into a decision. So she gave curt answers when he spoke to her, and after dinner he retired into the smoking-room with the excuse that he had some business letters to write. She sat reading most of the evening, her nerves on edge, hearing all sorts of mysterious sounds through the apparent stillness of the house.
When Jasper came to say good night she felt sorry for him. He looked forlorn and miserable, and reason told Rosemary that he of all people ought not to be allowed to suffer through a situation that was none of his making. Poor Jasper! She, his wife, had dragged him, unwillingly enough, into this impasse wherein his quiet habits of a wealthy English gentleman were hopelessly perturbed and his outlook outraged at every point. So, after she had returned his last kiss and saw him going upstairs, slowly, dragging one step after another, almost like an old man, she ran after him and linked her arm in his, and gave him a tender and sympathetic smile. The look of gratitude which he gave her in return warmed her heart. Here, at least, was no divided duty. In a moment of pique﻿—it was nothing less than that﻿—she had linked her fate with Jasper Tarkington, accepted from him all the lavish gifts that wealth could buy, and which he so generously bestowed upon her. In exchange for that he only asked for her love; and if the love which he gave and demanded did not reach that sublime ideal of which Rosemary had once dreamed, at any rate it was loyal and ungrudging and she had no right to let her caprice stand in the way of his happiness.
It was perhaps strange that these thoughts should come to her at a moment when her whole soul was torn with a terrible sorrow and a racking anxiety; perhaps they came because on this very day she had made the greatest abdication of her will that she had ever done in all her life.

那天罗斯玛丽再没见到埃尔莎。晚饭前，罗莎送来她的一封短短的潦草便条。“毛罗斯很不安静，”上面写道，“我不忍离开他。晚饭不同你们一起用，请您和亲爱的塔金顿勋爵原谅。”
这个晚上乏味极了。贾斯珀话很少，罗斯玛丽觉得自己同他彻底格格不入；他身上那副温驯的神气叫她恼。她的天性不同情悔恨，而贾斯珀的态度让人觉得，他在后悔逼她做出决定。于是他一开口，她便简短地应一句；晚饭后他退进吸烟室，托辞要写几封事务信件。大半个晚上她坐着读书，神经绷着，在宅子表面的一片寂静里听出种种神秘的声响。
贾斯珀来道晚安时，她替他难过。他显得孤苦、凄惨；理智告诉罗斯玛丽，这个人尤其不该为一个完全不是他造成的局面受苦。可怜的贾斯珀！她，他的妻子，相当不情愿地把他拖进了这个绝境，害得他那位阔绰英国绅士的安静积习被搅得不得安宁，他的见识处处受到冒犯。于是，回过他最后一个吻、望着他上楼——一步一步拖着，几乎像个老人——之后，她追上去挽住他的手臂，给他一个温柔体贴的微笑。他回望她的那道感激，暖了她的心。至少在这里，没有两难的责任。在一时负气之下——不是别的，正是负气——她把自己的命运同贾斯珀·塔金顿系在了一起，接受了他慷慨赠予的、财富所能置办的一切丰厚礼物。作为交换，他只求她的爱；这份他给予并索取的爱，纵然够不上罗斯玛丽曾梦想的那种崇高理想，至少是忠诚而不吝啬的；她没有权利让自己的任性妨害他的幸福。
说来也许奇怪，这些念头偏偏在她整个灵魂被可怕的悲哀和折磨人的焦虑撕裂的时刻涌上来；也许正因为就在今天，她平生第一次最大地放弃了她的意志。

===Original===

She had always acted for herself, judged for herself, set herself a high standard of straight living and straight thinking, and lived up to it. Today she had left a decision which should have been hers in the hands of another. She knew that she had done right, but her pride was humiliated, and to soothe that pride she set herself a fresh standard of duty to Jasper and determined to live up to that.
But ever afterwards she turned away with a shudder from thoughts of this evening, when she probed the full depth of Jasper’s passion for her, and saw before her like a row of spectres the vision of an endless vista of years, during which every caress would mean for her an effort, and every kiss a lie.
The new standard of duty which she had set herself would be very difficult to live up to. She had never loved Jasper, only hoped that she might learn to love him one day, but on this fateful evening she realised that she might in time learn to hate him.
When at last she was alone she found herself unable to rest. Through the open window the sounds of the oncoming storm became more and more insistent. It was rolling in on the bosom of the clouds from over the mountains in the west. Already one or two vivid flashes of lighting had rent the sky, and now and then great gusts of wind swept across the valley and sent a soughing and whispering through the trees. The poplars bowed their crests, and the twisted branches of the old acacias shivered and cracked in the blast. It was insufferably hot, and there was a smell of sulphur in the air. Rosemary in a thin lace wrap could not succeed in keeping cool. She stood by the open window, longing for the storm to break in all its fury, so that she might be rid of this feeling of oppression which was so unendurable, because the storm, far or near, had gone on almost uninterruptedly for over twenty-four hours. Rosemary’s thoughts now were with Elza. She pictured to herself the unfortunate woman wrestling with a decision which either way must mean the breaking of her heart. Elza, who outwardly seemed just a soft, futile, pampered doll, with thoughts fixed on her menus and her servants, was a veritable heroine, strong and tenacious, proud without vanity, loving without weakness, the type that represented everything that was finest and best in a woman.

她向来自己行事，自己判断，给自己立下正直地生活、正直地思考的高标准，并且始终坚守。今天，她把本该属于自己的决定，留在了另一个人手里。她知道自己做得对，可她的自尊受了屈辱；为安抚这份自尊，她给自己立下一条对贾斯珀的新义务标准，并决意坚守它。
可后来每逢想起这个晚上，她都战栗着躲开——那个晚上，她探到了贾斯珀对她的激情的全部深度，眼前像一排幽灵般展开一幅无尽的岁月长卷：在那岁月里，每一次温存对她都将是一次费力，每一个吻都是一句谎。
她给自己立下的这条新义务标准，将非常难以坚守。她从未爱过贾斯珀，只希望有一天能学着爱他；可就在这个决定命运的晚上，她明白了：假以时日，她也许会学着恨他。
终于独自一人时，她发觉自己无法安歇。敞开的窗外，越来越近的暴风雨声一阵紧似一阵。它驮在云的胸脯上，从西面群山那边滚滚而来。已有一两道炫目的闪电撕裂天空，阵阵狂风不时扫过山谷，在树林里刮出一片萧萧飒飒。杨树俯首，老刺槐虬曲的枝条在狂风里发抖、爆响。天气热得难堪，空气里有一股硫磺味。罗斯玛丽裹着薄薄的蕾丝晨衣，怎么也凉不下来。她站在敞开的窗前，只盼暴风雨全力倾泻——好摆脱这难以忍受的压迫感；这暴风雨，无论远近，已经几乎不停歇地肆虐了二十四个钟头以上。此刻罗斯玛丽的心思在埃尔莎身上。她想象那不幸的女人正同一个决定搏斗——无论怎么定，都意味着心碎。埃尔莎，外表看去不过一个柔软、无用、娇惯的玩偶，心思全在菜单和仆人身上，其实是一位真正的女英雄：坚强，坚韧，骄傲而不虚荣，深情而不软弱；她那种类型，代表着女性身上一切最美好、最优秀的东西。

She was of the stuff that religious martyrs were made of in the past, and she would not come to a decision without a terrible struggle. If in the end her heart overruled the dictates of justice and of right, her remorse would be as devastating as her courage hitherto had been sublime.
If Elza had been a religious woman she would not have suffered nearly so cruelly. The pagan knows nothing of the comfort of prayer, of diving blindly from the rocks of care into the ocean of God’s love. And Elza was only a pagan from whom the thin veneer of Christianity laid on in early life had been rubbed off long ago. She would not now be on her knees, murmuring with heaven-born resignation: “Lord, not my will, but thine be done!” she would be fighting a tough battle, wrestling with her heart, castigating her tenderest feelings, fighting alone, unaided, unconsoled.
Poor, poor Elza! Rosemary, looking out into the storm, seemed to see the pretty round face distorted by grief, the big, childlike eyes gazing bewildered on the immensity of the puzzle which the Fates had set for her to solve. And while Rosemary gazed the storm became full of pictures, each lightning flash revealed a face. Elza! Philip, dark-eyed, enthusiastic, the idealist! Anna, gentle and resigned. Maurus, the man, the head of the family, the trunk of the tree weaker than its branches. And then Peter. Oh, Peter filled the night with his presence. There was Peter in flannels, a boy with bright eyes and curly head, fighting life’s battles with a cricket bat and a joke. Peter home on leave from that hell in Belgium, receiving from his king the supreme reward for an act of almost unequalled bravery, of which, in his boyish way, he would often look quite ashamed. And Peter that night in June, long ago. Peter’s strong arms round her shoulders. Peter’s impassioned words, vying in melody with the nightingale. Peter’s kiss that opened wide the portals of Heaven; and, lastly, Peter the mysterious, the subtle, unseen influence in whose wake strode sorrow and disaster. And the rumbling of the thunder brought back to Rosemary’s ears Jasper’s words of warning: “I only wish I had your belief in coincidences;” and “Ever since Peter’s arrival I have seen nothing but one calamity after another fall upon these wretched people here.”

她身上流的，是从前宗教殉道者的血；不经过一场可怕的搏斗，她不会做出决定。倘若到头来她的心压倒了正义与公理的指令，她的悔恨将同她迄今的英勇一样彻底。
埃尔莎若是个虔诚的教徒，就不会受这样酷烈的苦。异教徒不懂祈祷的慰藉，不懂从忧虑的岩石上盲目一跃、跳进上帝之爱的海洋。而埃尔莎不过是个异教徒——幼年涂上的那层薄薄的基督教漆，早就磨掉了。此刻她不会跪着，以天赐的顺从喃喃：“主啊，不要照我的意思，只要照你的意思！”她是在打一场硬仗：同自己的心角力，鞭挞自己最柔软的感情，孤身苦战，无人相助，无人可慰。
可怜、可怜的埃尔莎！罗斯玛丽望着风雨，仿佛看见那张漂亮的圆脸被悲愁扭歪，那双孩子气的大眼睛茫然凝视着命运们给她出的那道浩大的谜。罗斯玛丽望着望着，暴风雨里幻象丛生，每一道闪电照出一张脸。埃尔莎！菲利普，黑眼睛、满腔热忱的理想主义者！安娜，温柔而认命。毛罗斯，这个男人、一家之长，一棵树干比枝条还弱的树。然后是彼得。哦，彼得以他的存在充满了整个黑夜。彼得穿着法兰绒运动装，一个眼睛发亮、鬈发的少年，拿一根板球棒和一句笑话打人生的仗。彼得从比利时那个地狱回家休假，从国王手里接过那枚几乎是无可匹敌的英勇行为的最高奖赏——那份英勇，他常带着孩子气的样子，像是怪难为情。还有彼得，很久以前那个六月的夜晚。彼得有力的手臂搂着她的肩。彼得热烈的话语，同夜莺比着婉啭。彼得那一吻，洞开了天国之门；最后，是神秘的彼得，那个微妙无形的影响，悲伤和灾难紧随其后。隆隆的雷声把贾斯珀的警告送回罗斯玛丽耳边：“我真希望我也有你对巧合的信仰”；还有“自打彼得到来，我只看见一场接一场的灾祸落到这些不幸的人头上”。

And then that awful, awful indictment which she had been unable to refute: “Don’t you know that Peter Blakeney is a paid spy of the Romanian Government?” The thunder brought the echo of those terrible words. Louder and louder, for the storm was drawing nearer, and the echo of those awful words drowned the very sound of thunder.
All at once the storm broke in all its fury; there was a deafening crash and a flash of lightning so vivid that for the space of one second the garden stood revealed as if in broad daylight before Rosemary’s gaze, clear-cut in every detail, every tree, every leaf, every flower, every ripple upon the lake, each pebble upon the garden walk; and in that one second Rosemary had seen Peter standing on the gravel walk, not fifty yards from her window, and looking up at her﻿—gazing. She caught his eyes in that one flash. He was dressed in a dark suit, his cricketing cap was on his head. It had been an instant’s flash, but she had seen him, and he was gazing up her window. And their eyes had met in that one flash, right through the storm.
After that all was darkness, and though from time to time the night was rent by lightning flashes, Rosemary did not see Peter again. And when later on the storm subsided, and, wearied out, she went to bed and slept, she dreamt that all her suspicions of Peter had been proved to be wrong. She dreamt that she was a few years younger, that they were on the river together, in a punt, and that the nightingale was singing. She dreamt of the lapping of the water against the low-lying river bank, of the scent of meadowsweet, and of the honey-coloured moon that painted long lines of golden light upon the reeds. She dreamt that Peter kissed her, and that she was free to give him kiss for kiss.

还有那句她无法驳倒的、可怕的控诉：“你难道不知道，彼得·布莱克尼是罗马尼亚政府雇用的密探？”雷声把那几个可怕的字眼的回响送来，一声高过一声——暴风雨越来越近了，那几个字的回响盖过了雷声本身。
骤然间，暴风雨全力迸发了；一声震耳的炸响，一道炫目的闪电，有那么一秒钟，园子在罗斯玛丽眼前亮如白昼，纤毫毕现：每一棵树，每一片叶，每一朵花，湖上每一道涟漪，园径上每一粒石子；而就在那一秒钟里，罗斯玛丽看见彼得站在碎石小径上，离她的窗不到五十码，正仰头望她——凝望着。就在那一闪里，她撞上了他的眼睛。他穿一身深色衣裳，头上戴着板球帽。那不过一瞬的闪光，可她看见了他，看见他仰望着她的窗。就在那一闪里，穿过风雨，他们的目光相遇了。
随后是一片漆黑；尽管夜不时被闪电撕开，罗斯玛丽却再没看见彼得。后来暴风雨平息了，她倦极上床睡去，梦见她对彼得的一切怀疑都被证明是错的。她梦见自己年轻了几岁，他们一起在河上，一条平底船上，夜莺在歌唱。她梦见河水轻轻拍着低低的河岸，梦见绣线菊的香气，梦见蜜色的月亮在芦苇上绘出长条金色的光。她梦见彼得吻她，而她自由了，可以吻还他的吻。
