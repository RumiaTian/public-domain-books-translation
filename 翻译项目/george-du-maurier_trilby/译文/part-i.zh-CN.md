It was a fine, sunny, showery day in April.
The big studio window was open at the top, and let in a pleasant breeze from the northwest. Things were beginning to look shipshape at last. The big piano, a semi-grand by Broadwood, had arrived from England by “the Little Quickness” (*la Petite Vitesse*, as the goods trains are called in France), and lay, freshly tuned, alongside the eastern wall; on the wall opposite was a panoply of foils, masks, and boxing-gloves.
A trapeze, a knotted rope, and two parallel cords, supporting each a ring, depended from a huge beam in the ceiling. The walls were of the usual dull red, relieved by plaster casts of arms and legs and hands and feet; and Dante’s mask, and Michelangelo’s altorilievo of Leda and the swan, and a centaur and Lapith from the Elgin marbles﻿—on none of these had the dust as yet had time to settle.
There were also studies in oil from the nude; copies of Titian, Rembrandt, Velasquez, Rubens, Tintoret, Leonardo da Vinci﻿—none of the school of Botticelli, Mantegna, and Co.﻿—a firm whose merits had not as yet been revealed to the many.
Along the walls, at a great height, ran a broad shelf, on which were other casts in plaster, terra-cotta, imitation bronze; a little Theseus, a little Venus of Milo, a little discobolus; a little flayed man threatening high heaven (an act that seemed almost pardonable under the circumstances!); a lion and a boar by Barye; an anatomical figure of a horse with only one leg left and no ears; a horse’s head from the pediment of the Parthenon, earless also; and the bust of Clytie, with her beautiful low brow, her sweet wan gaze, and the ineffable forward shrug of her dear shoulders that makes her bosom a nest, a rest, a pillow, a refuge﻿—to be loved and desired forever by generation after generation of the sons of men.
Near the stove hung a gridiron, a frying-pan, a toasting-fork, and a pair of bellows. In an adjoining glazed corner cupboard were plates and glasses, black-handled knives, pewter spoons, and three-pronged steel forks; a salad-bowl, vinegar cruets, an oil-flask, two mustard-pots (English and French), and suchlike things﻿—all scrupulously clean. On the floor, which had been stained and waxed at considerable cost, lay two cheetah-skins and a large Persian praying-rug.

那是一个四月里晴雨相间的明媚日子。
画室巨大的窗户上端开着，迎纳着西北方向吹来的怡人微风。屋里总算开始有了井井有条的模样。那架宽木（Broadwood）牌小型三角大钢琴刚刚经由“慢速货运”（*la Petite Vitesse*，即法国对慢速货运列车的称呼）从英格兰运抵，新调了音，倚靠着东侧墙壁摆放；对面的墙壁上则挂满了花剑、面罩和拳击手套。
房顶粗大的横梁上垂下一根吊架、一根打了结的绳子，以及两条各系着一个吊环的平行绳索。墙壁漆成了惯常的暗红色，上面挂着手臂、腿部、手脚的石膏模型作为点缀；还有但丁的面模、米开朗基罗表现勒达与天鹅的高浮雕，以及埃尔金大理石雕中的半人马与拉皮斯人——这些物件上全都还没来得及落上灰尘。
此外还有一些人体油画习作；临摹提香、伦勃朗、委拉斯开兹、鲁本斯、丁托列托、列奥纳多·达·芬奇的画作——但没有一幅属于波提切利、曼特尼亚及其同侪的流派，这一派的妙处当时尚未向大众揭晓。
沿着高高的墙壁设有一圈宽阔的隔板架，上面陈列着其他石膏、赤陶和仿青铜的模型雕塑：一座小忒修斯像，一座小米洛的维纳斯像，一座小掷铁饼者像；一个剥皮人小雕像向苍天挥拳威胁（在那般境遇下，这种举动似乎完全可以原谅！）；巴里雕刻的一头狮子和一头野猪；一匹只剩一条腿且没有耳朵的马体解剖模型；帕特农神庙山墙上雕下的一只马头，同样没有耳朵；还有克吕提厄的半身胸像，有着美丽低平的额头、温柔憔悴的目光，以及肩头难以言喻的前耸姿态，使她的胸脯宛如一个巢穴、一处歇息地、一个枕头、一处避风港——注定要被世世代代的人类子孙永远爱慕与向往。
火炉旁挂着烤架、煎锅、烤面包叉和一副风箱。紧邻的带玻璃门的转角橱柜里摆着盘子和玻璃杯、黑柄刀、锡匙和三齿钢叉；还有沙拉碗、醋壶、油瓶、两个芥末罐（英式和法式各一）以及诸如此类的物件——全都收拾得一尘不染。地板花了大价钱上了色并打过蜡，上面铺着两张猎豹皮和一块宽大的波斯祈祷地毯。

One-half of it, however (under the trapeze and at the farthest end from the window, beyond the model throne), was covered with coarse matting, that one might fence or box without slipping down and splitting one’s self in two, or fall without breaking any bones.
Two other windows of the usual French size and pattern, with shutters to them and heavy curtains of baize, opened east and west, to let in dawn or sunset, as the case might be, or haply keep them out. And there were alcoves, recesses, irregularities, odd little nooks and corners, to be filled up as time wore on with endless personal knickknacks, bibelots, private properties and acquisitions﻿—things that make a place genial, homelike, and good to remember, and sweet to muse upon (with fond regret) in after-years.
And an immense divan spread itself in width and length and delightful thickness just beneath the big north window, the business window﻿—a divan so immense that three well-fed, well-contented Englishmen could all lie lazily smoking their pipes on it at once without being in each other’s way, and very often did!
At present one of these Englishmen﻿—a Yorkshireman, by-the-way, called Taffy (and also the Man of Blood, because he was supposed to be distantly related to a baronet)﻿—was more energetically engaged. Bare-armed, and in his shirt and trousers, he was twirling a pair of Indian clubs round his head. His face was flushed, and he was perspiring freely and looked fierce. He was a very big young man, fair, with kind but choleric blue eyes, and the muscles of his brawny arm were strong as iron bands.
For three years he had borne her Majesty’s commission, and had been through the Crimean campaign without a scratch. He would have been one of the famous six hundred in the famous charge at Balaklava but for a sprained ankle (caught playing leapfrog in the trenches), which kept him in hospital on that momentous day. So that he lost his chance of glory or the grave, and this humiliating misadventure had sickened him of soldiering for life, and he never quite got over it. Then, feeling within himself an irresistible vocation for art, he had sold out; and here he was in Paris, hard at work, as we see.

不过，地面的另一半（在吊架下方以及离窗户最远的尽头，过了模特台之后）铺着粗糙的草席垫子，好让人在击剑或练拳击时不至于滑倒劈叉，摔倒时也不至于折断骨头。
另外两扇窗户是法国常见的尺寸和样式，装有百叶窗和厚重的粗呢窗帘，分别向东和向西开启，可以适时放进晨光或晚霞，或者碰巧将它们挡在室外。屋里还有许多凹室、壁龛、不规则的凹角和古怪的角落，随着岁月的推移，将填满无尽的个人小玩意儿、精巧摆设、私人物品与收藏——这些东西让一个地方显得亲切、温馨、值得留恋，并且在往后的岁月里能够（带着深情的惋惜）甜蜜地细细回味。
而在巨大的北窗——也就是用于干活的采光窗——正下方，摆着一张在长宽和厚度上都令人赞叹的硕大长沙发；这沙发大得足以让三个吃得饱饱、心满意足的英国人同时懒洋洋地躺在上面抽烟斗而互不妨碍，而且他们也确实常常这么干！
眼下，这几个英国人中的一位——顺便提一句，他是个约克郡人，名叫塔菲（Taffy，又称“贵胄之人”，因为据说他与一位从男爵沾点远亲）——正忙着进行更加消耗体力的活动。他光着胳膊，只穿着衬衫和长裤，正挥舞着一对印度棒槌绕着脑袋飞转。他满脸通红，大汗淋漓，神情显得格外威猛。他是个极其高大魁梧的年轻小伙，皮肤白皙，长着一双善良却又容易暴躁的蓝眼睛，健硕胳膊上的肌肉结实得宛如铁箍。
他在女王陛下的军队里服役过三年军官，毫发无损地经历了克里米亚战役。若不是因为在战壕里玩跳背游戏扭伤了脚踝，导致他在那个重大日子不得不躺在野战医院里，他本来也会成为巴拉克拉瓦著名冲锋中的那六百壮士之一。因此他失去了赢得荣耀或走向坟墓的机会，这桩丢脸的意外让他终生厌倦了当兵，并且心里一直未能完全释怀。后来，他感到自己内心有着对艺术不可抗拒的召唤，便变卖了军职；如今他就在巴黎，正如我们所见，正在刻苦用功。

He was good-looking, with straight features; but I regret to say that, besides his heavy plunger’s mustache, he wore an immense pair of drooping auburn whiskers, of the kind that used to be called Piccadilly weepers, and were afterwards affected by Mr. Sothern in Lord Dundreary. It was a fashion to do so then for such of our gilded youth as could afford the time (and the hair); the bigger and fairer the whiskers, the more beautiful was thought the youth! It seems incredible in these days, when even her Majesty’s household brigade go about with smooth cheeks and lips, like priests or playactors.

Another inmate of this blissful abode﻿—Sandy, the Laird of Cockpen, as he was called﻿—sat in similarly simple attire at his easel, painting at a lifelike little picture of a Spanish toreador serenading a lady of high degree (in broad daylight). And then, in his keen appreciation of the immortal stanza, he chuckled audibly, with a face so blithe and merry and well pleased that it did one good to look at him.
He also had entered life by another door. His parents (good, pious people in Dundee) had intended that he should be a solicitor, as his father and grandfather had been before him. And here he was in Paris famous, painting toreadors, and spouting the “Ballad of the Bouillabaisse,” as he would often do out of sheer lightness of heart﻿—much oftener, indeed, than he would say his prayers.
Kneeling on the divan, with his elbow on the windowsill, was a third and much younger youth. The third he was “Little Billee.” He had pulled down the green baize blind, and was looking over the roofs and chimney-pots of Paris and all about with all his eyes, munching the while a roll and a savory saveloy, in which there was evidence of much garlic. He ate with great relish, for he was very hungry; he had been all the morning at Carrel’s studio, drawing from the life.
Little Billee was small and slender, about twenty or twenty-one, and had a straight white forehead veined with blue, large dark-blue eyes, delicate, regular features, and coal-black hair. He was also very graceful and well built, with very small hands and feet, and much better dressed than his friends, who went out of their way to outdo the denizens of the quartier latin in careless eccentricity of garb, and succeeded. And in his winning and handsome face there was just a faint suggestion of some possible very remote Jewish ancestor﻿—just a tinge of that strong, sturdy, irrepressible, indomitable, indelible blood which is of such priceless value in diluted homeopathic doses, like the dry white Spanish wine called *montijo*, which is not meant to be taken pure; but without a judicious admixture of which no sherry can go round the world and keep its flavor intact; or like the famous bulldog strain, which is not beautiful in itself; and yet just for lacking a little of the same no greyhound can ever hope to be a champion. So, at least, I have been told by wine-merchants and dog-fanciers﻿—the most veracious persons that can be. Fortunately for the world, and especially for ourselves, most of us have in our veins at least a minim of that precious fluid, whether we know it or show it or not. *Tant pis pour les autres!

念罢，他由衷赞赏着这不朽的诗句，忍不住咯咯笑出声来，面容欢畅喜悦，令人看了便觉心旷神怡。
他同样是走另一扇门踏入人生的。他的父母（邓迪的虔诚善良之辈）本打算让他成为一名事务律师，正如他的父亲和祖父当年一样。如今他却身处巴黎，画着斗牛士，背诵着《普罗旺斯鱼汤歌谣》——他常常纯粹出于心情欢畅而背诵此诗，确实比他做祷告的次数还要频繁得多。
跪在长沙发上、胳膊肘支在窗台上的，是第三个、也是年轻得多的青年。这第三人便是“小比利”（Little Billee）。他拉下了绿呢窗帘，正睁大双眼越过巴黎的屋顶和烟囱向四处张望，一边吃着小面包和一根香喷喷的红肠，里面显然加了不少大蒜。他吃得津津有味，因为他确实饿坏了；他整个上午都在卡雷尔画室画人体写生。
小比利身量娇小匀称，大约二十或二十一岁，生着一片显露青筋的平整白皙前额，一双深蓝色的明亮大眼，五官清秀匀称，满头乌黑的头发。他体态优美匀称，手脚娇小，穿着也比他的两位同伴讲究得多——那两位同伴竭力要比拉丁区的本地住客穿得更加随性古怪，并且确实办到了。在他那招人喜爱、英俊端正的脸上，隐约透着几分可能有极为遥远的犹太祖先的迹象——略带一丝那种强健、坚韧、难以压制、百折不挠且不可磨灭的血脉，正如一种名为*蒙蒂霍*（montijo）的西班牙干白葡萄酒，哪怕只以稀薄的顺势疗法剂量掺入，也具有难以估量的价值；这种酒原不该纯饮，但若不适度调配入内，便没有任何雪莉酒能在环游世界时保持风味不失；又宛如著名的斗牛犬血统，其本身并不美观，然而一旦缺失了这一丝血统，便没有任何灵缇犬能够指望夺得冠军。至少，酒商和养犬专家是这么告诉我的——他们可是世上最诚实可靠的人了。对世界而言，尤其是对我们自己而言，幸运的是，不论我们自知显露与否，我们绝大多数人的血脉里至少都流淌着一滴那般珍贵的汁液。其他人可就倒霉了（*Tant pis pour les autres!*）！

As Little Billee munched he also gazed at the busy place below﻿—the Place St. Anatole des Arts﻿—at the old houses opposite, some of which were being pulled down, no doubt lest they should fall of their own sweet will. In the gaps between he would see discolored, old, cracked, dingy walls, with mysterious windows and rusty iron balconies of great antiquity﻿—sights that set him dreaming dreams of medieval French love and wickedness and crime, bygone mysteries of Paris!
One gap went right through the block, and gave him a glimpse of the river, the “Cité,” and the ominous old Morgue; a little to the right rose the gray towers of Notre Dame de Paris into the checkered April sky. Indeed, the top of nearly all Paris lay before him, with a little stretch of the imagination on his part; and he gazed with a sense of novelty, an interest and a pleasure for which he could not have found any expression in mere language.
Paris! Paris!! Paris!!!
The very name had always been one to conjure with, whether he thought of it as a mere sound on the lips and in the ear, or as a magical written or printed word for the eye. And here was the thing itself at last, and he, he himself, *ipsissimus*, in the very midst of it, to live there and learn there as long as he liked, and make himself the great artist he longed to be.
Then, his meal finished, he lit a pipe, and flung himself on the divan and sighed deeply, out of the overfull contentment of his heart.
He felt he had never known happiness like this, never even dreamed its possibility. And yet his life had been a happy one. He was young and tender, was Little Billee; he had never been to any school, and was innocent of the world and its wicked ways; innocent of French especially, and the ways of Paris and its Latin quarter. He had been brought up and educated at home, had spent his boyhood in London with his mother and sister, who now lived in Devonshire on somewhat straitened means. His father, who was dead, had been a clerk in the Treasury.
He and his two friends, Taffy and the Laird, had taken this studio together. The Laird slept there, in a small bedroom off the studio. Taffy had a bedroom at the Hôtel de Seine, in the street of that name. Little Billee lodged at the Hôtel Corneille, in the Place de l’Odéon.

小比利一边嚼着食物，一边俯瞰着下方喧闹的广场——圣阿纳托尔艺术广场（Place St. Anatole des Arts）——以及对面的老旧房屋，其中一些正在被拆除，无疑是担心它们随心所欲自行坍塌。在屋舍的缝隙间，他能看见褪色、陈旧、开裂且昏暗的墙壁，嵌着神秘的窗户与古色古香的生锈铁阳台——这般景象让他沉浸在中世纪法国的爱情、邪恶与罪案、那些消逝的巴黎谜案的遐想之中！
其中一条空隙径直穿透整个街区，让他瞥见了塞纳河、“西岱岛”（Cité）以及那座阴森的老停尸房；稍偏右侧，巴黎圣母院的灰色塔楼耸立在四月斑驳的天空之中。事实上，只要稍微发挥一点想象力，几乎整个巴黎的屋顶都尽收眼底；他凝望着这一切，心中满是新奇、趣味与愉悦，那是单凭言语无论如何也无法表达的情感。
巴黎！巴黎！！巴黎！！！
单单这个名字就曾是一道令人心驰神往的魔咒，不论是将它视作唇齿间与耳畔的声响，还是视作落入眼帘的奇妙文字与印刷字样。如今真切的景象终于展现在眼前，而他，他本人，实实在在地置身于这片天地的正中央，尽可以随心所欲在这里生活、学习，把自己造就成梦寐以求的伟大艺术家。
吃完饭后，他点上一斗烟，一头栽倒在长沙发上，心满意足地长舒了一口气。
