### XXXIV / 第三十四篇

===Original===
These towns of the Antipodes, not so great then as they are now, took an interest in the shipping, the running links with “home,” whose numbers confirmed the sense of their growing importance. They made it part and parcel of their daily interests. This was especially the case in Sydney, where, from the heart of the fair city, down the vista of important streets, could be seen the wool-clippers lying at the Circular Quay﻿—no walled prison-house of a dock that, but the integral part of one of the finest, most beautiful, vast, and safe bays the sun ever shone upon. Now great steam-liners lie at these berks, always reserved for the sea aristocracy﻿—grand and imposing enough ships, but here today and gone next week; whereas the general cargo, emigrant, and passenger clippers of my time, rigged with heavy spars, and built on fine lines, used to remain for months together waiting for their load of wool. Their names attained the dignity of household words. On Sundays and holidays the citizens trooped down, on visiting bent, and the lonely officer on duty solaced himself by playing the cicerone﻿—especially to the citizenesses with engaging manners and a well-developed sense of the fun that may be got out of the inspection of a ship’s cabins and staterooms. The tinkle of more or less untuned cottage pianos floated out of open stern-ports till the gas-lamps began to twinkle in the streets, and the ship’s night-watchman, coming sleepily on duty after his unsatisfactory day slumbers, hauled down the flags and fastened a lighted lantern at the break of the gangway.

===Chinese===
这些对跖地（Antipodes）的城镇，当时远不似今日这般宏伟，却对航运——那些与"家乡"的流动纽带——怀有浓厚兴趣，船舶的数量印证着它们日益增长的重要感。航运成了日常关切的一部分。在悉尼尤为如此，从这座美丽城市的中心，沿着重要街道的纵深望去，便能看见那些运羊毛快帆船（wool-clippers）停泊在圆形码头（Circular Quay）——那不是一道围困的牢狱，而是阳光照耀下最优良、最壮美、最辽阔、最安全的港湾的天然组成部分。如今，巨大的蒸汽邮轮停靠在这些泊位——它们总是为海上的贵族保留——船舰宏伟壮观，但今日在此，下周便不见踪影；而我那个时代的杂货、移民与客船快帆船，挂着厚重的帆桁，线条优美，往往一等就是几个月，等待装载羊毛。它们的名字获得了家喻户晓的尊严。在周日和节假日，市民们成群结队前来参观，而独自值勤的军官则以充当导游来自慰——尤其是对那些仪态迷人、深知参观船舶舱室与特等舱之乐趣的女市民们。或多或少走了调的家庭钢琴的叮咚声，从敞开的艉部舷窗飘出，直到街上的煤气灯开始闪烁，船上的夜间看守人睡眼惺忪地值勤——白日里他那不如人意的睡眠刚刚结束——降下旗帜，在舷梯的折角处挂上一盏点亮的提灯。

The night closed rapidly upon the silent ships with their crews on shore. Up a short, steep ascent by the King’s Head pub., patronized by the cooks and stewards of the fleet, the voice of a man crying “Hot saveloys!” at the end of George Street, where the cheap eating-houses (sixpence a meal) were kept by Chinamen (Sun-kum-on’s was not bad), is heard at regular intervals. I have listened for hours to this most pertinacious pedlar (I wonder whether he is dead or has made a fortune), while sitting on the rail of the old *Duke of S﻿—﻿—﻿* (she’s dead, poor thing! a violent death on the coast of New Zealand), fascinated by the monotony, the regularity, the abruptness of the recurring cry, and so exasperated at the absurd spell, that I wished the fellow would choke himself to death with a mouthful of his own infamous wares.

===Original===
A stupid job, and fit only for an old man, my comrades used to tell me, to be the night-watchman of a captive (though honoured) ship. And generally the oldest of the able seamen in a ship’s crew does get it. But sometimes neither the oldest nor any other fairly steady seaman is forthcoming. Ships’ crews had the trick of melting away swiftly in those days. So, probably on account of my youth, innocence, and pensive habits (which made me sometimes dilatory in my work about the rigging), I was suddenly nominated, in our chief mate Mr. B﻿—﻿—’s most sardonic tones, to that enviable situation. I do not regret the experience. The night humours of the town descended from the street to the waterside in the still watches of the night: larrikins rushing down in bands to settle some quarrel by a stand-up fight, away from the police, in an indistinct ring half hidden by piles of cargo, with the sounds of blows, a groan now and then, the stamping of feet, and the cry of “Time!” rising suddenly above the sinister and excited murmurs; night-prowlers, pursued or pursuing, with a stifled shriek followed by a profound silence, or slinking stealthily alongside like ghosts, and addressing me from the quay below in mysterious tones with incomprehensible propositions.

The cabmen, too, who twice a week, on the night when the A.S.N. Company’s passenger-boat was due to arrive, used to range a battalion of blazing lamps opposite the ship, were very amusing in their way. They got down from their perches and told each other impolite stories in racy language, every word of which reached me distinctly over the bulwarks as I sat smoking on the main-hatch. On one occasion I had an hour or so of a most intellectual conversation with a person whom I could not see distinctly, a gentleman from England, he said, with a cultivated voice, I on deck and he on the quay sitting on the case of a piano (landed out of our hold that very afternoon), and smoking a cigar which smelt very good. We touched, in our discourse, upon science, politics, natural history, and operatic singers. Then, after remarking abruptly, “You seem to be rather intelligent, my man,” he informed me pointedly that his name was Mr. Senior, and walked off﻿—to his hotel, I suppose. Shadows! Shadows! I think I saw a white whisker as he turned under the lamppost. It is a shock to think that in the natural course of nature he must be dead by now. There was nothing to object to in his intelligence but a little dogmatism maybe. And his name was Senior! Mr. Senior!

The position had its drawbacks, however. One wintry, blustering, dark night in July, as I stood sleepily out of the rain under the break of the poop something resembling an ostrich dashed up the gangway. I say ostrich because the creature, though it ran on two legs, appeared to help its progress by working a pair of short wings; it was a man, however, only his coat, ripped up the back and flapping in two halves above his shoulders, gave him that weird and fowl-like appearance. At least, I suppose it was his coat, for it was impossible to make him out distinctly. How he managed to come so straight upon me, at speed and without a stumble over a strange deck, I cannot imagine. He must have been able to see in the dark better than any cat. He overwhelmed me with panting entreaties to let him take shelter till morning in our forecastle. 十年后，我偶然地、出乎意料地在街上遇见了他，当时我刚从我的收货人办公室出来。我本不太可能忘记他，还有他那句"我现在能行了"。他立刻认出了我，记得我的名字，以及我在他麾下服役的那条船。他将我从头到脚打量了一番。

===Original===

===Chinese===
"你在这里做什么？"他问。

===Original===
I felt I would rather have bitten out my tongue. His jet-black, curly hair had turned iron-grey; he was scrupulously neat as ever, but frightfully threadbare. His shiny boots were worn down at heel. But he forgave me, and we drove off together in a hansom to dine on board my ship. He went over her conscientiously, praised her heartily, congratulated me on my command with absolute sincerity. At dinner, as I offered him wine and beer he shook his head, and as I sat looking at him interrogatively, muttered in an undertone:

===Chinese===
我感到我宁愿咬掉自己的舌头。他那乌黑的卷发已变成铁灰色；他一如既往地一丝不苟，却破旧得可怕。他闪亮的靴子后跟已磨损。但他原谅了我，我们一同乘一辆双轮马车离开，在我的船上用餐。他认真地检查了她，由衷地赞美她，以绝对的真诚祝贺我的指挥权。晚餐时，我给他斟酒和啤酒，他摇了摇头，当我坐在那里询问地看着他时，他低声咕哝道：

===Original===
“I’ve given up all that.”

===Chinese===
"我已经戒掉那些了。"

===Original===
After dinner we came again on deck. It seemed as though he could not tear himself away from the ship. We were fitting some new lower rigging, and he hung about, approving, suggesting, giving me advice in his old manner. Twice he addressed me as “My boy,” and corrected himself quickly to “Captain.” My mate was about to leave me (to get married), but I concealed the fact from Mr. B﻿—﻿—. I was afraid he would ask me to give him the berth in some ghastly jocular hint that I could not refuse to take. I was afraid. It would have been impossible. I could not have given orders to Mr. B﻿—﻿—, and I am sure he would not have taken them from me very long. He could not have managed that, though he had managed to break himself from drink﻿—too late.

===Chinese===
晚餐后我们再次来到甲板上。仿佛他无法离开这条船。我们正在安装一些新的下部索具，他四处闲逛，赞许着，提议着，以他旧时的方式给我建议。两次他称我为"我的孩子"（My boy），随即又迅速改口为"船长"（Captain）。我的大副即将离开我（去结婚），但我对B先生隐瞒了此事。我怕他会用某种可怕的玩笑暗示，要求我将那个职位给他，而我无法拒绝接受。我怕。那本是不可能的。我无法对B先生发号施令，而且我确信他也不会从我这里接受太久。他本无法设法做到这一点，尽管他已设法戒了酒——但为时已晚。

===Original===
He said goodbye at last. As I watched his burly, bull-necked figure walk away up the street, I wondered with a sinking heart whether he had much more than the price of a night’s lodging in his pocket. And I understood that if that very minute I were to call out after him, he would not even turn his head. He, too, is no more than a shadow, but I seem to hear his words spoken on the moonlit deck of the old *Duke of S﻿—﻿—﻿*:

===Chinese===
他终于道别了。当我望着他那魁梧、牛颈般的身影走上街头时，我心情沉重地揣测，他口袋里是否除了住一晚客栈的钱外，还有更多。而且我明白，如果那一刻我在他身后呼喊，他甚至不会回头。他也已不再是别的，只是一个影子，但我似乎能听到他的话语在古老《S公爵号》（*Duke of S—*）月光下的甲板上响起：

===Original===
“Ports are no good﻿—ships rot, men go to the devil!”

===Chinese===
"港口毫无用处——船会腐烂，人会堕落！"
