## Lecture X English Literature in Our Universities (I) / X 英国文学与大学（上）

===Original===
Wednesday, November 19

===Chinese===
11 月 19 日，星期三

===Original===
All lectures are too long. Towards the close of my last, Gentlemen, I let fall a sentence which, heard by you in a moment of exhausted or languid interest, has since, like enough, escaped your memory even if it earned passing attention. So let me repeat it, for a fresh start.

===Chinese===
一切讲座都太长了。诸位先生，上一讲临近收尾时，我随口落下一句话；你们当时听得兴趣耗竭、意兴阑珊，那句话此后多半已从你们的记忆中溜走——即便它曾博得片刻注意，也未必例外。所以，为着一个新的开端，让我把它重述一遍。

===Original===
Having quoted to you the words of our Holy Writ, “I will sing and give praise with the best member that I have,” I added, “But the old Greek was an ‘all-round’ man; he sought to praise and give thanks with all his members, and to tune each to perfection.” Now a great many instructive lectures might be written on that text: nevertheless you may think it a strange one, and obscure, for the discourse on “English Literature in our Universities,” which, according to promise, I must now attempt.

===Chinese===
先前我引了我们《圣经》上的话：「我要用我所拥有的最好肢体歌唱颂赞」，随后补了一句：「然而古希腊人是个『全面』（all-round）的人；他要以全部肢体去颂赞、去感恩，并把每一件都调校到完善。」就这条箴言，本可以写出许许多多富有教益的讲座来：然而你们或许觉得，拿它作「英国文学与大学」这篇讲演的题旨——按先前的许诺，我如今必须一试的这篇——未免奇特而费解。

===Original===
The term “an all-round man” may easily mislead you unless you take it with the rest of the sentence and particularly with the words “praise and give thanks.” Praise whom? Give thanks to whom? To *whom* did our Greek train all his members to render adoration?

===Chinese===
「全面的人」这个说法很容易把你们引入歧途，除非你们把它同句子的其余部分、尤其同「颂赞」「感恩」这几个词连在一起看。颂赞谁？向谁感恩？我们的希腊人调校他的全部肢体，是要向谁奉献崇敬？

===Original===
Why, to the gods﻿—his gods: to Zeus, Apollo, Aphrodite; and from them down to the lesser guardian deities of the hearth, the field, the farmstead. We modern men suffer a double temptation to misunderstand, by belittling, the reverence in which Hellas and Rome held their gods. To start with, our religion has superseded theirs. We approach the Olympians with no bent towards venerating them; with minds easy, detached, to which a great deal of their theology﻿—the amativeness of Zeus for example﻿—must needs seem broadly comic, and a great deal of it not only comic but childish. We are encouraged in this, moreover, when we read such writers as Aristophanes and Lucian, and observe how they poked fun at the gods. We assume﻿—so modern he seems﻿—Aristophanes’ attitude towards his immortals to be ours; that when, for example, Prometheus walks on to the stage under an umbrella, to hide himself from the gaze of all-seeing Zeus, the Athenian audience laughed just as we laugh who have read Voltaire. Believe me, they laughed quite differently; believe me, Aristophanes and Voltaire had remarkably different minds and worked on utterly different backgrounds. Believe me, you will understand Aristophanes only less than you will understand Aeschylus himself if you confuse Aristophanes’ mockery of Olympus with modern mockery. But, if you will not take my word for it, let me quote what Professor Gilbert Murray said, the other day, speaking before the English Association on Greek poetry, how constantly connected it is with religion:

===Chinese===
当然是向诸神——他的诸神：向宙斯（Zeus）、阿波罗（Apollo）、阿佛洛狄忒（Aphrodite）；再从他们一路下来，直到炉灶、田亩、农庄的那些较小的守护神明。我们现代人受着双重的诱惑，容易误解——而且是以贬低的方式误解——希腊与罗马对他们的诸神所怀的崇敬。首先，我们的宗教已经取代了他们的宗教。我们走近奥林匹斯诸神，全无尊奉之意；心境轻松而超然，于是他们的神学里有相当一部分——例如宙斯的好色多情——在我们看来必定大体上是喜剧性的，还有相当一部分不仅可哂，简直幼稚。何况，一读阿里斯托芬（Aristophanes）、琉善（Lucian）这样的作家，看到他们怎样拿诸神打趣，我们这种态度更得到了鼓励。我们想当然——他显得何等现代——把阿里斯托芬对待他不朽诸神的态度当成了我们自己的态度；以为当普罗米修斯（Prometheus）撑着一把伞走上舞台、躲避全知的宙斯的目光时，雅典观众笑得同我们这些读过伏尔泰（Voltaire）的人一模一样。相信我，他们笑得全然两样；相信我，阿里斯托芬与伏尔泰的心智大相径庭，其活动的背景也截然不同。相信我，你若把阿里斯托芬对奥林匹斯的嘲弄同现代的嘲弄混为一谈，那么你之不理解阿里斯托芬，将与你不理解埃斯库罗斯（Aeschylus）本人相去无几。但你们若不肯信我的话，且容我引一段吉尔伯特·默里教授（Professor Gilbert Murray）前些天在英国语文协会（the English Association）讲希腊诗歌时所说的话——他说希腊诗歌与宗教的联系何等恒常：

===Original===
> “All thoughts, all passions, all desires”﻿ ﻿… In our Art it is true, no doubt, that they are “the ministers of love”; in Greek they are as a whole the ministers of religion, and this is what in a curious degree makes Greek poetry matter, makes it relevant. There is a sense in each song of a relation to the whole of things, and it was apt to be expressed with the whole body, or, one may say, the whole being.14

===Chinese===
> 「一切思想、一切激情、一切欲望」……在我们的艺术里，不错，它们无疑「是爱的仆役」；而在希腊，它们整个儿是宗教的仆役：恰恰是在一种奇妙的程度上，这一点使希腊诗歌成其为大事、成其为切题。每一首歌里都有一种与万物全体的关联之感，而它惯于用整个身体——或者说整个存在——表达出来。14

===Original===
To a Greek, in short, his gods mattered enormously; and to a Roman. To a Roman they continued to matter enormously, down to the end. Do you remember that tessellated pavement with its emblems and images of the younger gods? and how I told you that a Roman general on foreign service would carry the little cubes in panniers on mule-back, to be laid down for his feet at the next camping place? Will you suggest that he did this because they were pretty? You know that practical men﻿—conquering generals﻿—don’t behave in that way. He did it because they were sacred; because, like most practical men, he was religious, and his gods must go with him. They filled his literature: for why? He believed himself to be sprung from their loins. Where would Latin literature be, for example, if you could cut Venus out of it? Consider Lucretius’ grand invocation:

===Chinese===
简言之，对希腊人而言，他的诸神至关重要；对罗马人亦然。直到最后，诸神对罗马人始终至关重要。你们还记得那方镶嵌铺面，连同其上较年轻诸神的徽记与图像么？还记得我怎么告诉你们的么——一位外任征战的罗马将军，会把那些小方块镶嵌石装进驮篮、由骡子驮着走，好在下一处营盘铺在他的脚下？你们会说，他这样做是因为那些石片好看么？你们知道，讲求实际的人——开疆取胜的将军——是不这么行事的。他这样做，是因为它们神圣；因为同大多数讲求实际的人一样，他有宗教心，他的诸神必须随他同行。诸神充盈着他的文学：何以故？因为他相信自己是他们的骨血所出。譬如说，倘若你能把维纳斯从拉丁文学里剜掉，拉丁文学还成个什么？且看卢克莱修（Lucretius）那堂皇的祷唤：

===Original===
> Æneadum genetrix, hominum divumque voluptas, Alma Venus!

===Chinese===
> Æneadum genetrix, hominum divumque voluptas, Alma Venus!（埃涅阿斯族人的母亲，人与神的欢愉，慈爱的维纳斯啊！）

===Original===
Consider the part Virgil makes her play as moving spirit of his whole great poem. So follow her down to the days of the later Empire and open the “Pervigilium Veneris” and discover her, under the name of Dione, still the eternal Aphrodite sprung from the foam amid the churning hooves of the seahorses﻿—*inter et bipedes equos*:﻿—

===Chinese===
且看维吉尔（Virgil）派定她扮演的角色：整部伟大诗篇的推动灵魂。再追随她下到帝国后期的岁月，翻开《维纳斯守夜歌》（Pervigilium Veneris），你会发现她化名狄俄涅（Dione），依然是那位永恒的阿佛洛狄忒，在翻搅的海马蹄与浪沫之间诞生——*inter et bipedes equos*（在那些双足的马之间）：——

===Original===
> Time was that a rain-cloud begat her, impregning the heave of the deep,
 

 ’Twixt hooves of seahorses a-scatter, stampeding the dolphins as sheep.
 

 Lo! arose of that bridal Dione, rainbow’d and besprent of its dew!
 

 
 *Now learn ye to love who loved never﻿—now ye who have loved, love anew!*

 With the Ramnes, Quirites, together ancestrally proud as they drew
 

 From Romulus down to our Caesar﻿—last, best of that blood, of that thew.
 

 
 *Now learn ye to love who loved never﻿—now ye who have loved, love anew!*

> 昔日一片雨云孕育了她，使深海的翻腾受了孕，
 

 散蹄乱奔的海马之间，海豚如羊群般惊窜四逃。
 

 看哪！那桩婚配里升起了狄俄涅，披着虹彩、缀着雨露！
 

 
 *不曾爱过的，且学着爱——爱过的，重新去爱！*
 
 

 是她的恩宠，鼓满了特洛伊人驶往拉丁姆的船帆，
 

 是她的恩宠，在劳伦图姆为埃涅阿斯赢来了新娘，
 

 转眼又诱得贞女、灶神侍女出了圣堂，委身玛尔斯；
 

 她又以萨宾抢婚的奇智，为连绵战事重造了罗马，
 

 连同拉姆尼斯人、奎里特斯人，怀着同源的祖先之骄，
 

 从罗慕路斯直抵我们的恺撒——那血脉与筋骨里最后、最好的一个。
 

 
 *不曾爱过的，且学着爱——爱过的，重新去爱！*

===Original===
“Last, best of that blood”﻿—her blood, *fusa Paphies de cruore*, and the blood of Teucer, *revocato a sanguine Teucri*, “of that thew”﻿—the thew of Tros and of Mars. Of these and no less than these our Roman believed himself the son and inheritor.

===Chinese===
「那血脉与筋骨里最后、最好的一个」——她的血脉，*fusa Paphies de cruore*（出自帕福斯人的血）；透克洛斯（Teucer）的血，*revocato a sanguine Teucri*（从透克洛斯之血召回）；「那筋骨」——特洛斯（Tros）与玛尔斯（Mars）的筋骨。我们的罗马人相信，自己正是这些——而且不减于这些——的儿子与继承人。

===Original===
If we grasp this, that the old literature was packed with the old religion, and not only packed with it but permeated by it, we have within our ten fingers the secret of the Dark Ages, the real reason why the Christian Fathers fought down literature and almost prevailed to the point of stamping it out. They hated it, not as literature; or at any rate, not to begin with; nor, to begin with, because it happened to be voluptuous and they austere: but they hated it because it held in its very texture, not to be separated, a religion over which they had hardly triumphed, a religion actively inimical to that of Christ, inimical to truth; so that for the sake of truth and in the name of Christ they had to fight it, accepting no compromise, yielding no quarter, foreseeing no issue save that one of the twain﻿—Jupiter or Christ, Deus Optimus Maximus or the carpenter’s son of Nazareth﻿—must go under.

===Chinese===
我们若把握住这一点——古代文学里塞满了古代宗教，不仅塞满，而且浸透——那么黑暗时代的秘密、教父们打压文学并且几乎得手到把它扑灭的真实原因，就尽在我们十指之间了。他们恨它，不是把它当文学恨；或者说无论如何起初不是；起初也不是因为它恰巧耽于声色而他们厉行苦修：他们恨它，是因为它的质地里织着一种宗教，无从剥离——一种他们尚未真正战胜的宗教，一种积极敌视基督教、敌视真理的宗教；于是，为了真理的缘故、奉基督之名，他们不得不与它作战：不容妥协，不留饶赦，预见的出路只有一条——二者之一必须倒下：朱庇特（Jupiter）或基督，至高至善之神（Deus Optimus Maximus）或拿撒勒的木匠之子。

===Original===
It all ended in compromise, to be sure; as all struggles must between adversaries so tremendous. Today, in Dr. Smith’s *Classical Dictionary*, Origen rubs shoulders with Orpheus and Orcus; Tertullian reposes cheek by jowl with Terpsichore. But we are not concerned, here, with what happened in the end. We are concerned with what these forthright Christian fighters had in their minds﻿—to trample out the old literature *because* of the false religion. Milton understood this, and was thinking of it when he wrote of the effect of Christ’s Nativity﻿—

===Chinese===
当然，这一切终以妥协收场；势均力敌到这般地步的对手之间的争斗，历来只能如此。今天，在史密斯博士的《古典辞典》（*Classical Dictionary*）里，奥利金（Origen）与俄耳甫斯（Orpheus）、俄耳库斯（Orcus）摩肩接踵；德尔图良（Tertullian）与忒耳普西科瑞（Terpsichore）抵足而眠。但我们在此关心的不是最终的结局。我们关心的是这些决绝的基督教斗士心中所想——为了那种伪宗教的缘故，把旧文学踏灭。弥尔顿懂得这一点；他写到基督降生的功效时，心里想的正是它——

===Original===
> The Oracles are dumb;
 

 No voice or hideous hum
 

 Runs through the archèd roof in words deceiving.

 Can no more divine,
 

 With hollow shriek the steep of Delphos leaving.

> 神谕喑哑了；

 穿过拱顶，说着欺人的话语。
 

 阿波罗离开了他的神龛，

===Original===
—as Swinburne understands and expresses it in his “Hymn to Proserpine,” supposed to be chanted by a Roman of the “old profession” on the morrow of Constantine’s proclaiming the Christian faith﻿—

===Chinese===
——正如斯温伯恩所理解并表达的那样；在他的《献给普罗塞皮娜的赞歌》（“Hymn to Proserpine”）里，一位操「旧业」的罗马人，在君士坦丁（Constantine）宣布基督教信仰的次日，唱出这首歌——

> 被黜而死的诸神啊，遭放逐、遭抹煞，就在一日之间！

===Original===
“Thou hast conquer’d, O pale Galilean!” However the struggle might sway in this or that other part of the field, Literature had to be beaten to her knees, and still beaten flat until the breath left her body. You will not be surprised that the heavy hand of these Christian fathers fell first upon the Theatre: for the actor in Rome was by legal definition an “infamous” man, even as in England until the other day he was by legal definition a vagabond and liable to whipping. The policy of religious reformers has ever been to close the theatres, as our Puritans did in 1642; and a recent pronouncement by the Bishop of Kensington would seem to show that the instinct survives to this day. Queen Elizabeth﻿—like her brother, King Edward VI﻿—signalized the opening of a new reign by inhibiting stage-plays; and I invite you to share with me the pensive speculation, “How much of English Literature, had she not relented, would exist today for a King Edward VII Professor to talk about?” Certainly the works of Shakespeare would not; and that seems to me a thought so impressive as to deserve the attention of Bishops as well as of Kings.

===Chinese===
「你得胜了，苍白的加利利人！」无论战事在这块或那块战场上如何翻覆，文学注定要被打得跪下，还要被摁倒在地，直打到断气为止。这些基督教教父的重手最先落在戏剧上，你们不必惊讶：因为在罗马，演员按法律定义便是「不名誉」之人，正如在英格兰，直到不久之前，他在法律定义上还是个流浪汉、要受鞭笞。宗教改革家的政策从来就是关闭剧院，我们的清教徒 1642 年干的就是这一手；而肯辛顿主教（Bishop of Kensington）最近的一次宣告似乎表明，这本能至今犹存。伊丽莎白女王——同她的王弟爱德华六世（King Edward VI）一样——以禁演舞台剧来标志新朝的开端；我请诸位同我一起作一番怅然的推想：「倘若她不曾心软回转，今日还会有多少英国文学，供一位爱德华七世讲席教授谈论？」莎士比亚的著作肯定是没有了；而这在我看来，是一个分量重得足以引起主教们、同样也引起国王们注意的念头。

===Original===
Apart from this instinct the Christian Fathers, it would appear, had plenty of provocation. For the actors, who had jested with the Old Religion on a ground of accepted understanding﻿—much as a good husband (if you will permit the simile) may gently tease his wife, not loving her one whit the less, taught by affection to play without offending﻿—had mocked at the New Religion in a very different way: savagely, as enemies, holding up to ridicule the Church’s most sacred mysteries. Tertullian, in an uncompromising treatise “De Spectaculis,” denounces stage-plays root and branch; tells of a demon who entered into a woman in a theatre and on being exorcised pleaded that the mistake might well be excused, since he had found her in his own demesne. Christians should avoid these shows and await the greatest *spectaculum* of all﻿—the Last Judgment. “Then,” he promises genially, “will be the time to listen to the tragedians, whose lamentations will be more poignant, for their proper pain. Then will the comedians turn and twist in capers rendered nimbler than ever by the sting of the fire that is not quenched.” By 400 AD Augustine cries triumphantly that the theatres are falling﻿—the very walls of them tumbling﻿—throughout the Empire. “*Per omnes paene civitates cadunt theatra﻿ ﻿… cadunt et fora vel moenia in quibus demonia colebantur*”; the very walls within which these devilments were practised. But the fury is unabated and goes on stamping down the embers. In the eighth century our own Alcuin (as the school of Freeman would affectionately call him) is no less fierce.
