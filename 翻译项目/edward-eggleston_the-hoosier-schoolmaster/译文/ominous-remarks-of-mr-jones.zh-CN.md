## VII Ominous Remarks of Mr. Jones / 琼斯先生的凶话

===Original===
The schoolmaster’s mind was like ancient Gaul﻿—divided into three parts. With one part he mechanically performed his school duties. With another he asked himself, What shall I do about the robbery? And with the third he debated about Bud and Hannah. For Bud was not present, and it was clear that he was angry, and there was a storm brewing. In fact, it seemed to Ralph that there was a storm brewing all round the sky. For Pete Jones was evidently angry at the thought of having been watched, and it was fair to suppose that Dr. Small was not in any better humor than usual. And so, between Bud’s jealousy and revenge and the suspicion and resentment of the men engaged in the robbery at “the Dutchman’s” (as the only German in the whole region was called), Ralph’s excited nerves had cause for tremor. At one moment he would resolve to have Hannah at all costs. In the next his conscience would question the rightfulness of the conclusion. Then he would make up his mind to tell all he knew about the robbery. But if he told his suspicions about Small, nobody would believe him. And if he told about Pete Jones, he really could tell only enough to bring vengeance upon himself. And how could he explain his own walk through the pasture and down the road? What business had he being out of bed at two o’clock in the morning? The circumstantial evidence was quite as strong against him as against the man on the horse with the white left forefoot and the white nose. Suspicion might fasten on himself. And then what would be the effect on his prospects? On the people at Lewisburg? On Hannah? It is astonishing how much instruction and comfort there is in a bulldog. This slender schoolmaster, who had been all his life repressing the animal and developing the finer nature, now found a need of just what the bulldog had. And so, with the thought of how his friend the dog would fight in a desperate strait, he determined to take hold of his difficulties as Bull took hold of the raccoon. Moral questions he postponed for careful decision. But for the present he set his teeth together in a desperate, bulldog fashion, and he set his feet down slowly, positively, bulldoggedly. After a wretched supper at Pete Jones’s he found himself at the spelling-school, which, owing to the absence of Hannah, and the excitement about the burglary, was a dull affair. Half the evening was spent in talking in little knots. Pete Jones had taken the afflicted “Dutchman” under his own particular supervision.

这位老师的心思活像古高卢——分作三块。一块机械地应付着教学的本职。另一块问自己：劫案这件事，我该怎么办？第三块则拿来斟酌巴德和汉娜的事。因为巴德没来上学，明摆着在生气，一场风暴正在酝酿。其实，在拉尔夫看来，满天四下里都在酝酿风暴。皮特·琼斯一想到被人盯了梢，显然动了火；而斯莫尔医生的心情，料想也不会比平常好到哪里去。于是，一边是巴德的醋意和报复心，一边是参与抢劫「荷兰佬」家（这一带唯一的德国人就得了这么个称呼）的那伙人的疑心和怨毒，拉尔夫绷紧的神经有的是发抖的理由。这一刻他下定决心，不惜一切也要得到汉娜；下一刻良心又来质问这结论正当不正当。然后他拿定主意，要把劫案里自己知道的全说出来。可他要是说出对斯莫尔的怀疑，没有人会信；要是说皮特·琼斯，他真正能说的那点东西，只够把仇引到自己头上。再说，他自己穿过牧场、顺着大路走的那一段，又怎么解释？凌晨两点不躺在床上，他有什么正经事？旁证指向他的分量，跟指向那个骑马的人——骑一匹左前脚和鼻子都是白色的马的人——的分量一样沉。嫌疑没准儿就落到他自己头上。到那时，他的前程会怎样？刘易斯堡那些人会怎样？汉娜会怎样？一条斗牛犬身上竟有这么多教益和安慰，实在叫人吃惊。这位瘦削的老师，一辈子都在压抑身上的兽性、涵养精细的天性，如今却发现，自己恰恰缺一条斗牛犬有的东西。于是，想到他的朋友那条狗在绝境里会怎样厮打，他决意像布尔咬住浣熊那样，一口咬住自己的难题不放。道德问题，他暂且搁起，留待从长计议。但眼下，他绝望地、斗牛犬式地把牙关咬紧，把脚步放慢、放实、放到斗牛犬那份固执。在皮特·琼斯家吃过一顿难以下咽的晚饭，他来到了拼字赛——汉娜没来，人人又为失窃的事激动，拼字赛开得兴味索然。半个晚上都耗在一小团一小团的窃窃私语里。皮特·琼斯把那位受害的「荷兰佬」置于自己特别的照管之下。

“I s’pose,” said Pete, “that them air fellers what robbed your house must a come down from Jinkins Run. They’re the blamedest set up there I ever see.”

“Ya‑as,” said Schroeder, “put how did Yinkins vellers know dat I sell te medder to te Shquire, hey? How tid Yinkins know anyting ’bout the Shquire’s bayin’ me dree huntert in te hard gash﻿—hey?”

“Some scoundrels down in these ’ere parts is a-layin’ in with Jinkins Run, I’ll bet a hoss,” said Pete. Ralph wondered whether he’d bet the one with the white left forefoot and the white nose. “Now,” said Pete, “ef I could find the feller that’s a-helpin’ them scoundrels rob us folks, I’d help stretch him to the neardest tree.”

“So vood I,” said Schroeder. “I’d shtretch him dill he baid me my dree huntert tollars pack, so I vood.”

And Betsey Short, who had found the whole affair very funny, was transported with a fit of tittering at poor Schroeder’s English. Ralph, fearing that his silence would excite suspicion, tried to talk. But he could not tell what he knew, and all that he said sounded so hollow and hypocritical that it made him feel guilty. And so he shut his mouth, and meditated profitably on the subject of bull dogs. And when later he overheard the garrulous Jones declare that he’d bet a hoss he could p’int out somebody as know’d a blamed sight more’n they keerd to tell, he made up his mind that if it came to p’inting out he should try to be even with Jones.

「俺琢磨，」皮特说，「抢你家的那帮家伙，准是从金金斯溪（Jinkins Run）那边下来的。那一带的货色，是俺这辈子见过最操蛋的一窝。」

「是——呀，」施罗德（Schroeder）说，「可是金金斯的伙计怎么地晓得我要把草场卖给乡绅，嘿？他们怎么地晓得乡绅要拿现款付我三百块——嘿？」

「咱们这地界里有些恶棍，跟金金斯溪是一伙的，俺敢拿一匹马打赌。」皮特说。拉尔夫暗想，不知他敢不敢拿那匹左前脚和鼻子都是白色的马来赌。「这么着，」皮特说，「要是叫俺逮着那个帮这帮恶棍抢咱们乡亲的家伙，俺乐意搭把手，把他吊上最近的一棵树。」

「我也一样，」施罗德说。「我要吊着他，吊到他赔我那三百块钱为止，我一定这么干。」

贝琪·肖特本就觉得整件事好笑得很，这会儿听可怜的施罗德讲英语，更是乐得一阵吃吃笑个不住。拉尔夫生怕自己一言不发惹人疑心，也试着搭话。可他知道的内情说不得，而说出口的每一句又都那么空洞虚伪，倒叫他自己都觉得心虚。于是他索性闭上嘴，就「斗牛犬」这个题目默想了一阵，颇有所得。后来，他又听见那张碎嘴子琼斯宣称：他敢拿一匹马打赌，有一个人知道的鬼名堂，比他们肯说出来的多出一大截——听到这儿，拉尔夫打定了主意：真到了指认的那一天，他要设法跟琼斯算个平手。
