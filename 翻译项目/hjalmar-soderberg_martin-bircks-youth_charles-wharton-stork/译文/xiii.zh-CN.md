### XIII / 十三

===Original===

It was dusk now, almost dark, and Martin was still sitting in his rocking-chair at the table and could not get up energy to light the lamp. There was a little wine left in the bottle; he poured it into his glass and drank. He had raised the window to let the smoke drift out, and through the trampling of feet which rose from below like the sound of a hundred ticking clocks he heard the house door open and close again and steps going off down the street﻿—they were Henrik’s. Martin thought about his love and what he had said about it, and he was at once struck with the fact that at the mere touch of this bit of reality his own love affair evaporated and was gone like mist and dream. Harriet Skottë.﻿ ﻿… He asked himself: “If I should read in the paper tomorrow that she was engaged or married, or that she was dead﻿—what would that signify to me? Nothing, no reality lost, no expectation gone to shipwreck﻿—just a mood burst, which would soon have burst anyhow.”

He took from his pocket the letter he had written, tore it open, and read it again. “I’ll burn it,” he thought﻿—“but why burn it? I may be able to use it sometime in a story.”

He tossed it into a table drawer among other manuscripts. Then he sank again into reverie.

这时已是黄昏，差不多黑了，马丁还坐在桌前的摇椅里，打不起精神去点灯。瓶里还剩一点酒；他倒进杯子，喝了。他把窗子推上去，好让烟散出去；楼下升起一片脚步声，像一百座钟滴答作响，透过这片声音，他听见楼门开了又关，脚步顺着街道远去——是亨里克的。马丁想着自己的爱情，想着亨利克方才说的那些话，随即吃了一惊：这一点点现实一碰，他自己的风流韵事竟像雾和梦一样蒸发掉了。哈丽特·斯科特。……他问自己：“倘若明天我在报上读到她订了婚、结了婚，或是死了——那对我意味着什么？什么也不意味，没有失去任何实在的东西，没有任何指望遭了难——只是一个心情破了，而这心情横竖很快也要破的。”

他从口袋里掏出那封写好的信，撕开封口，又读了一遍。“把它烧了吧，”他想——“可干吗烧掉？说不定哪天还能用在哪篇故事里。”

他把它丢进桌上一只抽屉，同别的稿子放在一起。随后他又沉入遐想。

===Original===

Suddenly his mother stood in the doorway. She held a lamp in her hand and was leaning forward, looking at him.

“You’re sitting in the dark,” she said. “Papa has gone out. May I sit with you here a while?”

Martin nodded. She set the lamp on the table, fetched a basket with her sewing, and sat down to sew.

She sat silent, bent over her work. At length she raised her eyes, large with tears and sleeplessness.

“Tell me, Martin,” she said; “you mustn’t be cross, but one day when you were out I couldn’t help pulling out a drawer of your table and glancing at your papers. Otherwise I should never know what you’re thinking about. And what I got hold of made me so worried that I had to sit down and cry. I didn’t understand it, I don’t know if it was supposed to be verse or what it was, but I thought it was only full of terrible blasphemies. I got so frightened, I almost thought for a moment that you were out of your head. I know I don’t understand anything, but so much I can still see, that you’ll never get anywhere with writing that way. You can write very finely, too, if you want to.”

Martin was silent. What should he answer? He divined, or at least supposed, that his mother had really wished to say something quite different, and that her saying he wouldn’t get on in the world was merely a forced expedient which she caught at when thoughts and words deserted her. She had of course felt and suspected that the poem she had found in the drawer was meant to be taken quite differently from the way she now feigned to think, she wanted him to explain himself, to talk to her about his thoughts. She was pounding at the door, “Let me in! don’t make me stand outside; I’m cold and it’s so lonely!” And yet he didn’t open the door, he couldn’t; he hadn’t fastened it, it had locked itself.

母亲忽然站在门口。她手里端着一盏灯，俯身望着他。

“你摸黑坐着，”她说，“爸爸出去了。我可以在这儿陪你坐一会儿吗？”

马丁点点头。她把灯放在桌上，取来针线篮，坐下做针线。

她默默地坐着，俯在活计上。末了她抬起眼睛，那双眼睛因为泪水和不眠而变大了。

“告诉我，马丁，”她说，“你可别恼：有一天你出去，我忍不住拉开你桌子的抽屉，看了几眼你的稿子。不然我永远不知道你在想什么。我看到的那些叫我这般担心，只得坐下来哭。我看不懂，不知道那算不算诗，还是别的什么，我只觉得满纸都是可怕的渎神的话。我吓坏了，有一阵几乎以为你疯了。我知道我什么都不懂，可这一点我还看得出来：你这样写下去，永远不会有什么出息。你想写的话，也能写得很漂亮。”

马丁不作声。他该回答什么呢？他猜到——至少设想——母亲真正想说的其实是另一回事，她说他在世上混不出头，只是情急之下抓住的权宜之计，那时她的思想和言语都丢下了她。她当然感觉得到、也猜得到，她在抽屉里找到的那首诗，本该按全然不同的方式去领会，而不是她现在装出的那种想法；她要他解释自己，要他把自己的思想讲给她听。她在捶门：“让我进去！别让我站在外头；我冷，这儿太孤单！”可他没有开门，他开不了；门不是他闩上的，它是自己锁上的。

What ought he to answer her? Her words had filled him with a deep discouragement. If he had any ambition, it was to write so that each and all who really cared to could understand him. He had no taste for any literary freemasonry; he did not believe in a literature for the élite, nor had he failed to observe how often it happened that no one wanted to be of the élite. Now it suddenly became clear to him how hopeless was his ideal: there was no art for all, there were no thoughts for all; on the contrary the simplest ideas in the clearest language were but seldom understood by others than those who were familiar beforehand with just that type of thought. How should he be able to speak with her about his thoughts, when her vocabulary, as the monotony of the years had developed it, did not even suffice to express what she herself thought and felt at the bottom of her heart? The god with whom his poem dealt was of course Spinoza’s god, the World Soul; but this god was merely an intellectual experiment, whereas hers﻿—his mother’s﻿—was at least a product of the imagination and as such had a bit more life and more blood. How should he explain that what she called blasphemies did not apply to her god? She would have answered that there was only one god. He knew all she would answer and say; therefore he remained silent and looked out of the window, listening to the Saturday tread of tired feet on the pavement, and the rain which began to fall against the windowpanes.

And as to what she had said about his future, what could he say? To that there was but one answer: to be successful, to become famous. And that answer he could not give. “If I win recognition some day,” he thought to himself, “a recognition such as would gratify her, it will be when she is no longer alive. So it always is. Why should I hope for an exception for her and me?” What was he to do? Ought he to put his arms around her neck, ought he to stroke her hair and kiss her? No, that wouldn’t seem natural. He didn’t care for that sort of deception and she didn’t either; he knew her; she wouldn’t be satisfied with that. She had asked, and it was an answer she awaited. He could answer nothing, and he was silent.

He was silent and felt at the same time how the silence burned in her breast, and though he could say nothing he sought instead with his glance to meet her eyes, those eyes which used to smile so bright and blue when they looked into his. It still happened sometimes in the midst of dinner or in the evening at the tea-table that she looked at him and nodded and smiled brightly as before, as mothers nod and smile to their little children before they are able to talk. Perhaps she had the feeling that time had gone in a circle, and that this smile was the only form of expression she still had in her power when she wished to communicate with her children. It was just so that he wished she could have looked at him and nodded and smiled, with a smile far beyond all the unimportant things which separated them.

But she did not smile now; she sat silent with hands crossed on her knees, and her eyes, generally so near to weeping, now stared tearless into the shadows as if they sought and asked, “Are all mothers as unhappy as I? As lonely? As deserted by their children?”

The lamp flame fluttered in the night wind. She rose and said good night, took the lamp, and went out.

===Chinese===

他该回答她什么呢？她的话使他充满深深的沮丧。他若还有什么抱负，那就是要写得让每一个真正愿意懂的人都能够懂他。他对任何文学的行会暗语都没有趣味；他不相信有什么为精英预备的文学，也不曾忽视这样的事多么常有：谁也不想做精英。此刻他忽然看清自己的理想多么无望：没有为所有人的艺术，没有为所有人的思想；恰恰相反，最简单的意思用最清楚的语言说出来，也难得被那些事先不熟悉这类思想的人所理解。她的词汇，随着年月的单调磨成了如今的样子，连表达她自己心底的所想所感都不够用，他还怎么能同她谈自己的思想呢？他诗里写的那个神当然是斯宾诺莎的神，世界灵魂；可那个神不过是一桩智力的试验，而她的——母亲的——神至少是想象的产物，因此反倒多一点血肉。他怎么解释她所谓的渎神的话并不针对她的神呢？她会回答：神只有一个。她要回答什么、要说什么他全知道；所以他保持沉默，望着窗外，听礼拜六疲倦的脚步踏过人行道，听雨点开始敲打窗玻璃。

至于她说的他的前途，他能说什么呢？那只有一个回答：成功，成名。而这个回答他给不出。“倘若有一天我赢得了承认，”他暗想，“赢得一种能叫她欢喜的承认，那时她已经不在人世了。事情从来如此。凭什么指望她和我就例外呢？”他怎么办呢？莫非搂住她的脖子，莫非抚她的头发、吻她？不，那显得不自然。他不欢喜那种欺哄，她也不欢喜；他了解她；那满足不了她。她问了，等的就是回答。他什么也回答不出，于是沉默。

他沉默着，同时觉出这沉默在她胸口里怎样烧着；他虽说不出话，却用目光去迎她的眼睛——那双眼睛从前望着他的时候，笑得又亮又蓝。如今有时在饭桌中间、或者傍晚的茶桌上，她还会那样望他、点头、明亮地微笑，像母亲对还不会说话的小孩子那样点头微笑。也许她有一种感觉：时间兜了一个圈子，当她想同儿女相通的时候，这种微笑是她仅剩的、还使得出的表达方式。他正希望她能那样望着他、点头微笑，那微笑远远越过把他们隔开的一切无关紧要的东西。

可她此刻没有笑；她默默地坐着，两手交叠在膝上，那双素来几乎含泪的眼睛，此刻无泪地瞪着暗处，仿佛在寻找、在发问：“做母亲的都像我这样不幸吗？这样孤单？这样被儿女撇下吗？”

灯焰在夜风里抖。她起身道了晚安，端起灯，出去了。
