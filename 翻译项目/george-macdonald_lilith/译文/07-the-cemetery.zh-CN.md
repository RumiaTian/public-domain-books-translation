## VII The Cemetery / 第七章 公墓
===Original===

The air as of an icehouse met me crossing the threshold. The door fell-to behind us. The sexton said something to his wife that made her turn toward us.﻿—What a change had passed upon her! It was as if the splendour of her eyes had grown too much for them to hold, and, sinking into her countenance, made it flash with a loveliness like that of Beatrice in the white rose of the redeemed. Life itself, life eternal, immortal, streamed from it, an unbroken lightning. Even her hands shone with a white radiance, every “pearl-shell helmet” gleaming like a moonstone. Her beauty was overpowering; I was glad when she turned it from me.
But the light of the candle reached such a little way, that at first I could see nothing of the place. Presently, however, it fell on something that glimmered, a little raised from the floor. Was it a bed? Could live thing sleep in such a mortal cold? Then surely it was no wonder it should not wake of itself! Beyond that appeared a fainter shine; and then I thought I descried uncertain gleams on every side.
A few paces brought us to the first; it was a human form under a sheet, straight and still﻿—whether of man or woman I could not tell, for the light seemed to avoid the face as we passed.
I soon perceived that we were walking along an aisle of couches, on almost every one of which, with its head to the passage, lay something asleep or dead, covered with a sheet white as snow. My soul grew silent with dread. Through aisle after aisle we went, among couches innumerable. I could see only a few of them at once, but they were on all sides, vanishing, as it seemed, in the infinite.﻿—Was it here lay my choice of a bed? Must I go to sleep among the unwaking, with no one to rouse me? Was this the sexton’s library? were these his books? Truly it was no halfway house, this chamber of the dead!
“One of the cellars I am placed to watch!” remarked Mr. Raven﻿—in a low voice, as if fearing to disturb his silent guests. “Much wine is set here to ripen!﻿—But it is dark for a stranger!” he added.
“The moon is rising; she will soon be here,” said his wife, and her clear voice, low and sweet, sounded of ancient sorrow long bidden adieu.
Even as she spoke the moon looked in at an opening in the wall, and a thousand gleams of white responded to her shine. But not yet could I descry beginning or end of the couches.

前行数步，我们来到了第一件物件前；那是一具白布覆体的人形，笔直而静穆——是男是女我无从知晓，因为当我们走过时，光线似乎特意避开了那张面孔。
我很快察觉，我们正漫步在一排排列席的卧榻走道间，几乎每一张榻席上，头朝过道，皆卧着某个沉睡或死去的存在，覆着如雪般洁白的殓布。我的灵魂在惊骇中陷入死寂。穿过一条又一条走道，我们穿行在数不胜数的卧榻之间。我一次只能看清其中的寥寥几张，但它们却绵延分布在四面八方，仿佛在无尽的虚空中渐渐隐没。——难道我的选榻之处就在这里？难道我必须在这些永不苏醒的亡者中睡去，且无人能唤醒我？这难道就是教堂司事的图书馆？这些难道就是他的藏书？诚然，这间亡者之殿绝非供人休憩的中途客栈！
“这是派我看守的酒窖之一！”渡鸦先生（Mr. Raven）低声说道，仿佛生怕惊扰了他静默的宾客，“许多美酒皆陈放于此以待成熟！——不过对于异乡人来说，这里未免太昏暗了！”他补充道。
“月亮正冉冉升起；她很快就会来到这里，”他的妻子说道，她那清澈的嗓音低沉而甜美，仿佛回荡着早已辞别的久远悲戚。
正当她说话间，月光从墙壁的一处裂口探视进来，千百道白芒呼应着她的清辉。然而，我依然无法看清那些卧榻的开端或尽头。

They stretched away and away, as if for all the disparted world to sleep upon. For along the far receding narrow ways, every couch stood by itself, and on each slept a lonely sleeper. I thought at first their sleep was death, but I soon saw it was something deeper still﻿—a something I did not know.
The moon rose higher, and shone through other openings, but I could never see enough of the place at once to know its shape or character; now it would resemble a long cathedral nave, now a huge barn made into a dwelling of tombs. She looked colder than any moon in the frostiest night of the world, and where she shone direct upon them, cast a bluish, icy gleam on the white sheets and the pallid countenances﻿—but it might be the faces that made the moon so cold!
Of such as I could see, all were alike in the brotherhood of death, all unlike in the character and history recorded upon them. Here lay a man who had died﻿—for although this was not death, I have no other name to give it﻿—in the prime of manly strength; his dark beard seemed to flow like a liberated stream from the glacier of his frozen countenance; his forehead was smooth as polished marble; a shadow of pain lingered about his lips, but only a shadow. On the next couch lay the form of a girl, passing lovely to behold. The sadness left on her face by parting was not yet absorbed in perfect peace, but absolute submission possessed the placid features, which bore no sign of wasting disease, of “killing care or grief of heart”: if pain had been there, it was long charmed asleep, never again to wake. Many were the beautiful that there lay very still﻿—some of them mere children; but I did not see one infant. The most beautiful of all was a lady whose white hair, and that alone, suggested her old when first she fell asleep. On her stately countenance rested﻿—not submission, but a right noble acquiescence, an assurance, firm as the foundations of the universe, that all was as it should be. On some faces lingered the almost obliterated scars of strife, the marrings of hopeless loss, the fading shadows of sorrows that had seemed inconsolable: the aurora of the great morning had not yet quite melted them away; but those faces were few, and everyone that bore such brand of pain seemed to plead, “Pardon me: I died only yesterday!” or, “Pardon me: I died but a century ago!”

That some had been dead for ages I knew, not merely by their unutterable repose, but by something for which I have neither word nor symbol.
We came at last to three empty couches, immediately beyond which lay the form of a beautiful woman, a little past the prime of life. One of her arms was outside the sheet, and her hand lay with the palm upward, in its centre a dark spot. Next to her was the stalwart figure of a man of middle age. His arm too was outside the sheet, the strong hand almost closed, as if clenched on the grip of a sword. I thought he must be a king who had died fighting for the truth.
“Will you hold the candle nearer, wife?” whispered the sexton, bending down to examine the woman’s hand.
“It heals well,” he murmured to himself: “the nail found in her nothing to hurt!”
At last I ventured to speak.
“Are they not dead?” I asked softly.
“I cannot answer you,” he replied in a subdued voice. “I almost forget what they mean by *dead* in the old world. If I said a person was dead, my wife would understand one thing, and you would imagine another.﻿—This is but one of my treasure vaults,” he went on, “and all my guests are not laid in vaults: out there on the moor they lie thick as the leaves of a forest after the first blast of your winter﻿—thick, let me say rather, as if the great white rose of heaven had shed its petals over it. All night the moon reads their faces, and smiles.”
“But why leave them in the corrupting moonlight?” I asked.
“Our moon,” he answered, “is not like yours﻿—the old cinder of a burnt-out world; her beams embalm the dead, not corrupt them. You observe that here the sexton lays his dead on the earth; he buries very few under it! In your world he lays huge stones on them, as if to keep them down; I watch for the hour to ring the resurrection-bell, and wake those that are still asleep. Your sexton looks at the clock to know when to ring the dead-alive to church; I hearken for the cock on the spire to crow; ‘*Awake, thou that sleepest, and arise from the dead*!’ ”
I began to conclude that the self-styled sexton was in truth an insane parson: the whole thing was too mad! But how was I to get away from it? I was helpless! In this world of the dead, the raven and his wife were the only living I had yet seen: whither should I turn for help?

在死者丛中折返回去的那条路是何等漫长！起初我愤怒得忘却了恐惧，但随着我逐渐平静下来，那些静止的身形变得越发狰狞可怖。最终，伴随着对那优雅寂静的刺耳冒犯，我狂奔起来，疯狂逃窜，冲出大门，将门在身后重重甩合。它带着一种可怖的死寂闭合了。
我伫立在漆黑一团的幽暗之中。我摸索着四周，摸到了一扇门，将其推开，便察觉到一盏孤灯的昏黄微光。我已站在自己的图书馆（library）里，手中正握着那扇暗门的把手。
我究竟是从一场幻象中回到了现实？——还是因折返而彻底迷失了自我？哪一个才是真实的——是我此刻所见的，还是我刚刚失去踪迹的？两者是否可能皆是真实的，彼此交融渗透却又互不混淆？
我一头栽倒在一张长沙发上，沉沉睡去。
图书馆东面有一扇小窗，每年这个时节，清晨的第一缕晨曦会透过它照射在镜子上，再由镜面反射到暗门之上：当我苏醒时，阳光正映照在那里，将我的视线吸引了过去。心中涌起一种强烈的预感，觉得那扇门后必定依然连着我方才逃离的那座无垠之殿，我一跃而起，拉开了暗门。光线如同一只迫不及待的猎犬，先我一步窜入壁橱，猛地扑在了一本大书的镀金书口上。
“哪个蠢才，”我叫喊道，“把书反着塞进书架里了？”
然而，那镀金的书口再次反射了光线，将光斑投射在昏暗角落的一组抽屉上，我看见其中一个抽屉半开着。
“又在胡乱捣鬼！”我叫道，走上前去打算合上抽屉。
里面塞满了旧文件，似乎装得过满，怎么也关不上。
