## XXX Where Ignorance Is Bliss, ’Tis Folly to Be Wise / 第30章 无知是福，聪明反被聪明误

===Original===
When by myself, I fretted so constantly that the traces it left upon me became evident even to the dull comprehension of Mrs. M’Swat.

===Chinese===
独自一人时，我无时无刻不在烦恼，那烦恼在我身上留下的印痕，连麦斯沃特太太那迟钝的悟性也看得分明。

===Original===
“I don’t hold with too much pleasure and disherpation, but you ain’t had overmuch of it lately. You’ve stuck at home pretty constant, and ye and Lizer can have a little fly round. It’ll do yous good,” she said.

===Chinese===
“寻欢作乐、‘散心耗神’的事儿太多，我是不赞成的，不过你近来也实在没沾过多少。你在家守得够久也够安分了，你跟莱泽（Lizer）可以出去小小地撒个欢儿。这对你们有好处，”她说。

===Original===
The dissipation, pleasure, and flying round allotted to “Lizer” and me were to visit some of the neighbours. Those, like the M’Swats, were sheep-farming selectors. They were very friendly and kind to me, and I found them superior to my employers, in that their houses were beautifully clean; but they lived the same slow life, and their soul’s existence fed on the same small ideas. I was keenly disappointed that none of them had a piano, as my hunger for music could be understood only by one with a passion for that art.

===Chinese===
派给“莱泽”和我的那点儿作乐、享受和撒欢儿，不过是去拜访几家邻居。这些人家同麦斯沃特家一样，都是养羊的选地农。他们待我非常友好和善，而且我发觉他们比我的东家强——强在屋子里拾掇得漂漂亮亮、干干净净；可他们过的同样是那种慢吞吞的日子，灵魂的生计照旧靠同样狭小的念头喂养。他们谁家都没有钢琴，这叫我大失所望——我对音乐的饥渴，唯有对这门艺术怀有一腔热恋的人才懂得。

===Original===
I borrowed something to read, but all that I could get in the way of books were a few *Young Ladies’ Journals*, which I devoured ravenously, so to speak.

===Chinese===
我借了些东西读，可弄到手的书报无非几册《青年女士杂志》（*Young Ladies’ Journals*），不妨说，我是把它们如饥似渴地狼吞虎咽了一气。

===Original===
When Lizer’s back would be turned, the girls would ask me how I managed to live at Barney’s Gap, and expressed themselves of the opinion that it was the most horrible hole in the world, and Mrs. M’Swat the dirtiest creature living, and that they would not go there for £50 a week. I made a point of never saying anything against Mrs. M’Swat; but I fumed inwardly that this life was forced upon me, when girls with no longings or aspirations beyond being the wife of a Peter M’Swat recoiled from the thought of it.

===Chinese===
每当莱泽转过背去，姑娘们便问我，我到底是怎么在巴尼山口活得下去的，还纷纷发表高见，说那是天底下最可怕的一个窟窿，说麦斯沃特太太是世上顶腌臜的活物，说一个星期给 50 镑她们也不肯去。我给自己立下规矩，从不说麦斯沃特太太一句坏话；可我心里暗暗冒火：那些除了嫁给彼得·麦斯沃特再无半点渴望、半点志气的姑娘，尚且一想起来就打怵，这样的生活却硬是压到了我头上。

===Original===
My mother insisted upon my writing to her regularly, so once a week I headed a letter “Black’s Camp,” and condemned the place, while mother as unfailingly replied that these bad times I should be thankful to God that I was fed and clothed. I knew this as well as anyone, and was aware there were plenty of girls willing to jump at my place; but they were of different temperament to me, and when one is seventeen, that kind of reasoning does not weigh very heavily.

===Chinese===
母亲坚持要我按时给她写信，于是每星期我都在信头上题作“黑人营”（Black's Camp），把那地方数落一顿；母亲也从不失约地回信说，这样艰难的年月，我有吃有穿，就该感谢上帝。这个道理我同谁一样明白，也知道多的是姑娘巴不得顶我的缺；可她们同我脾性不同，况且一个人十七岁的时候，这类道理是压不了多重的。

===Original===
> Why the deuce don’t you give up writing those letters to mother? We get tongue-pie on account of them, and it’s not as if they did you any good. She says you are that conceited you think you ought to have something better, and you’re not fit for the place you have, and she’s glad it is such a place, and it will do you the world of good and take the nonsense out of you﻿—that it’s time you got a bit of sense. Sullivan’s Ginger. After she gets your letters she does jaw, and wishes she never had a child, and what a good mother she is, and what bad devils we are to her. You are a fool not to stay where you are. I wish I could get away to M’Swat or Mack Pot, and I would jump at the chance like a good un. The boss still sprees and loafs about town till someone has to go and haul him home. I’m about full of him, and I’m going to leave home before next Christmas, or my name ain’t what it is. Mother says the kiddies would starve if I leave; but Stanley is coming on like a haystack, I tell him, and he does kick up, and he ought to be able to plough next time. I ploughed when I was younger than him. I put in fourteen acres of wheat and oats this year, and I don’t think I’ll cut a wheelbarrow-load of it. I’m full of the place. I never have a single penny to my name, and it ain’t father’s drinking that’s all to blame; if he didn’t booze it wouldn’t be much better. It’s the slowest hole in the world, and I’ll chuck it and go shearing or droving. I hate this dairying, it’s too slow for a funeral: there would be more life in trapping possums out on Timlinbilly. Mother always says to have patience, and when the drought breaks and good seasons come round again things will be better, but it’s no good of trying to stuff me like that. I remember when the seasons were wet. It was no good growing anything, because everyone grew so much that there was no market, and the sheep died of foot-rot and you couldn’t give your butter away, and it is not much worse to have nothing to sell than not be able to sell a thing when you have it. And the long and short of it is that I hate dairying like blue murder. It’s as tame as a clucking hen. Fancy a cove sitting down every morning and evening pulling at a cow’s tits fit to bust himself, and then turning an old separator, and washing it up in a dish of water like a blooming girl’s work. And if you go to a picnic, just when the fun commences you have to nick off home and milk, and when you tog yourself on Sunday evening you have to undress again and lay into the milking, and then you have to change everything on you and have a bath, or your best girl would scent the cow-yard on you, and not have you within cooee of her. We won’t know what rain is when we see it; but I suppose it will come in floods and finish the little left by the drought. The grasshoppers have eaten all the fruit and even the bark off the trees, and the caterpillars made a croker of the few tomatoes we kept alive with the suds. All the cockeys round here and dad are applying to the Government to have their rents suspended for a time. We have not heard yet whether it will be granted, but if Gov. doesn’t like it, they’ll have to lump it, for none of us have a penny to bless ourselves with, let alone dub up for taxes. I’ve written you a long letter, and if you growl about the spelling and grammar I won’t write to you any more, so there, and you take my tip and don’t write to mother on that flute any more, for she won’t take a bit of notice.
> Yr loving brother,
> Horace.
