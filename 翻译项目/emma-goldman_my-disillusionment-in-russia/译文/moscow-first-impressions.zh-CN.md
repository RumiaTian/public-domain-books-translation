## IV Moscow: First Impressions / 四、莫斯科：初印象
===Original===

Coming from Petrograd to Moscow is like being suddenly transferred from a desert to active life, so great is the contrast. On reaching the large open square in front of the main Moscow station I was amazed at the sight of busy crowds, cabbies, and porters. The same picture presented itself all the way from the station to the Kremlin. The streets were alive with men, women, and children. Almost everybody carried a bundle, or dragged a loaded sleigh. There was life, motion, and movement, quite different from the stillness that oppressed me in Petrograd.
I noticed considerable display of the military in the city, and scores of men dressed in leather suits with guns in their belts. “Cheka men, our Extraordinary Commission,” explained Radek. I had heard of the Cheka before: Petrograd talked of it with dread and hatred. However, the soldiers and Chekists were never much in evidence in the city on the Neva. Here in Moscow they seemed everywhere. Their presence reminded me of a remark Jack Reed had made: “Moscow is a military encampment,” he had said; “spies everywhere, the bureaucracy most autocratic. I always feel relieved when I get out of Moscow. But, then, Petrograd is a proletarian city and is permeated with the spirit of the Revolution. Moscow always was hierarchical. It is much more so now.” I found that Jack Reed was right. Moscow was indeed hierarchical. Still the life was intense, varied, and interesting. What struck me most forcibly, besides the display of militarism, was the preoccupation of the people. There seemed to be no common interest between them. Everyone rushed about as a detached unit in quest of his own, pushing and knocking against everyone else. Repeatedly I saw women or children fall from exhaustion without anyone stopping to lend assistance. People stared at me when I would bend over the heap on the slippery pavement or gather up the bundles that had fallen into the street. I spoke to friends about what looked to me like a strange lack of fellow-feeling. They explained it as a result partly of the general distrust and suspicion created by the Cheka, and partly due to the absorbing task of getting the day’s food. One had neither vitality nor feeling left to think of others. Yet there did not seem to be such a scarcity of food as in Petrograd, and the people were warmer and better dressed.

从彼得格勒到莫斯科，就像从一片荒漠骤然转入忙碌的人间，反差就是这么大。一到莫斯科总站前的大广场，熙攘的人群、马车夫、搬运夫便令我惊叹。从车站到克里姆林宫，一路都是这番景象。街上满是男人、女人、孩子，几乎人人挟着包袱，或拖着满载的雪橇。一派生机与奔忙，与彼得格勒压得人喘不过气的死寂迥然不同。

我注意到城里到处是军人，还有成群穿皮衣、腰插手枪的人。「契卡的人，我们的特别委员会，」拉杰克解释道。契卡我早有耳闻：彼得格勒人谈起它，又是恐惧又是仇恨。不过在涅瓦河畔那座城，士兵和契卡分子并不常见。可在莫斯科，他们似乎无处不在。这景象使我想起杰克·里德说过的话：「莫斯科是座兵营，」他说，「到处是密探，官僚最专横。我一出莫斯科就松口气。不过话说回来，彼得格勒是座无产者的城，浸透革命精神；莫斯科从来等级森严，如今更甚。」我发现杰克·里德说得对，莫斯科确实等级森严。但这里的生活紧张、多样、有趣。除了军国气象，最震动我的是人人的自顺：彼此之间似乎毫无共同的关切，人人都像一个孤立的单元，各自奔各自的，互相推搡冲撞。我不止一次看见妇女或孩子累倒在地，无人驻足相助；我去扶那倒在结滑的人行道上的人、去拾散落街心的包袱，人们还直盯着我看。我对朋友们说起这种在我看来奇异的冷漠。他们解释说，部分是契卡造成的普遍猜忌与怀疑，部分是因为觅食这件大事把人吸干了：连想一想别人的气力和心思都不剩了。可莫斯科的食物似乎并不像彼得格勒那样匮乏，人也穿得暖些、好些。

I spent much time on the streets and in the marketplaces. Most of the latter, as also the famous Soukharevka, were in full operation. Occasionally soldiers would raid the markets; but as a rule they were suffered to continue. They presented the most vital and interesting part of the city’s life. Here gathered proletarian and aristocrat, Communist and bourgeois, peasant and intellectual. Here they were bound by the common desire to sell and buy, to trade and bargain. Here one could find for sale a rusty iron pot alongside of an exquisite icon; an old pair of shoes and intricately worked lace; a few yards of cheap calico and a beautiful old Persian shawl. The rich of yesterday, hungry and emaciated, denuding themselves of their last glories; the rich of today buying﻿—it was indeed an amazing picture in revolutionary Russia.
Who was buying the finery of the past, and where did the purchasing power come from? The buyers were numerous. In Moscow one was not so limited as to sources of information as in Petrograd; the very streets furnished that source.
The Russian people even after four years of war and three years of revolution remained unsophisticated. They were suspicious of strangers and reticent at first. But when they learned that one had come from America and did not belong to the governing political party, they gradually lost their reserve. Much information I gathered from them and some explanation of the things that perplexed me since my arrival. I talked frequently with the workers and peasants and the women on the markets.
The forces which had led up to the Russian Revolution had remained terra incognita to these simple folk, but the Revolution itself had struck deep into their souls. They knew nothing of theories, but they believed that there was to be no more of the hated *barin*3 and now the *barin* was again upon them. “The *barin* has everything,” they would say, “white bread, clothing, even chocolate, while we have nothing.” “Communism, equality, freedom,” they jeered, “lies and deception.”
I would return to the National bruised and battered, my illusions gradually shattered, my foundations crumbling. But I would not let go.

我大量时间泡在街头和集市上。多数集市，连同著名的苏哈列夫卡，都在照常营业。偶尔士兵会去查抄，但一般都听之任之。集市是这城市最有生气、最有味道的部分：无产者与贵族、共产党人与资产阶级、农民与知识分子，齐聚于此，被同一个欲望系在一起——卖与买，交易与讨价还价。在这里，一只生锈的铁锅旁边可以摆着一尊精美的圣像；一双旧鞋旁边是花工繁复的蕾丝；几码便宜印花布旁边，是一条漂亮的波斯旧披肩。昨天的富人，饥肠辘辘、形容枯槁，正剥下身上最后一点体面；今天的富人，在旁边买进——革命俄国的奇景。

是谁在买进这些昔日的华服？购买力从何而来？买主为数众多。在莫斯科，消息来源不像彼得格勒那样闭塞；街道本身就供着消息。

俄国人民历经四年战争、三年革命，仍旧质朴。他们初见生人存有戒心、言语迟疑，可一旦得知来人来自美国、又不属于执政党，便渐渐卸下拘束。我从他们那里收集到许多资讯，也解开了抵俄以来困扰我的某些疑问。我常同工人、农民和集市上的妇女交谈。

把俄国革命推到今天的种种力量，对这些单纯的人是化外之地，但革命本身扎进了他们灵魂深处。理论他们一无所知，可他们相信从此再不会有可恨的*老爷*3——如今老爷又骑到了他们头上。「老爷什么都有，」他们说，「白面包、衣裳，连巧克力都有，我们什么都没有。」「共产主义、平等、自由，」他们讥讽道，「全是谎言和骗局。」

我一次次回到国民饭店，遍体鳞伤，幻想渐渐破灭，信念的地基渐渐崩塌。但我不肯放手。

After all, I thought, the common people could not understand the tremendous difficulties confronting the Soviet Government: the imperialist forces arraigned against Russia, the many attacks which drained her of her men who otherwise would be employed in productive labour, the blockade which was relentlessly slaying Russia’s young and weak. Of course, the people could not understand these things, and I must not be misled by their bitterness born of suffering. I must be patient. I must get to the source of the evils confronting me.
The National, like the Petrograd Astoria, was a former hotel but not nearly in as good condition. No rations were given out there except three quarters of a pound of bread every two days. Instead there was a common dining room where dinners and suppers were served. The meals consisted of soup and a little meat, sometimes fish or pancakes, and tea. In the evening we usually had kasha and tea. The food was not too plentiful, but one could exist on it were it not so abominably prepared.
I saw no reason for this spoiling of provisions. Visiting the kitchen I discovered an array of servants controlled by a number of officials, commandants, and inspectors. The kitchen staff were poorly paid; moreover, they were not given the same food served to us. They resented this discrimination and their interest was not in their work. This situation resulted in much graft and waste, criminal in the face of the general scarcity of food. Few of the tenants of the National, I learned, took their meals in the common dining room. They prepared or had their meals prepared by servants in a separate kitchen set aside for that purpose. There, as in the Astoria, I found the same scramble for a place on the stove, the same bickering and quarrelling, the same greedy, envious watching of each other. Was that Communism in action, I wondered. I heard the usual explanation: Yudenich, Denikin, Kolchak, the blockade﻿—but the stereotyped phrases no longer satisfied me.
Before I left Petrograd Jack Reed said to me: “When you reach Moscow, look up Angelica Balabanova. She will receive you gladly and will put you up should you be unable to find a room.” I had heard of Balabanova before, knew of her work, and was naturally anxious to meet her.
A few days after reaching Moscow I called her up. Would she see me? Yes, at once, though she was not feeling well.

毕竟，我想，普通百姓理解不了苏维埃政府面对的巨大困难：帝国主义势力联合对付俄国，一次次进攻抽干了她的精壮劳力，封锁正无情地杀死俄国的幼弱。百姓当然不懂这些，我不能被他们苦难中生出的怨愤带偏。我必须有耐心，必须找到眼前弊害的根源。

国民饭店同彼得格勒的阿斯托里亚一样是旧旅馆改的，状况却差得多。这里不发口粮，只有每两天四分之三磅面包，另设公共食堂供应午饭晚饭：一份汤、一点肉，有时是鱼或薄饼，加茶；晚上通常是荞麦粥和茶。饭食不算丰盛，若不是做得那么恶劣，本也够活命。

我不明白粮食为何精蹋成这样。去厨房一看，只见一大队仆役，归好几个官员、管理员、督查员管着。厨房工人工钱微薄，吃的又同我们两样，他们怨恨这种差别，心思全不在活计上。于是贪污与浪费丛生——在普遍缺粮的当口，简直是犯罪。我又得知，国民饭店的房客很少在公共食堂用膳，他们自办伙食，或让仆役在专设的小厨房里做。那里同阿斯托里亚一样，抢炉灶、拌嘴吵架、彼此贪婪地眼红窥伺。这就是实行中的共产主义？我纳闷。我听着那套熟透了的解释：尤登尼奇、邓尼金、高尔察克、封锁——可这些套话已满足不了我。

离开彼得格勒前，杰克·里德对我说：「到了莫斯科，去找安热莉卡·巴拉班诺娃。她会高兴接待你，你找不到房间还能住她那儿。」巴拉班诺娃其人其事我早有耳闻，自然急于一见。

抵莫斯科几天后，我给她去了电话。她愿意见我吗？愿意，立刻就来——虽然她正不舒服。

I found Balabanova in a small, cheerless room, lying huddled up on the sofa. She was not prepossessing but for her eyes, large and luminous, radiating sympathy and kindness. She received me most graciously, like an old friend, and immediately ordered the inevitable samovar. Over our tea we talked of America, the labour movement there, our deportation, and finally about Russia. I put to her the questions I had asked many Communists regarding the contrasts and discrepancies which confronted me at every step. She surprised me by not giving the usual excuses; she was the first who did not repeat the old refrain. She did refer to the scarcity of food, fuel, and clothing which was responsible for much of the graft and corruption; but on the whole she thought life itself mean and limited. “A rock on which the highest hopes are shattered. Life thwarts the best intentions and breaks the finest spirits,” she said. Rather an unusual view for a Marxian, a Communist, and one in the thick of the battle. I knew she was then secretary of the Third International. Here was a personality, one who was not a mere echo, one who felt deeply the complexity of the Russian situation. I went away profoundly impressed, and attracted by her sad, luminous eyes.
I soon discovered that Balabanova﻿—or Balabanoff, as she preferred to be called﻿—was at the beck and call of everybody. Though poor in health and engaged in many functions, she yet found time to minister to the needs of her legion callers. Often she went without necessaries herself, giving away her own rations, always busy trying to secure medicine or some little delicacy for the sick and suffering. Her special concern were the stranded Italians of whom there were quite a number in Petrograd and Moscow. Balabanova had lived and worked in Italy for many years until she almost became Italian herself. She felt deeply with them, who were as far away from their native soil as from events in Russia. She was their friend, their advisor, their main support in a world of strife and struggle. Not only the Italians but almost everyone else was the concern of this remarkable little woman: no one needed a Communist membership card to Angelica’s heart. No wonder some of her comrades considered her a “sentimentalist who wasted her precious time in philanthropy.”

我见到巴拉班诺娃时，她正蜷在一间不见天日的小屋里沙发上。她算不上动人，唯独那双眼睛又大又亮，放着同情与慈和的光。她像老友一样亲切地接待我，立刻吩咐摆上那少不了的茶炊。就着茶，我们谈美国、谈那边的劳工运动、谈我们的被驱逐，最后谈到俄国。我把对许多共产党人问过的那些问题又端出来——我在在处处碰到的反差与扞格。她没有搬出惯常的遁词，令我意外；她是第一个不弹老调的人。她当然也提到食物、燃料、衣着的匮乏是诸多贪污腐化的原因，但总的说来，她觉得生活本身卑微而逼仄。「一块撞碎最高希望的岩石。生活挫败最好的意图，压垮最好的灵魂。」她说。对一个马克思主义者、共产党人、且身处激流中心的人来说，这见解可真不寻常。我知道她当时是第三国际的书记。这是一位真正的人物，不是回声，一位深切感受到俄国局势之复杂的人。我离去时深受触动，忘不了她那双忧伤而明亮的眼睛。

我很快发现，巴拉班诺娃——或者照她喜欢的叫法，巴拉班诺夫——人人都可以使唤她。她身体孱弱，兼职累累，却总有时间照应她那成群的来访者。她自己常常缺这少那，把口粮都送了人，整天忙着为病人和受难者弄药品、弄点可口的东西。她特别牵挂那些流落的意大利人——彼得格勒和莫斯科有为数不少的一批。巴拉班诺娃在意大利生活工作多年，几乎自己也成了意大利人。她深深体恤这些人：他们既远离故土，也与俄国的事隔膜。她是他们的朋友、顾问，是这争斗世界里他们的主心骨。不止意大利人，几乎人人都是这位了不起的小个子妇女的牵挂：进安热莉卡的心，不需要共产党党证。难怪有些同志说她是个「把宝贵时间浪费在慈善上的温情主义者」。

Many verbal battles I had on this score with the type of Communist who had become callous and hard, altogether barren of the qualities which characterized the Russian idealist of the past.
Similar criticism as of Balabanova I heard expressed of another leading Communist, Lunacharsky. Already in Petrograd I was told sneeringly, “Lunacharsky is a scatterbrain who wastes millions on foolish ventures.” But I was eager to meet the man who was the Commissar of one of the important departments in Russia, that of education. Presently an opportunity presented itself.
The Kremlin, the old citadel of Tsardom, I found heavily guarded and inaccessible to the “common” man. But I had come by appointment and in the company of a man who had an admission card, and therefore passed the guard without trouble. We soon reached the Lunacharsky apartments, situated in an old quaint building within the walls. Though the reception room was crowded with people waiting to be admitted, Lunacharsky called me in as soon as I was announced.
His greeting was very cordial. Did I “intend to remain a free bird” was one of his first questions, or would I be willing to join him in his work? I was rather surprised. Why should one have to give up his freedom, especially in educational work? Were not initiative and freedom essential? However, I had come to learn from Lunacharsky about the revolutionary system of education in Russia, of which we had heard so much in America. I was especially interested in the care the children were receiving. The Moscow *Pravda*, like the Petrograd newspapers, had been agitated by a controversy about the treatment of the morally defective. I expressed surprise at such an attitude in Soviet Russia. “Of course, it is all barbarous and antiquated,” Lunacharsky said, “and I am fighting it tooth and nail. The sponsors of prisons for children are old criminal jurists, still imbued with Tsarist methods. I have organized a commission of physicians, pedagogues, and psychologists to deal with this question. Of course, those children must not be punished.” I felt tremendously relieved. Here at last was a man who had gotten away from the cruel old methods of punishment. I told him of the splendid work done in capitalist America by Judge Lindsay and of some of the experimental schools for backward children. Lunacharsky was much interested.

为此，我同那种变得麻木冷硬的共产党人打过多少口水仗——旧日俄国理想主义者的那些品格，在他们身上荡然无存。

对另一位共产党要人卢那察尔斯基，我听到过类似的非议。早在彼得格勒，就有人讥讽地告诉我：「卢那察尔斯基是个败家子，把几百万糟蹋在荒唐事上。」但我渴望见见这位执掌俄国一个要害部门——教育——的人民委员。不久机会来了。

克里姆林宫这座沙皇制度的旧堡垒，戒备森严，「平民」不得其门而入。我是应约而来，又有持入宫证的人作伴，便毫无阻碍地过了岗哨，很快到了卢那察尔斯基的寓所——宫墙内一座古雅的老楼。接待室里挤满候见的人，但一通报我的名字，卢那察斯基立刻叫我进去。

他的问候极为热诚。头几个问题之一是：我打算继续做「自由之鸟」呢，还是愿意同他一起工作？我颇为诧异：为什么人就得放弃自由，尤其是在教育工作里？主动精神与自由难道不是命脉？不过，我是来向卢那察尔斯基请教俄国革命教育制度的——在美国我们听得太多了。我尤其关心儿童受到的照护。莫斯科《真理报》同彼得格勒各报一样，正为如何对待道德缺陷儿童闹得沸沸扬扬。我对苏维埃俄国有这种论调表示惊讶。「那当然又野蛮又陈腐，」卢那察尔斯基说，「我正拼命斗它。主张给孩子坐牢的是一班老刑事法学家，满脑子还是沙皇那套。我已组织了一个由医生、教育家和心理学家组成的委员会来处理这个问题。这些孩子当然不能惩罚。」我大大松了口气：终于有一个人挣脱了惩罚的残酷旧法。我对他讲了林赛法官在资本主义美国的出色工作，讲了几所收容迟钝儿童的试验学校。卢那察尔斯基极感兴趣。

“Yes, that is just what we want here, the American system of education,” he exclaimed. “You surely do not mean the American public school system?” I asked. “You know of the insurgent movement in America against our public school method of education, the work done by Professor Dewey and others?” Lunacharsky had heard little about it. Russia had been so long cut off from the western world and there was great lack of books on modern education. He was eager to learn of the new ideas and methods. I sensed in Lunacharsky a personality full of faith and devotion to the Revolution, one who was carrying on the great work of education in a physically and spiritually difficult environment.
He suggested the calling of a conference of teachers if I would talk to them about the new tendencies in education in America, to which I readily consented. Schools and other institutions in his charge were to be visited later. I left Lunacharsky filled with new hope. I would join him in his work, I thought. What greater service could one render the Russian people?
During my visit to Moscow I saw Lunacharsky several times. He was always the same kindly gracious man, but I soon began to notice that he was being handicapped in his work by forces within his own party: most of his good intentions and decisions never saw the light. Evidently Lunacharsky was caught in the same machine that apparently held everything in its iron grip. What was that machine? Who directed its movements?
Although the control of visitors at the National was very strict, no one being able to go in or out without a special *propusk*,4 men and women of different political factions managed to call on me: Anarchists, Left Social Revolutionists, Cooperators, and people I had known in America and who had returned to Russia to play their part in the Revolution. They had come with deep faith and high hope, but I found almost all of them discouraged, some even embittered. Though widely differing in their political views, nearly all of my callers related an identical story, the story of the high tide of the Revolution, of the wonderful spirit that led the people forward, of the possibilities of the masses, the role of the Bolsheviki as the spokesmen of the most extreme revolutionary slogans and their betrayal of the Revolution after they had secured power.

「对，这正是我们这儿要的，美国的教育制度！」他叫道。「您总不是指美国的公立学校制度吧？」我问，「您知道美国国内反对我们公立学校教育方式的革新运动，杜威教授等人的工作吧？」卢那察尔斯基对此所知甚少。俄国与西方世界隔绝太久，现代教育的书籍极缺。他急于了解新思想新方法。我在卢那察尔斯基身上感到一种对革命满腔信念与挚诚的人格，一个在物质与精神都艰难的环境里挑着教育大梁的人。

他提议召集一个教师会议，由我讲讲美国教育的新趋向，我欣然应允。他管辖的学校和其他机构容后再看。离开时我满心新的希望：我要同他一起工作了，我想——为国国人民效力，还有比这更大的吗？
