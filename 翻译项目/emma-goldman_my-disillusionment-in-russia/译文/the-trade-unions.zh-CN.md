## XV The Trade Unions / 十五、工会
===Original===

It was the month of June and the time of our departure was approaching. Petrograd seemed more beautiful than ever; the white nights had come﻿—almost broad daylight without its glare, the mysterious soothing white nights of Petrograd. There were rumours of counterrevolutionary danger and the city was guarded against attack. Martial law prevailing, it was forbidden to be out on the streets after 1 a.m., even though it was almost daylight. Occasionally special permits were obtained by friends and then we would walk through the deserted streets or along the banks of the dark Neva, discussing in whispers the perplexing situation. I sought for some outstanding feature in the blurred picture﻿—the Russian Revolution, a huge flame shooting across the world illuminating the black horizon of the disinherited and oppressed﻿—the Revolution, the new hope, the great spiritual awakening. And here I was in the midst of it, yet nowhere could I see the promise and fulfilment of the great event. Had I misunderstood the meaning and nature of revolution? Perhaps the wrong and the evil I have seen during those five months were inseparable from a revolution. Or was it the political machine which the Bolsheviki have created﻿—is that the force which is crushing the Revolution? If I had witnessed the birth of the latter I should now be better able to judge. But apparently I arrived at the end﻿—the agonizing end of a people. It is all so complex, so impenetrable, a *tupik*, a blind alley, as the Russians call it. Only time and earnest study, aided by sympathetic understanding, will show me the way out. Meanwhile, I must keep up my courage and﻿—away from Petrograd, out among the people.
Presently the long-awaited moment arrived. On June 30, 1920, our car was coupled to a slow train called “Maxim Gorky,” and we pulled out of the Nikolayevski station, bound for Moscow.
In Moscow there were many formalities to go through with. We thought a few days would suffice, but we remained two weeks. However, our stay was interesting. The city was alive with delegates to the Second Congress of the Third International; from all parts of the world the workers had sent their comrades to the promised land, revolutionary Russia, the first republic of the workers.

===Chinese===

时值六月，我们动身的日子近了。彼得格勒比以往任何时候都美；白夜来了——近乎白昼却没有烈日的那种光，彼得格勒神秘而安详的白夜。反革命危险的传闻不断，全城严防攻击。戒严令下，凌晨一点后不得上街，尽管天几乎还是亮的。偶尔朋友们弄到特别通行证，我们便在空寂的街上、在黝黑的涅瓦河岸散步，低声讨论着令人困惑的局势。我在模糊的图景中寻找一个凸出的焦点——俄国革命，一道射向世界的巨大火焰，照亮被剥夺者和被压迫者的黑色地平线——革命，新的希望，伟大的精神觉醒。而我正身处其中，却到处看不到这伟大事件的许诺与实现。是我误解了革命的意义与本质吗？也许我五个月来所见的不义与罪恶，本就与革命不可分。抑或是布尔什维克造出的那台政治机器——碾碎革命的正是它？倘若我目击的是革命诞生，如今评判起来会更有把握。可我显然到得太晚——赶上的是一个民族弥留的痛苦尽头。这一切太复杂、太不可测，一个*tupik*、一条死胡同——俄国人这么叫它。唯有时间加上认真的研究，辅以同情的理解，会给我指出路。眼下，我必须鼓起勇气，并且——离开彼得格勒，到人民中间去。

盼望已久的时刻终于到了。1920 年 6 月 30 日，我们的车厢挂上一列叫「马克西姆·高尔基」的慢车，驶出尼古拉耶夫斯基车站，开往莫斯科。

在莫斯科要办的手续很多。本以为几天够了，结果待了两个星期。不过此行很有意思：城里到处是来参加第三国际第二次代表大会的代表。世界各地的工人把同志们派到应许之地——革命的俄国，第一个工人共和国。

===Original===

Among the delegates there were also Anarchists and syndicalists who believed as firmly as I did six months previously that the Bolsheviki were the symbol of the Revolution. They had responded to the Moscow call with enthusiasm. Some of them I had met in Petrograd and now they were eager to hear of my experiences and learn my opinions. But what was I to tell them, and would they believe me if I did? Would I have believed any adverse criticism before I came to Russia? Besides, I felt that my views regarding the Bolsheviki were still too unformed, too vague, a conglomeration of mere impressions. My old values had been shattered and so far I have been unable to replace them. I could therefore not speak on the fundamental questions, but I did inform my friends that the Moscow and Petrograd prisons were crowded with Anarchists and other revolutionists, and I advised them not to content themselves with the official explanations but to investigate for themselves. I warned them that they would be surrounded by guides and interpreters, most of them men of the Cheka, and that they would not be able to learn the facts unless they made a determined, independent effort.
There was considerable excitement in Moscow at the time. The Printers’ Union had been suppressed and its entire managing board sent to prison. The Union had called a public meeting to which members of the British Labour Mission were invited. There the famous Socialist Revolutionist Chernov had unexpectedly made his appearance. He severely criticised the Bolshevik regime, received an ovation from the huge audience of workers, and then vanished as mysteriously as he had come. The Menshevik Dan was less successful. He also addressed the meeting, but he failed to make his escape: he landed in the Cheka. The next morning the Moscow *Pravda* and the *Izvestia* denounced the action of the Printers’ Union as counterrevolutionary, and raged about Chernov having been permitted to speak. The papers called for exemplary punishment of the printers who dared defy the Soviet Government.
The Bakers’ Union, a very militant organization, had also been suppressed, and its management replaced by Communists. Several months before, in March, I had attended a convention of the bakers. The delegates impressed me as a courageous group who did not fear to criticise the Bolshevik regime and present the demands of the workers.

===Chinese===

代表中也有无政府主义者和工团主义者，他们同六个月前的我一样，坚信布尔什维克是革命的象征。他们怀着热忱响应了莫斯科的召唤。有几个我在彼得格勒见过，如今他们急于听我的经历、我的看法。可我能对他们说什么呢？说了他们信吗？我来俄国之前，若有逆耳的批评，我信吗？何况我自觉对布尔什维克的看法还太不成形、太模糊，只是一堆印象的堆积；旧的价值碎了，至今没换上新的。因此根本的问题我谈不了，但我确实告诉朋友们：莫斯科和彼得格勒的监狱里关满了无政府主义者和其他革命者；我劝他们不要满足于官方的解释，要自己去调查。我警告他们：向导和译员会包围他们——多半是契卡的人——除非拿出决绝的独立劲头，否则摸不到实情。

当时莫斯科很不太平。印刷工会被取缔，整个理事会进了监狱。该工会召集了一次公开大会，请了英国劳工代表团的成员。会上，那位著名的社会革命党人切尔诺夫出人意料地现身，痛批布尔什维克政权，赢得满场工人的欢呼，随即又神秘地失踪了，如他来时一样。孟什维克丹恩就没这么走运：他也上台讲了话，却没能脱身——进了契卡。第二天早晨，莫斯科《真理报》和《消息报》痛斥印刷工会的行径为反革命，为切尔诺夫居然被允许发言而大光其火，并要求对敢于违抗苏维埃政府的印刷工人惩一儆百。

面包工人工会——一个很强的战斗性组织——也被取缔，领导班子换上了共产党人。几个月前的三月，我出席过面包工人的一次代表大会。代表们给我的印象是一群有勇气的人，不怕批评布尔什维克政权，敢把工人的要求摆出来。

===Original===

I wondered then that they were permitted to continue the conference, for they were outspoken in their opposition to the Communists. “The bakers are ‘*Shkurniki*,’ ”14 I was told; “they always instigate strikes, and only counterrevolutionists can wish to strike in the workers’ Republic.” But it seemed to me that the workers could not follow such reasoning. They did strike. They even committed a more heinous crime: they refused to vote for the Communist candidate, electing instead a man of their own choice. This action of the bakers was followed by the arrest of several of their more active members. Naturally the workers resented the arbitrary methods of the Government.
Later I met some of the bakers and found them much embittered against the Communist Party and the Government. I inquired about the condition of their union, telling them that I had been informed that the Russian unions were very powerful and had practical control of the industrial life of the country. The bakers laughed. “The trade unions are the lackeys of the Government,” they said; “they have no independent function, and the workers have no say in them. The trade unions are doing mere police duty for the Government.” That sounded quite different from the story told by Melnichansky, the chairman of the Moscow Trade Union Soviet, whom I had met on my first visit to Moscow.
On that occasion he had shown me about the trade union headquarters known as the Dom Soyusov, and explained how the organization worked. 7,000,000 workers were in the trade unions, he said; all trades and professions belonged to it. The workers themselves managed the industries and owned them. “The building you are in now is also owned by the unions,” he remarked with pride; “formerly it was the House of the Nobility.” The room we were in had been used for festive assemblies and the great nobles sat in crested chairs around the table in the centre. Melnichansky showed me the secret underground passage hidden by a little turntable, through which the nobles could escape in case of danger. They never dreamed that the workers would some day gather around the same table and sit in the beautiful hall of marble columns. The educational and cultural work done by the trade unions, the chairman further explained, was of the greatest scope.

===Chinese===

我当时就纳闷，这样的会怎么能开得下去——他们反共话说得太直了。「面包工人是『*自私鬼*』14，」我被告知，「他们总爱煽动罢工；在工人共和国里想罢工的，只能是反革命。」可依我看，工人们听不进这套逻辑。他们照样罢工，甚至还犯下更重的罪：拒绝投票给共产党候选人，选了自己中意的人。此举招来几名活跃分子被捕。工人自然怨恨政府的专横手段。

后来我见到几个面包工人，发现他们对党和政府怨愤已深。我问起工会的状况，说有人告诉我，俄国工会势力强大，实际执掌着国家的工业生活。面包工人们笑了。「工会是政府的走卒，」他们说，「没有任何独立职能，工人在里面没有发言权。工会干的纯粹是替政府当警察的差。」这话同梅尔尼昌斯基的说法大相径庭——他是莫斯科工会苏维埃的主席，我初访莫斯科时见过他。

那次他领我参观了工会总部「工会大厦」，讲解了组织如何运作。他说，工会里有七百万工人，各行各业全在册。工人自己管理工业并拥有工业。「您现在待的这栋楼也是工会的产业，」他自豪地说，「从前是贵族会堂。」我们所在的那间厅，旧日专办节庆盛会，大贵族们围着中央长桌坐在家徽椅里。梅尔尼昌斯基指给我看藏在一张小转台下的秘密地下通道——贵族们遇险时可以由此逃生。他们做梦也想不到，有朝一日工人会围坐同一张桌子，坐进这间大理石圆柱的华厅。主席又讲解说，工会的教育文化工作规模宏大。

===Original===

“We have our workers’ colleges and other cultural institutions giving courses and lectures on various subjects. They are all managed by the workers. The unions own their own means of recreation, and we have access to all the theatres.” It was apparent from his explanation that the trade unions of Russia had reached a point far beyond anything known by labour organizations in Europe and America.
A similar account I had heard from Tsiperovitch, the chairman of the Petrograd trade unions, with whom I had made my first trip to Moscow. He had also shown me about the Petrograd Labour Temple, a beautiful and spacious building where the Petrograd unions had their offices. His recital also made it clear that the workers of Russia had at last come into their own.
But gradually I began to see the other side of the medal. I found that like most things in Russia the trade union picture had a double facet: one paraded before foreign visitors and “investigators,” the other known by the masses. The bakers and the printers had recently been shown the other side. It was a lesson of the benefits that accrued to the trade unions in the Socialist Republic.
In March I had attended an election meeting arranged by the workers of one of the large Moscow factories. It was the most exciting gathering I had witnessed in Russia﻿—the dimly lit hall in the factory club rooms, the faces of the men and women worn with privation and suffering, the intense feeling over the wrong done them, all impressed me very strongly. Their chosen representative, an Anarchist, had been refused his mandate by the Soviet authorities. It was the third time the workers gathered to reelect their delegate to the Moscow Soviet, and every time they elected the same man. The Communist candidate opposing him was Semashko, the Commissar of the Department of Health. I had expected to find an educated and cultured man. But the behaviour and language of the Commissar at that election meeting would have put a hod-carrier to shame. He raved against the workers for choosing a non-Communist, called anathema upon their heads, and threatened them with the Cheka and the curtailment of their rations. But he had no effect upon the audience except to emphasize their opposition to him, and to arouse antagonism against the party he represented. The final victory, however, was with Semashko.

===Chinese===

「我们有工人大学和其他文化机构，开各科课程和讲座，全由工人管理。工会自办娱乐场所，所有剧院我们都能进。」听他这一讲，俄国工会显然已远远超出欧美劳工组织的水平。

类似的说法我也从齐佩罗维奇那里听过——彼得格勒工会的主席，我头一趟去莫斯科时与他同行。他还领我参观过彼得格勒劳动宫，一座漂亮宽敞的大楼，彼得格勒各工会的办公处。听他一讲，俄国工人也总算翻身当家了。

但我渐渐看到了勋章的另一面。我发现，同俄国的多数事物一样，工会这幅画有两副面孔：一副给外国来客和「考察者」检阅，另一副归大众知道。面包工人和印刷工人最近就领教了另一面——这是社会主义共和国工会所得实惠的一课。

三月里，我参加过莫斯科一家大工厂工人安排的选举大会。那是我在俄国见过的最激动的集会——工厂俱乐部里灯光昏暗，男男女女的脸被匮乏与苦难磨蚀，人们对所受不公的强烈情绪，都给我极深的印象。他们选出的代表是无政府主义者，被苏维埃当局拒绝了当选证书。这已是工人第三次集会改选他们出席莫斯科苏维埃的代表，每次选中的都是同一个人。与他对阵的共产党候选人是谢马什科，卫生人民委员。我原以为会见到一位有教养的文化人，可这位委员在选举大会上的言行，连个搬砖的都不如。他咆哮着骂工人选了非共产党人，咒他们遭天谴，拿契卡和削减口粮吓唬他们。可他除了把听众的反感烧得更旺、把人们推向他所代表的党的对立面，毫无效果。不过，最后的胜利属于谢马什科。

The workers’ choice was repudiated by the authorities and later even arrested and imprisoned. That was in March. In May, during the visit of the British Labour Mission, the factory candidate together with other political prisoners declared a hunger strike, which resulted in their liberation.
The story told me by the bakers of their election experiences had the quality of our own Wild West during its pioneer days. Chekists with loaded guns were in the habit of attending gatherings of the unions and they made it clear what would happen if the workers should fail to elect a Communist. But the bakers, a strong and militant organization, would not be intimidated. They declared that no bread would be baked in Moscow unless they were permitted to elect their own candidate. That had the desired effect. After the meeting the Chekists tried to arrest the candidate-elect, but the bakers surrounded him and saw him safely home. The next day they sent their ultimatum to the authorities, demanding recognition of their choice and threatening to strike in case of refusal. Thus the bakers triumphed and gained an advantage over their less courageous brothers in the other labour organizations of minor importance. In starving Russia the work of the bakers was as vital as life itself.

===Chinese===

工人选出的人被当局否决，后来索性被捕入狱。那是三月的事。五月，英国劳工代表团访问期间，那位工厂候选人同其他政治犯一起宣布绝食，方才换得获释。

面包工人对我讲的选举经历，颇有我们拓荒年代「狂野西部」的味道。契卡分子荷枪赴会已成惯例，把不选共产党人的后果交代得明明白白。可面包工人这个坚强好斗的组织不吃吓：他们宣告，不让他们选自己的人，莫斯科就一块面包也烤不出来。这一招立竿见影。会后契卡想抓当选人，面包工人们把他围在中间，护送他平安到家。第二天他们向当局发出最后通牒：要求承认他们的选择，否则罢工。面包工人就这样赢了，在那些无足轻重的行业组织里不如他们勇敢的弟兄们面前，占了上风。在饥馑的俄国，面包工人的活计，同生命本身一样攸关。
