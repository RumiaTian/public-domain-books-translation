## I Deportation to Russia / 一、放逐俄国
===Original===

On the night of December 21, 1919, together with 248 other political prisoners, I was deported from America. Although it was generally known we were to be deported, few really believed that the United States would so completely deny her past as an asylum for political refugees, some of whom had lived and worked in America for more than thirty years.
In my own case, the decision to eliminate me first became known when, in 1909, the Federal authorities went out of their way to disfranchise the man whose name gave me citizenship. That Washington waited till 1917 was due to the circumstance that the psychologic moment for the finale was lacking. Perhaps I should have contested my case at that time. With the then-prevalent public opinion, the Courts would probably not have sustained the fraudulent proceedings which robbed me of citizenship. But it did not seem credible then that America would stoop to the Tsaristic method of deportation.
Our anti-war agitation added fuel to the war hysteria of 1917, and thus furnished the Federal authorities with the desired opportunity to complete the conspiracy begun against me in Rochester, NY, 1909.
It was on December 5, 1919, while in Chicago lecturing, that I was telegraphically apprised of the fact that the order for my deportation was final. The question of my citizenship was then raised in court, but was of course decided adversely. I had intended to take the case to a higher tribunal, but finally I decided to carry the matter no further: Soviet Russia was luring me.
Ludicrously secretive were the authorities about our deportation. To the very last moment we were kept in ignorance as to the time. Then, unexpectedly, in the wee small hours of December 21st we were spirited away. The scene set for this performance was most thrilling. It was six o’clock Sunday morning, December 21, 1919, when under heavy military convoy we stepped aboard the *Buford*.
For twenty-eight days we were prisoners. Sentries at our cabin doors day and night, sentries on deck during the hour we were daily permitted to breathe the fresh air. Our men comrades were cooped up in dark, damp quarters, wretchedly fed, all of us in complete ignorance of the direction we were to take. Yet our spirits were high﻿—Russia, free, new Russia was before us.
All my life Russia’s heroic struggle for freedom was as a beacon to me.

===Chinese===

1919 年 12 月 21 日夜，我与另外 248 名政治犯一起被驱逐出美国。尽管人人皆知我们要被驱逐，但真正相信美国会如此彻底地背弃她作为政治流亡者庇护所的过去的，却寥寥无几——这些人中，有的在美国生活工作了三十多年。

就我而言，除掉我的图谋早在 1909 年就露了端倪：联邦当局那一年小题大做，剥夺了那个以姓氏赋予我国籍的男人的公民权。华盛顿之所以等到 1917 年，是因为缺少完成最后一击的心理时机。或许我当时就该抗争。以当年盛行的舆论，法院多半不会支持那场剥夺我国籍的骗局。但那时看来，美国竟会堕落到采用沙皇式的驱逐手段，实在难以置信。

我们的反战宣传为 1917 年的战争歇斯底里火上浇油，从而给了联邦当局梦寐以求的机会，去完成 1909 年在纽约州罗切斯特对我布下的阴谋。

1919 年 12 月 5 日，我正在芝加哥演讲，电报通知我：驱逐令已定案。国籍问题被提交法院，当然败诉了。我本想上诉到更高法庭，但终于决定不再纠缠下去：苏维埃俄国在召唤我。

当局对我们这批驱逐行动保密得近乎滑稽。直到最后一刻，我们都不知道启程的时间。随后，12 月 21 日凌晨，出其不意地，我们被秘密押走。这场戏的布景扣人心弦。1919 年 12 月 21 日，星期日早晨六点，我们在重兵押送下登上「布福德号」。

二十八天里我们是囚徒。舱门外昼夜站着哨兵，每天放风呼吸新鲜空气的一小时里，甲板上也站着哨兵。我们的男同志被关在阴暗潮湿的舱室里，伙食恶劣；我们全然不知航向何方。但我们的情绪高涨——俄国，自由了的、崭新的俄国就在前方。

俄国为自由而作的英勇斗争，我一生奉为灯塔。

===Original===

The revolutionary zeal of her martyred men and women, which neither fortress nor *katorga* could suppress, was my inspiration in the darkest hours. When the news of the February Revolution flashed across the world, I longed to hasten to the land which had performed the miracle and had freed her people from the age-old yoke of Tsarism. But America held me. The thought of thirty years of struggle for my ideals, of my friends and associates, made it impossible to tear myself away. I would go to Russia later, I thought.
Then came America’s entry into the war and the need of remaining true to the American people who were swept into the hurricane against their will. After all, I owed a great debt, I owed my growth and development to what was finest and best in America, to her fighters for liberty, to the sons and daughters of the revolution to come. I would be true to them. But the frenzied militarists soon terminated my work.
At last I was bound for Russia and all else was almost blotted out. I would behold with mine own eyes *matushka Rossiya*, the land freed from political and economic masters; the Russian *dubinushka*, as the peasant was called, raised from the dust; the Russian worker, the modern Samson, who with a sweep of his mighty arm had pulled down the pillars of decaying society. The twenty-eight days on our floating prison passed in a sort of trance. I was hardly conscious of my surroundings.
Finally we reached Finland, across which we were forced to journey in sealed cars. On the Russian border we were met by a committee of the Soviet Government, headed by Zorin. They had come to greet the first political refugees driven from America for opinion’s sake.
It was a cold day, with the earth a sheet of white, but spring was in our hearts. Soon we were to behold revolutionary Russia. I preferred to be alone when I touched the sacred soil: my exaltation was too great, and I feared I might not be able to control my emotion. When I reached Beloostrov the first enthusiastic reception tendered the refugees was over, but the place was still surcharged with intensity of feeling. I could sense the awe and humility of our group who, treated like felons in the United States, were here received as dear brothers and comrades and welcomed by the Red soldiers, the liberators of Russia.

她的殉难男女的革命热忱——无论堡垒还是*苦役*都压不灭——是我最黑暗时刻的鼓舞。二月革命的消息传遍世界时，我恨不得立刻飞向那片创造了奇迹、把人民从沙皇专制千年枷锁下解放出来的土地。但美国拴住了我。三十年理想的奋斗、我的朋友和同志们，使我无法弃之而去。以后再去俄国吧，我想。

接着美国参战了，被违背意愿卷进飓风的美国人民需要我坚守。毕竟，我负着一笔重债——我的成长，得益于美国最精粹最好的一切，得益于她的自由战士，得益于未来革命的儿子和女儿。我要忠于他们。但狂热的军国主义者们很快终结了我的事业。

如今我终于奔赴俄国，其余的一切几乎都淡出了。我将亲眼看到*俄罗斯母亲*——那片从政治与经济主子手中解放出来的土地；看到俄国的*庄稼汉*——农民的旧称——从尘土中挺起腰来；看到俄国的工人，现代参孙，挥动强劲的臂膀一举拉垮了衰朽社会的支柱。二十八天的水上囚禁是在恍惚中度过的，我几乎觉察不到周围的一切。

我们终于抵达芬兰，被迫乘坐密封车厢横穿该国。在俄国边境，以佐林为首的苏维埃政府委员会前来迎接，他们是为问候第一批因政见被逐出美国的政治流亡者而来。

天很冷，大地一片白，但我们心中是春天。很快我们就要亲眼见到革命的俄国了。踏上这片神圣的土地时，我宁愿独自一人：我的激动太满，唯恐控制不住感情。抵达别洛奥斯特罗夫时，迎接流亡者的第一场热烈欢迎已经结束，但那地方仍激荡着炽烈的情绪。我能感觉到我们这群人的敬畏与谦卑——在美国被当作重罪犯对待的人，在这里被当作亲爱的兄弟和同志，受到红军士兵——俄国的解放者——的欢迎。

From Beloostrov we were driven to the village where another reception had been prepared: A dark hall filled to suffocation, the platform lit up by tallow candles, a huge red flag, on the stage a group of women in black nuns’ attire. I stood as in a dream in the breathless silence. Suddenly a voice rang out. It beat like metal on my ears and seemed uninspired, but it spoke of the great suffering of the Russian people and of the enemies of the Revolution. Others addressed the audience, but I was held by the women in black, their faces ghastly in the yellow light. Were these really nuns? Had the Revolution penetrated even the walls of superstition? Had the Red Dawn broken into the narrow lives of these ascetics? It all seemed strange, fascinating.
Somehow I found myself on the platform. I could only blurt out that like my comrades I had not come to Russia to teach: I had come to learn, to draw sustenance and hope from her, to lay down my life on the altar of the Revolution.
After the meeting we were escorted to the waiting Petrograd train, the women in the black hood intoning the “Internationale,” the whole audience joining in. I was in the car with our host, Zorin, who had lived in America and spoke English fluently. He talked enthusiastically about the Soviet Government and its marvellous achievements. His conversation was illuminative, but one phrase struck me as discordant. Speaking of the political organization of his Party, he remarked: “Tammany Hall has nothing on us, and as to Boss Murphy, we could teach him a thing or two.” I thought the man was jesting. What relation could there be between Tammany Hall, Boss Murphy, and the Soviet Government?
I inquired about our comrades who had hastened from America at the first news of the Revolution. Many of them had died at the front, Zorin informed me, others were working with the Soviet Government. And Shatov? William Shatov, a brilliant speaker and able organizer, was a well-known figure in America, frequently associated with us in our work. We had sent him a telegram from Finland and were much surprised at his failure to reply. Why did not Shatov come to meet us? “Shatov had to leave for Siberia, where he is to take the post of Minister of Railways,” said Zorin.
In Petrograd our group again received an ovation.

从别洛奥斯特罗夫我们被送到一个村子，那里准备了另一场欢迎：一间黑暗的大厅挤得透不过气，台上点着牛油烛，一面巨大的红旗，台上站着一群穿黑色修女服的女人。我如在梦中，站在屏息的寂静里。忽然一个声音响起，它像金属敲在耳边，听来缺乏灵感，但讲述着俄国人民的深重苦难和革命的敌人。另一些人上台讲话，我却始终被那几个黑衣女人牵动着，她们的脸在昏黄的烛光下煞白。这些真是修女吗？革命竟穿透了迷信的高墙？红色的曙光竟照进了这些苦行者狭窄的生活？一切都显得离奇而迷人。

不知怎么我已站在台上。我只能脱口说：像我的同志们一样，我到俄国来不是来施教的，我是来学习的，来从她身上汲取滋养与希望，来把我的生命献上革命的祭坛。

会后我们被护送到候着的彼得格勒列车上。戴黑头巾的妇女们领唱《国际歌》，全场齐声相和。我与东道主佐林同乘一节车厢，他在美国生活过，英语流利。他热情地大谈苏维埃政府及其辉煌成就。他的谈话颇开眼界，唯有一句话听来刺耳。谈到他党的政治组织时，他说：「坦慕尼协会比不上我们；至于墨菲老板，我们能教他一两手。」我以为此人在说笑。坦慕尼协会、墨菲老板，同苏维埃政府能有什么关系？

我打听那些一闻革命消息便从美国赶回来的同志们。佐林告诉我，许多人已在前线牺牲，其余的在与苏维埃政府共事。那么沙托夫呢？威廉·沙托夫，杰出的演说家和干练的组织者，在美国是个知名人物，常与我们合作共事。我们从芬兰给他发过电报，他竟不回，令人纳罕。沙托夫为什么不来接我们？「沙托夫去西伯利亚了，他要去就任铁道部长，」佐林说。

在彼得格勒，我们一行又受到热烈欢迎。

Then the deportees were taken to the famous Tauride Palace, where they were to be fed and housed for the night. Zorin asked Alexander Berkman and myself to accept his hospitality. We entered the waiting automobile. The city was dark and deserted; not a living soul to be seen anywhere. We had not gone very far when the car was suddenly halted, and an electric light flashed into our eyes. It was the militia, demanding the password. Petrograd had recently fought back the Yudenich attack and was still under martial law. The process was repeated frequently along the route. Shortly before we reached our destination we passed a well-lighted building. “It is our station house,” Zorin explained, “but we have few prisoners there now. Capital punishment is abolished and we have recently proclaimed a general political amnesty.”
Presently the automobile came to a halt. “The First House of the Soviets,” said Zorin, “the living place of the most active members of our Party.” Zorin and his wife occupied two rooms, simply but comfortably furnished. Tea and refreshments were served, and our hosts entertained us with the absorbing story of the marvellous defence the Petrograd workers had organized against the Yudenich forces. How heroically the men and women, even the children, had rushed to the defence of the Red City! What wonderful self-discipline and cooperation the proletariat demonstrated. The evening passed in these reminiscences, and I was about to retire to the room secured for me when a young woman arrived who introduced herself as the sister-in-law of “Bill” Shatov. She greeted us warmly and asked us to come up to see her sister who lived on the floor above. When we reached their apartment I found myself embraced by big jovial Bill himself. How strange of Zorin to tell me that Shatov had left for Siberia! What did it mean? Shatov explained that he had been ordered not to meet us at the border, to prevent his giving us our first impressions of Soviet Russia. He had fallen into disfavour with the Government and was being sent to Siberia into virtual exile. His trip had been delayed and therefore we still happened to find him.
We spent much time with Shatov before he left Petrograd. For whole days I listened to his story of the Revolution, with its light and shadows, and the developing tendency of the Bolsheviki toward the right.

随后被驱逐的难友们被带往著名的塔夫利宫，当晚在那里吃饭住宿。佐林邀亚历山大·伯克曼和我去做客。我们上了候着的汽车。城市漆黑而空寂，到处不见一个活人。车没走出多远，突然被拦住，一道电光直射我们眼睛。是民兵在查口令。彼得格勒刚击退尤登尼奇的进攻，仍处于戒严之中。一路上车程频繁重演。快到目的地时，我们经过一座灯光明亮的建筑。「这是我们的警察分局，」佐林解释说，「不过现在在押的囚犯寥寥。死刑已经废除，我们还宣布了大赦政治犯。」

不一会儿，汽车停了。「苏维埃第一宫，」佐林说，「我们党最活跃成员的住地。」佐林夫妇住两间房，陈设简朴却舒适。茶点端上来，主人们给我们讲述彼得格勒工人组织的抵抗尤登尼奇匪军的精彩防御战，听得我们入了迷。男人女人、连孩子们都怎样奋起保卫红色城市啊！无产阶级表现出的自我纪律与协作何等了不起！晚问就在这样的回忆中过去。我正要去为我安排的房间歇息，来了一位年轻女子，自称是「比尔」沙托夫的妻妹。她热情地招呼我们上楼去看望住在上一层的姐姐。到得她们家，我竟被高大快活的比尔本人一把抱住。佐林竟告诉我沙托夫去了西伯利亚，多奇怪！这是怎么回事？沙托夫解释说，上头命令他不得赴边境迎接我们，免得他向我们描绘对苏维埃俄国的第一印象。他已失宠于政府，将被送往西伯利亚，形同流放。行期耽搁了，所以我们才碰巧还遇得上他。

沙托夫离开彼得格勒前，我们同他盘桓良久。接连几天，我听他讲革命的故事，讲它的光明与阴影，讲布尔什维克向右转的发展趋势。

Shatov, however, insisted that it was necessary for all the revolutionary elements to work with the Bolsheviki Government. Of course, the Communists had made many mistakes, but what they did was inevitable, imposed upon them by Allied interference and the blockade.
A few days after our arrival Zorin asked Alexander Berkman and myself to accompany him to Smolny. Smolny, the erstwhile boarding school for the daughters of the aristocracy, had been the centre of revolutionary events. Almost every stone had played its part. Now it was the seat of the Petrograd Government. I found the place heavily guarded and giving the impression of a beehive of officials and government employees. The Department of the Third International was particularly interesting. It was the domain of Zinoviev. I was much impressed by the magnitude of it all.
After showing us about, Zorin invited us to the Smolny dining room. The meal consisted of good soup, meat and potatoes, bread and tea﻿—rather a good meal in starving Russia, I thought.
Our group of deportees was quartered in Smolny. I was anxious about my travelling companions, the two girls who had shared my cabin on the *Buford*. I wished to take them back with me to the First House of the Soviet. Zorin sent for them. They arrived greatly excited and told us that the whole group of deportees had been placed under military guard. The news was startling. The people who had been driven out of America for their political opinions, now in Revolutionary Russia again prisoners﻿—three days after their arrival. What had happened?
We turned to Zorin. He seemed embarrassed. “Some mistake,” he said, and immediately began to make inquiries. It developed that four ordinary criminals had been found among the politicals deported by the United States Government, and therefore a guard was placed over the whole group. The proceeding seemed to me unjust and uncalled for. It was my first lesson in Bolshevik methods.

但沙托夫坚持说，一切革命力量都必须与布尔什维克政府合作。共产党人当然犯了许多错误，但他们所做的都是不可避免的，是协约国干涉和封锁强加给他们的。

抵达几天后，佐林邀亚历山大·伯克曼和我同去斯莫尔尼宫。斯莫尔尼宫原是贵族小姐的寄宿学校，曾是革命事件的中心，几乎每一块石头都尽过它的本分，如今是彼得格勒政府所在地。只见那地方戒备森严，给人大蜂巢般的印象，满是在职官员和政府雇员。第三国际的部门尤其可观，那是季诺维也夫的领地。这一切的规模令我印象深刻。

参观完毕，佐林请我们去斯莫尔尼食堂用餐。饭食有好汤、肉和土豆、面包和茶——在饥馑的俄国，这算相当不错的一餐了，我想。

我们这批被驱逐者被安置在斯莫尔尼宫。我惦记着我的旅伴——「布福德号」上与我同舱的两个姑娘。我想带她们回苏维埃第一宫去住。佐林派人去接。她们兴冲冲地到了，却告诉我们：全队被驱逐者已被置于军事看管之下。消息令人震惊。因政治见解被逐出美国的人们，到了革命的俄国竟又成了囚犯——抵境才三天。这是怎么回事？

我们转向佐林。他显得尴尬。「搞错了吧，」他说，随即着手查问。原来，美国政府驱逐的政治犯中发现了四名普通刑事犯，因此全队都被看管起来。这做法在我看来既不公正又无必要。这是我领教布尔什维克手法的头一课。
