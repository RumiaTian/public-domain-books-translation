## II Petrograd / 二、彼得格勒
===Original===

My parents had moved to St. Petersburg when I was thirteen. Under the discipline of a German school in Königsberg and the Prussian attitude toward everything Russian, I had grown up in the atmosphere of hatred to that country. I dreaded especially the terrible Nihilists who had killed Tsar Alexander II, so good and kind, as I had been taught. St. Petersburg was to me an evil thing. But the gaiety of the city, its vivacity and brilliancy, soon dispelled my childish fancies and made the city appear like a fairy dream. Then my curiosity was aroused by the revolutionary mystery which seemed to hang over everyone, and of which no one dared to speak. When four years later I left with my sister for America I was no longer the German Gretchen to whom Russia spelt evil. My whole soul had been transformed and the seed planted for what was to be my life’s work. Especially did St. Petersburg remain in my memory a vivid picture, full of life and mystery.
I found Petrograd of 1920 quite a different place. It was almost in ruins, as if a hurricane had swept over it. The houses looked like broken old tombs upon neglected and forgotten cemeteries. The streets were dirty and deserted; all life had gone from them. The population of Petrograd before the war was almost 2,000,000; in 1920 it had dwindled to 500,000. The people walked about like living corpses; the shortage of food and fuel was slowly sapping the city; grim death was clutching at its heart. Emaciated and frostbitten men, women, and children were being whipped by the common lash, the search for a piece of bread or a stick of wood. It was a heartrending sight by day, an oppressive weight at night. Especially were the nights of the first month in Petrograd dreadful. The utter stillness of the large city was paralysing. It fairly haunted me, this awful oppressive silence broken only by occasional shots. I would lay awake trying to pierce the mystery. Did not Zorin say that capital punishment had been abolished? Why this shooting? Doubts disturbed my mind, but I tried to wave them aside. I had come to learn.
Much of my first knowledge and impressions of the October Revolution and the events that followed I received from the Zorins. As already mentioned, both had lived in America, spoke English, and were eager to enlighten me upon the history of the Revolution.

我十三岁那年，父母移居圣彼得堡。此前我在科尼斯堡一所德国学校受管教，在普鲁士人对一切俄国事物的态度里长大，浸透了对那个国家的仇恨。我尤其惧怕可怕的虚无党人——他们杀害了沙皇亚历山大二世，而我被教导说那是位多么仁善的君王。圣彼得堡在我心里曾是邪恶的化身。但这座城的欢乐、生气与光彩，很快驱散了我的童稚幻想，使它在我眼里变成一场童话般的梦。接着，那似乎笼罩着每个人、人人不敢言说的革命神秘，又勾起了我的好奇。四年后我随妹妹赴美时，已不再是那个把俄国等同于罪恶的德国「格蕾辛」。我的灵魂整个变了，一生事业的种子已经播下。圣彼得堡尤其留在我的记忆里，一幅鲜活神秘、生机勃勃的画卷。

我发现 1920 年的彼得格勒全然变了样。它几乎成了废墟，仿佛被飓风扫荡过。房屋像荒弃坟场里破损的旧墓碑。街道肮脏而空寂，生命尽数褪去。彼得格勒战前人口近二百万，到 1920 年锐减至五十万。人们像活尸一样游荡；食物与燃料的匮乏正慢慢抽干这座城，狰狞的死亡攥住了它的心脏。形容枯槁、冻伤累累的男女老幼，被一条共同的鞭子抽打着——寻觅一块面包、一根柴火。白日里是令人心碎的景象，入夜便成了压顶的重负。尤其在彼得格勒的头一个月，夜晚可怕至极：大都会死一般的寂静，令人瘫软。这可怕的、压抑的沉寂，只偶尔被枪声划破，简直像鬼魂一样缠着我。我夜夜醒着，想刺透这哑谜。佐林不是说死刑已经废除了吗？为什么还有枪声？疑虑扰着我的心，但我竭力把它们挥开。我是来学习的。

关于十月革命及其后种种，我最初的知识和印象，多来自佐林夫妇。如前所述，两人都在美国住过，会说英语，热心给我讲解革命的历史。

===Original===

They were devoted to the cause and worked very hard; he, especially, who was secretary of the Petrograd committee of his party, besides editing the daily, *Krasnaya Gazetta*, and participating in other activities.
It was from Zorin that I first learned about that legendary figure, Makhno. The latter was an Anarchist, I was informed, who under the Tsar had been sentenced to *katorga*. Liberated by the February revolution, he became the leader of a peasant army in the Ukraine, proving himself extremely able and daring and doing splendid work in the defence of the Revolution. For some time Makhno worked in harmony with the Bolsheviki, fighting the counterrevolutionary forces. Then he became antagonistic, and now his army, recruited from bandit elements, was fighting the Bolsheviki. Zorin related that he had been one of a committee sent to Makhno to bring about an understanding. But Makhno would not listen to reason. He continued his warfare against the Soviets and was considered a dangerous counterrevolutionist.
I had no means of verifying the story, and I was far from disbelieving the Zorins. Both appeared most sincere and dedicated to their work, types of religious zealots ready to burn the heretic, but equally ready to sacrifice their own lives for their cause. I was much impressed by the simplicity of their lives. Holding a responsible position, Zorin could have received special rations, but they lived very poorly, their supper often consisting only of herring, black bread, and tea. I thought it especially admirable because Lisa Zorin was with child at the time.
Two weeks after my arrival in Russia I was invited to attend the Alexander Herzen commemoration in the Winter Palace. The white marble hall where the gathering took place seemed to intensify the bitter frost, but the people present were unmindful of the penetrating cold. I also was conscious only of the unique situation: Alexander Herzen, one of the most hated revolutionists of his time, honoured in the Winter Palace! Frequently before the spirit of Herzen had found its way into the house of the Romanovs. It was when the *Kolokol*, published abroad and sparkling with the brilliancy of Herzen and Turgenev, would in some mysterious manner be discovered on the desk of the Tsar.

他们忠于事业，工作极勤；佐林尤其如此，他是党彼得格勒委员会的书记，还主编日报《红报》，此外尚有多项活动。

马赫诺这个传奇人物，我最初就是从佐林口中听说的。据说他是无政府主义者，沙皇时代被判过*苦役*；二月革命获释后，成为乌克兰一支农民军的领袖，才干胆略超群，为保卫革命屡建奇功。有一段时期，马赫诺同布尔什维克合作无间，共打反革命。后来反目成仇，如今他那支由匪帮成分纠集的军队正在打布尔什维克。佐林说，他参加过派往马赫诺处谋求谅解的委员会，可马赫诺不可理喻，一意与苏维埃为敌，被视为危险的革命敌人。

我无从核实这个故事，也远未到不信佐林夫妇的地步。两人看上去极其诚恳，专注于事业，是那种宗教狂热者式的人物——烧起异端来毫不手软，为事业捐躯也同样在所不惜。他们生活的俭朴令我动容。佐林身居要职，本可领取特别口粮，可他们过得很清苦，晚饭常常只有青鱼、黑面包和茶。莉莎·佐林当时正怀着身孕，这尤其让我敬佩。

抵俄两周后，我应邀到冬宫出席亚历山大·赫尔岑纪念会。举行集会的白色大理石厅，仿佛使酷寒更甚，但与会者浑然不觉那刺骨的冷。我也只意识到这一独一无二的情境：亚历山大·赫尔岑——他那个时代最遭恨的革命者之一——竟在冬宫受礼遇！从前，赫尔岑的幽灵也曾不止一次潜入罗曼诺夫的家门：在国外出版、闪耀着赫尔岑与屠格涅夫才华的《钟声》，总会神不知鬼不觉地出现在沙皇的书桌上。

===Original===

Now the Tsars were no more, but the spirit of Herzen had risen again and was witnessing the realization of the dream of one of Russia’s great men.
One evening I was informed that Zinoviev had returned from Moscow and would see me. He arrived about midnight. He looked very tired and was constantly disturbed by urgent messages. Our talk was of a general nature, of the grave situation in Russia, the shortage of food and fuel then particularly poignant, and about the labour situation in America. He was anxious to know “how soon the revolution could be expected in the United States.” He left upon me no definite impression, but I was conscious of something lacking in the man, though I could not determine at the time just what it was.
Another Communist I saw much of the first weeks was John Reed. I had known him in America. He was living in the Astoria, working hard and preparing for his return to the United States. He was to journey through Latvia and he seemed apprehensive of the outcome. He had been in Russia during the October days and this was his second visit. Like Shatov he also insisted that the dark sides of the Bolshevik regime were inevitable. He believed fervently that the Soviet Government would emerge from its narrow party lines and that it would presently establish the Communistic Commonwealth. We spent much time together, discussing the various phases of the situation.
So far I had met none of the Anarchists and their failure to call rather surprised me. One day a friend I had known in the States came to inquire whether I would see several members of an Anarchist organization. I readily assented. From them I learned a version of the Russian Revolution and the Bolshevik regime utterly different from what I had heard before. It was so startling, so terrible that I could not believe it. They invited me to attend a small gathering they had called to present to me their views.
The following Sunday I went to their conference. Passing Nevsky Prospekt, near Liteiny Street, I came upon a group of women huddled together to protect themselves from the cold. They were surrounded by soldiers, talking and gesticulating. Those women, I learned, were prostitutes who were selling themselves for a pound of bread, a piece of soap or chocolate. The soldiers were the only ones who could afford to buy them because of their extra rations.

如今沙皇没有了，赫尔岑的精神复活了，正亲见俄国一位伟人的梦想成真。

一天傍晚，有人通知我，季诺维也夫已从莫斯科回来，要见我。他半夜光临，满面倦色，急件不断打搅。我们的谈话是一般性的：俄国的严重局势、当时特别尖锐的粮荒与燃料荒、美国的劳工状况。他急于知道「美国多久可以盼来革命」。他没有给我留下确定的印象，只觉得此人身上缺了点什么——当时还说不清缺的究竟是什么。

最初几周我时常见到的另一位共产党人是约翰·里德，我在美国就认识他。他住在阿斯托里亚，埋头工作，做着返美的准备。他打算取道拉脱维亚，似乎对结果隐隐担忧。十月革命的日子里他就在俄国，这是第二次来。同沙托夫一样，他也坚持说布尔什维克政权的阴暗面在所难免；他热切相信苏维埃政府会跳出狭隘的党派框框，不久就会建成共产主义共同体。我们常在一起，讨论局势的方方面面。

到目前为止，我一个无政府主义者也没见到，他们迟迟不来拜访，倒让我纳罕。一天，一位在美国认识的朋友来问，愿不愿见一个无政府主义组织的几位成员。我欣然同意。从他们那里，我听到了一个与先前所闻截然不同的俄国革命和布尔什维克政权的版本，骇人得叫我无法相信。他们邀我参加他们召集的一次小会，好当面向我陈述见解。

下一个星期日，我去了他们的会场。走过涅瓦大街近铸炮街的拐角，撞见一群妇女挤作一团御寒，四周围着士兵，又说又比划。一问才知，那些是妓女，为一磅面包、一块肥皂或一块巧克力出卖自己；买得起的只有士兵，因为他们有额外配给。

Prostitution in revolutionary Russia. I wondered. What is the Communist Government doing for these unfortunates? What are the Workers’ and Peasants’ Soviets doing? My escort smiled sadly. The Soviet Government had closed the houses of prostitution and was now trying to drive the women off the streets, but hunger and cold drove them back again; besides, the soldiers had to be humoured. It was too ghastly, too incredible to be real, yet there they were﻿—those shivering creatures for sale and their buyers, the red defenders of the Revolution. “The cursed interventionists, the blockade﻿—they are responsible,” said my escort. Why, yes, the counterrevolutionists and the blockade are responsible, I reassured myself. I tried to dismiss the thought of that huddled group, but it clung to me. I felt something snap within me.
At last we reached the Anarchist quarters, in a dilapidated house in a filthy backyard. I was ushered into a small room crowded with men and women. The sight recalled pictures of thirty years ago when, persecuted and hunted from place to place, the Anarchists in America were compelled to meet in a dingy hall on Orchard Street, New York, or in the dark rear room of a saloon. That was in capitalistic America. But this is revolutionary Russia, which the Anarchists had helped to free. Why should they have to gather in secret and in such a place?
That evening and the following day I listened to a recital of the betrayal of the Revolution by the Bolsheviki. Workers from the Baltic factories spoke of their enslavement, Kronstadt sailors voiced their bitterness and indignation against the people they had helped to power and who had become their masters. One of the speakers had been condemned to death by the Bolsheviki for his Anarchist ideas, but had escaped and was now living illegally. He related how the sailors had been robbed of the freedom of their Soviets, how every breath of life was being censored. Others spoke of the Red Terror and repression in Moscow, which resulted in the throwing of a bomb into the gathering of the Moscow section of the Communist Party in September, 1919. They told me of the overfilled prisons, of the violence practised on the workers and peasants. I listened rather impatiently, for everything in me cried out against this indictment. It sounded impossible; it could not be.

革命俄国的娼妓。我愣住了。共产党政府为这些不幸的人做了什么？工农苏维埃做了什么？护送的同志苦笑。苏维埃政府封闭了妓院，如今又想把她们赶下街头，可饥饿和寒冷一次次把她们赶回来；再说，士兵也得有人安抚。这太骇人、太不像真的了，可她们就在那里——那些打着寒战的、待售的生灵，和买主——革命的红色保卫者们。「该死的干涉者，还有封锁——都怪他们，」护送的同志说。是啊，是啊，都怪反革命和封锁，我自我宽解。我竭力不去想那挤作一团的人群，但它缠着我不放。我感到心里有什么东西断了。

我们终于到了无政府主义者的会所——一个肮脏后院里的破屋。我被领进一间小屋，挤满了男男女女。这景象让我想起三十年前的画面：在美国，无政府主义者受迫害、被四处驱赶，只好在纽约奥查德街的昏暗会堂或酒馆后屋的暗间里聚会。那还是资本主义的美国。而这里是革命的俄国，是无政府主义者帮着解放的俄国。为什么他们还得秘密聚集在这样的地方？

那一晚和次日，我听了一场关于布尔什维克背叛革命的陈述。波罗的海工厂的工人讲他们的被奴役；喀琅施塔得的水兵倾诉他们对亲手扶上台、如今做了他们老爷的人们的怨愤。一名演讲者曾因无政府主义思想被布尔什维克判死刑，越狱后至今非法藏身。他讲水兵的苏维埃自由如何被剥夺，讲连每一口呼吸都受到检查。另一些人讲莫斯科的红色恐怖与镇压——那镇压酿成了 1919 年 9 月共产党莫斯科市组织集会时掷进会场的一颗炸弹。他们对我讲人满为患的监狱，讲对工人农民的暴力。我听得颇不耐烦：我全身心都在抗拒这份控诉。它听来不可能，不可能是真的。

Someone was surely at fault, but probably it was they, my comrades, I thought. They were unreasonable, impatient for immediate results. Was not violence inevitable in a revolution, and was it not imposed upon the Bolsheviki by the Interventionists? My comrades were indignant. “Disguise yourself so the Bolsheviki do not recognize you; take a pamphlet of Kropotkin and try to distribute it in a Soviet meeting. You will soon see whether we told you the truth. Above all, get out of the First House of the Soviet. Live among the people and you will have all the proofs you need.”
How childish and trifling it all seemed in the face of the world event that was taking place in Russia! No, I could not credit their stories. I would wait and study conditions. But my mind was in a turmoil, and the nights became more oppressive than ever.
The day arrived when I was given a chance to attend the meeting of the Petro-Soviet. It was to be a double celebration in honour of the return of Karl Radek to Russia and Joffe’s report on the peace treaty with Estonia. As usual I went with the Zorins. The gathering was in the Tauride Palace, the former meeting place of the Russian Duma. Every entrance to the hall was guarded by soldiers, the platform surrounded by them holding their guns at attention. The hall was crowded to the very doors. I was on the platform overlooking the sea of faces below. Starved and wretched they looked, these sons and daughters of the people, the heroes of Red Petrograd. How they had suffered and endured for the Revolution! I felt very humble before them.
Zinoviev presided. After the “Internationale” had been sung by the audience standing, Zinoviev opened the meeting. He spoke at length. His voice is high pitched, without depth. The moment I heard him I realized what I had missed in him at our first meeting﻿—depth, strength of character. Next came Radek. He was clever, witty, sarcastic, and he paid his respects to the counterrevolutionists and to the White Guards. Altogether an interesting man and an interesting address.
Joffe looked the diplomat. Well fed and groomed, he seemed rather out of place in that assembly. He spoke of the peace conditions with Estonia, which were received with enthusiasm by the audience. Certainly these people wanted peace. Would it ever come to Russia?
Last spoke Zorin, by far the ablest and most convincing that evening.

肯定有谁错了，但多半是他们，我的同志们，我想。他们不讲情理，急于求成。革命中暴力难道不是难免的吗？难道不是干涉者强加给布尔什维克的吗？同志们愤然了。「您化个装，别让布尔什维克认出来；带一册克鲁泡特金的小册子，到苏维埃会场去发发看。很快您就知道我们说的是不是真话。还有，搬出苏维埃第一宫。住到人民中间去，您要的证据全都会有。」

面对正在俄国发生的这场世界性事变，这一切显得多么幼稚、多么琐碎！不，我不能信他们的话。我要等一等，要研究实况。可我心乱如麻，夜晚比先前更沉得压人。

这一天终于来了：我得到机会出席彼得格勒苏维埃的会议。那是一场双重庆祝——欢迎卡尔·拉杰克回到俄国，和约飞关于对爱沙尼亚和约的报告。我照例同佐林夫妇同去。集会在塔夫利宫，从前俄国国家杜马开会的地方。大厅每个入口都有士兵把守，主席台四周士兵持枪立正。厅里人挤到门口。我在台上，俯瞰下面的人海。这些人民的儿女、红色彼得格勒的英雄们，看上去饥饿而憔悴。他们为革命受过多少苦、忍过多少难啊！在他们面前，我自感卑微。

季诺维也夫主持。全场起立唱完《国际歌》，他宣布开会，讲了很久。他嗓音尖细，缺乏厚度。一听到他的声音，我就明白初见时他身上缺的是什么了——深度，性格的力量。接着是拉杰克，聪明、机智、尖刻，把他的「敬意」致给了反革命和白卫军。总之是个有趣的人，一篇有趣的演说。

约飞一副外交家风度，保养良好，衣着整齐，在那样的会场里显得格格不入。他讲对爱沙尼亚的和平条件，博得全场欢呼。这些人当然是渴望和平的。和平几时才会来到俄国呢？

最后是佐林发言，那晚数他最能干、最有说服力。

Then the meeting was thrown open to discussion. A Menshevik asked for the floor. Immediately pandemonium broke loose. Yells of “Traitor!” “Kolchak!” “Counterrevolutionist!” came from all parts of the audience and even from the platform. It looked to me like an unworthy proceeding for a revolutionary assembly.
On the way home I spoke to Zorin about it. He laughed. “Free speech is a bourgeois superstition,” he said; “during a revolutionary period there can be no free speech.” I was rather dubious about the sweeping statement, but I felt that I had no right to judge. I was a newcomer, while the people at the Tauride Palace had sacrificed and suffered so much for the Revolution. I had no right to judge.

===Chinese===

随后会议开放讨论。一位孟什维克要求发言，全场顿时大乱。「叛徒！」「高尔察克！」「反革命！」的吼声从四面八方、甚至从主席台上炸开。在我看来，这于一个革命会议实在有失体面。

回家路上我向佐林提起此事。他笑了。「言论自由是资产阶级的迷信，」他说，「革命时期不可能有什么言论自由。」对这种一刀切的说法我颇为怀疑，但我自觉无权评判。我是新来的，而塔夫利宫里那些人，为革命牺牲过、受难过那么多。我无权评判。
