## IX At Chicago / IX 在芝加哥

===Original===
Mr. Peacocke went on alone to San Francisco from the Ogden Junction, and there obtained full information on the matter which had brought him upon this long and disagreeable journey. He had no difficulty in obtaining the evidence which he required. He had not been twenty-four hours in the place before he was, in truth, standing on the stone which had been placed over the body of Ferdinand Lefroy, as he had declared to Robert Lefroy that he would stand before he would be satisfied. On the stone was cut simply the names, Ferdinand Lefroy of Kilbrack, Louisiana; and to these were added the dates of the days on which the man had been born and on which he died. Of this stone he had a photograph made, of which he took copies with him; and he obtained also from the minister who had buried the body and from the custodian who had charge of the cemetery certificates of the interment. Armed with these he could no longer doubt himself, or suppose that others would doubt, that Ferdinand Lefroy was dead.

Having thus perfected his object, and feeling but little interest in a town to which he had been brought by such painful circumstances, he turned round, and on the second day after his arrival, again started for Chicago. Had it been possible, he would fain have avoided any further meeting with Robert Lefroy. Short as had been his stay at San Francisco he had learnt that Robert, after his brother’s death, had been concerned in buying mining shares and paying for them with forged notes. It was not supposed that he himself had been engaged in the forgery, but that he had come into the city with men who had been employed for years on this operation, and had bought shares and endeavoured to sell them on the following day. He had, however, managed to leave the place before the police had got hold of him, and had escaped, so that no one had been able to say at what station he had got upon the railway. Nor did anyone in San Francisco know where Robert Lefroy was now to be found. His companions had been taken, tried, and convicted, and were now in the State prison﻿—where also would Robert Lefroy soon be if any of the officers of the State could get hold of him. Luckily Mr. Peacocke had said little or nothing of the man in making his own inquiries. Much as he had hated and dreaded the man; much as he had suffered from his companionship﻿—good reason as he had to dislike the whole family﻿—he felt himself bound by their late companionship not to betray him. The man had assisted Mr. Peacocke simply for money; but still he had assisted him. Mr. Peacocke therefore held his peace and said nothing. But he would have been thankful to have been able to send the money that was now due to him without having again to see him. That, however, was impossible.

On reaching Chicago he went to an hotel far removed from that which Lefroy had designated. Lefroy had explained to him something of the geography of the town, and had explained that for himself he preferred a “modest, quiet hotel.” The modest, quiet hotel was called Mrs. Jones’s boardinghouse, and was in one of the suburbs far from the main street. “You needn’t say as you’re coming to me,” Lefroy had said to him; “nor need you let on as you know anything of Mrs. Jones at all. People are so curious; and it may be that a gentleman sometimes likes to lie *perdu*.” Mr. Peacocke, although he had but small sympathy for the taste of a gentleman who likes to lie *perdu*, nevertheless did as he was bid, and found his way to Mrs. Jones’s boardinghouse without telling anyone whither he was going.

Before he started he prepared himself with a thousand dollars in banknotes, feeling that this wretched man had earned them in accordance with their compact. His only desire now was to hand over the money as quickly as possible, and to hurry away out of Chicago. He felt as though he himself were almost guilty of some crime in having to deal with this man, in having to give him money secretly, and in carrying out to the end an arrangement of which no one else was to know the details. How would it be with him if the police of Chicago should come upon him as a friend, and probably an accomplice, of one who was “wanted” on account of forgery at San Francisco? But he had no help for himself, and at Mrs. Jones’s he found his wife’s brother-in-law seated in the bar of the public-house﻿—that everlasting resort for American loungers﻿—with a cigar as usual stuck in his mouth, loafing away his time as only American frequenters of such establishments know how to do. In England such a man would probably be found in such a place with a glass of some alcoholic mixture beside him, but such is never the case with an American. If he wants a drink he goes to the bar and takes it standing﻿—will perhaps take two or three, one after another; but when he has settled himself down to loaf, he satisfies himself with chewing a cigar, and covering a circle around him with the results. With this amusement he will remain contented hour after hour;﻿—nay, throughout the entire day if no harder work be demanded of him. So was Robert Lefroy found now. When Peacocke entered the hall or room the man did not rise from his chair, but accosted him as though they had parted only an hour since. “So, old fellow, you’ve got back all alive.”

目的既已办妥，他对这座因如此伤心的缘由才把自己引来的城市，也就提不起多大兴致；于是掉转身，在抵达后的第二天，重新启程往芝加哥去。但凡有法可想，他巴不得从此不再同罗伯特·勒弗罗伊见面。他在旧金山停留虽然短暂，却已打听到：罗伯特在其兄死后，曾插手购买矿业股票，而付账用的竟是一叠伪造钞票。人们并不认为伪造一事是他亲自动手，只说他同几个操这行当已有多年的人一道进了城，买下股票，指望第二天便设法脱手。不过，他赶在警察缉拿之前脱身出城，逃得无影无踪，以致谁也说不上他是在哪个车站上的火车。旧金山也没有一个人说得出，如今到哪里能寻见罗伯特·勒弗罗伊。他的同伙已一一落网、受审、定罪，眼下正关在州监狱里——只要本州的差员有谁能把他捉住，罗伯特·勒弗罗伊很快也会进那同一座牢门。所幸皮科克先生自行打听的时候，对此人几乎一字未提。他尽管恨透此人的行止、惧怕此人的为人；尽管他因这番同行受尽了苦楚——他厌恶勒弗罗伊全家，自有十二分的理由——却觉得自己受着近来这层同行情分的束缚，不能把他出卖。这人帮皮科克先生的忙，固然纯粹是为了钱；可帮忙总归是帮了。因此皮科克先生缄口不言，什么也没说。只是，倘能把眼下欠他的那笔钱送去，而免得同他再见一面，他真要千恩万谢。然而这终究办不到。

到了芝加哥，他投宿了一家客店，离勒弗罗伊指定给他的那一家远得很。勒弗罗伊先前向他讲过这个城市的地理大概，还说自己这个人，只喜欢一家“朴素清静的客店”。这家朴素清静的客店，名叫琼斯太太的寄宿公寓（Mrs. Jones's boardinghouse），坐落在远离大街的一处城郊。“你不必说你是来找我的，”勒弗罗伊当时这样嘱咐他，“也不必让人瞧出你认得琼斯太太半分。人们好奇心太重；再说，一位绅士有时候兴许就爱深藏不露（lie perdu）。”皮科克先生对这种爱深藏不露的绅士趣味，虽说共鸣有限，却还是照办不误，一路没向任何人吐露去向，径直寻到了琼斯太太的寄宿公寓。

动身之前，他先备下一千美元的钞票带在身上——他觉得，照着两人的约定，这个可怜虫这份钱总算挣下了。他如今唯一的心愿，便是尽快把钱交割清楚，赶紧离开芝加哥。他只觉得自己仿佛也有几分做下亏心事的意味：不得不与这个人打交道，不得不偷偷给他钱，不得不把一桩旁人无由得知底细的安排贯彻到底。倘若芝加哥的警察撞见了他，把他当作一个正因旧金山伪造钞票案而遭“通缉”之人的朋友、甚至多半是同党，他该如何自处？可他自己也是无可奈何。在琼斯太太的店里，他看见他太太的小叔子正坐在酒馆的柜台边——那是美国闲汉们永远的盘踞之地——照例嘴里叼着一支雪茄，那份消磨光阴的功夫，全美国常泡这类地方的人独擅胜场。在英格兰，这样的人坐在这样的地方，身边多半得搁一杯掺了酒的什么混合饮料；美国人却从来不这么着。他要喝酒，便走到柜台前站着喝——兴许一杯接一杯，连饮两三杯；可一旦安顿下来要闲混了，他便嚼一支雪茄自足，把四周一圈地面吐满渣滓。靠着这点消遣，他尽可以一个钟头又一个钟头地心满意足待下去——只要没人逼他干更重的活计，甚至混上一整天也使得。此刻的罗伯特·勒弗罗伊便是这副模样。皮科克走进堂屋的时候，那人连身子也不从椅上抬一抬，只招呼了一声，仿佛他们一个钟头前才分手似的。“我说，老伙计，你可囫囵着回来啦。”
