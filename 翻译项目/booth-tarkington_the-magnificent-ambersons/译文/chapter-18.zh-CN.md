## XVIII / 十八

===Original===
That evening, after dinner, George sat with his mother and his Aunt Fanny upon the veranda. In former summers, when they sat outdoors in the evening, they had customarily used an open terrace at the side of the house, looking toward the Major’s, but that more private retreat now afforded too blank and abrupt a view of the nearest of the new houses; so, without consultation, they had abandoned it for the Romanesque stone structure in front, an oppressive place.

Its oppression seemed congenial to George; he sat upon the copestone of the stone parapet, his back against a stone pilaster; his attitude not comfortable, but rigid, and his silence not comfortable, either, but heavy. However, to the eyes of his mother and his aunt, who occupied wicker chairs at a little distance, he was almost indistinguishable except for the stiff white shield of his evening frontage.

“It’s so nice of you always to dress in the evening, Georgie,” his mother said, her glance resting upon this surface. “Your Uncle George always used to, and so did father, for years; but they both stopped quite a long time ago. Unless there’s some special occasion, it seems to me we don’t see it done any more, except on the stage and in the magazines.”

===Chinese===
那天傍晚，晚饭过后，乔治同母亲和姑妈范妮一道坐在阳台上。往年夏天，他们傍晚到户外坐，惯常是用房子侧面那方露天的平台，朝着少校家的方向；可那处更清静的退步之地，如今正对着新起的房子里最近的一幢，望过去空空荡荡、突兀逼人；于是谁也没商量，他们便弃了它，挪到前头那座罗马风式的石头凉台上——一个沉闷压人的地方。

这份沉闷倒像是同乔治投缘；他坐在石栏杆的压顶石上，背靠着根石壁柱；那姿势并不舒服，只是僵直；他的沉默也不舒服，只是沉重。不过，在他母亲和姑妈眼里——两位坐在稍远处的藤椅上——他几乎无从辨认，唯有那身晚礼服的前襟，挺得像一方僵硬的白盾。

“小乔治，你天天晚上都换上礼服，真是难得，”他母亲说，目光正落在这方白盾上。“你乔治舅舅从前一向如此，你父亲也是，好些年都是；可他们俩都歇下好久了。若没有什么特别的场合，我看如今已经见不到谁这么做了——戏台上和画报里除外。”

===Original===
He made no response, and Isabel, after waiting a little while, as if she expected one, appeared to acquiesce in his mood for silence, and turned her head to gaze thoughtfully out at the street.

There, in the highway, the evening life of the Midland city had begun. A rising moon was bright upon the tops of the shade trees, where their branches met overhead, arching across the street, but only filtered splashings of moonlight reached the block pavement below; and through this darkness flashed the firefly lights of silent bicycles gliding by in pairs and trios—or sometimes a dozen at a time might come, and not so silent, striking their little bells; the riders’ voices calling and laughing; while now and then a pair of invisible experts would pass, playing mandolin and guitar as if handlebars were of no account in the world—their music would come swiftly, and then too swiftly die away. Surreys rumbled lightly by, with the plod-plod of honest old horses, and frequently there was the glitter of whizzing spokes from a runabout or a sporting buggy, and the sharp, decisive hoof-beats of a trotter. Then, like a cowboy shooting up a peaceful camp, a frantic devil would hurtle out of the distance, bellowing, exhaust racketing like a machine gun gone amuck—and at these horrid sounds the surreys and buggies would hug the curbstone, and the bicycles scatter to cover, cursing; while children rushed from the sidewalks to drag pet dogs from the street. The thing would roar by, leaving a long wake of turbulence; then the indignant street would quiet down for a few minutes—till another came.

“There are a great many more than there used to be,” Miss Fanny observed, in her lifeless voice, as the lull fell after one of these visitations. “Eugene is right about that; there seem to be at least three or four times as many as there were last summer, and you never hear the ragamuffins shouting ‘Get a horse!’ nowadays; but I think he may be mistaken about their going on increasing after this. I don’t believe we’ll see so many next summer as we do now.”

===Chinese===
他没有应声。伊莎贝尔等了片刻，像是在等一句回话，随后像是默许了他这份沉默的性子，转过头去，若有所思地望着街上。

那里，大路上，米德兰城的夜生活已经开场。初升的月亮明晃晃地照着行道树的树梢——枝叶在头顶交握，拱成一道街廊——可落到下头的木块路面上，只剩下筛过的几点斑驳月光；黑暗之中，萤火虫似的点点亮光倏忽闪烁，是一辆辆无声的自行车结对成串滑行而过——有时一口气来上十几辆，就不那么安静了：小铃铛乱响，骑车人的喊声、笑声此起彼伏；还不时有一对看不见的高手路过，一边扶把一边弹着曼陀林和吉他，仿佛车把手在世上全然不算回事——乐声倏忽而来，又倏忽而逝，逝去得太快了些。轻便四轮马车辚辚驶过，伴着老实老马嘚、嘚的蹄声；轻便敞车或是竞赛轻便马车也常一闪而过，飞转的辐条闪闪发亮，快步马的蹄声干脆利落、斩钉截铁。接着，好比牛仔突袭一处酣睡的营地，一头发疯的恶煞会从远处横冲直撞而来，吼声如雷，排气管吵得像一挺发了狂的机关枪——一听见这骇人的声响，轻便四轮马车和轻便马车便纷纷贴向路缘，自行车四散隐蔽，咒骂不绝；人行道上的孩子一拥而下，把爱犬从街心拖开。那玩意儿吼叫着呼啸而过，留下长长一道翻腾的尾流；随后，满街的义愤平息几分钟——直到下一辆再来。

一场肆扰过后，街上暂归沉寂，范妮小姐用她那有气无力的嗓音说道：“比从前多出好多啦。这一点尤金说得不错；瞧着至少有去年夏天的三四倍，而且如今再也听不见街头的野孩子喊‘弄匹马来！’了；不过要说到往后还会一年多过一年，他兴许就想岔了。我不信明年夏天还能有眼下这么多。”

===Original===
“Why?” asked Isabel.

“Because I’ve begun to agree with George about their being more a fad than anything else, and I think it must be the height of the fad just now. You know how roller-skating came in—everybody in the world seemed to be crowding to the rinks—and now only a few children use rollers for getting to school. “Fanny!” exclaimed her sister-in-law. “You’re not in earnest?”

“I am, though!”

===Chinese===
“为什么？”伊莎贝尔问。

“因为我渐渐开始同意乔治的话了：汽车这东西，与其说是别的，不如说是一阵时髦，而眼下必定正是这时髦的顶点。你知道溜冰是怎样风行起来的——普天之下的人仿佛都往溜冰场里挤——如今只剩几个孩子踩着轮子鞋上学去了。再说，人们也不会容许汽车横行。真的，我看他们会立法治它们。你瞧它们把骑车和驾马兜风糟蹋成什么样；人人看样子都恨透了它们！大家绝不会容忍——断乎不会容忍！当然，这样的祸事若落到尤金头上，我心里也不好受；可要是真见一道法令颁下来，禁止销售汽车，就像禁售暗藏凶器那样，我也不会当真吃惊。”

“范妮！”她的妯娌失声叫道，“你不是当真吧？”

“我可是当真的！”

===Original===
Isabel’s sweet-toned laugh came out of the dusk where she sat. “Then you didn’t mean it when you told Eugene you’d enjoyed the drive this afternoon?”

“I didn’t say it so very enthusiastically, did I?”

“Perhaps not, but he certainly thought he’d pleased you.”

“I don’t think I gave him any right to think he’d pleased me,” Fanny said slowly.

“Why not? Why shouldn’t you, Fanny?”

Fanny did not reply at once, and when she did, her voice was almost inaudible, but much more reproachful than plaintive. “I hardly think I’d want anyone to get the notion he’d pleased me just now. It hardly seems time, yet—to me.”

范妮没有立刻答话；开口时，声音低得几乎听不见，可那腔调与其说是哀婉，不如说更是责备。“眼下这个节骨眼上，我可不觉得愿意让谁存下念头，以为他讨了我的欢心。时候还没到呢——照我看来。”

===Original===
Isabel made no response, and for a time the only sound upon the dark veranda was the creaking of the wicker rocking-chair in which Fanny sat—a creaking which seemed to denote content and placidity on the part of the chair’s occupant, though at this juncture a series of human shrieks could have been little more eloquent of emotional disturbance. However, the creaking gave its hearer one great advantage: it could be ignored.

“Have you given up smoking, George?” Isabel asked presently.

“No.”

“I hoped perhaps you had, because you’ve not smoked since dinner. We shan’t mind if you care to.”

“No, thanks.”

There was silence again, except for the creaking of the rocking-chair; then a low, clear whistle, singularly musical, was heard softly rendering an old air from *Fra Diavolo*. The creaking stopped.

Abruptly he swung his feet down from the copestone to the floor of the veranda. “Pardon nothing!” No meek Lucy had thrown herself in remorse at his feet; and now he pictured her as she probably really was at this moment: sitting on the white steps of her own front porch in the moonlight, with redheaded Fred Kinney and silly Charlie Johnson and four or five others—all of them laughing, most likely, and some idiot playing the guitar!
