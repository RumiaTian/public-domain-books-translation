===Original===
3. In Murakami’s *Harima Meisho-zuwe* (*Illustrated Description of the Province of Harima*, 1863), vol. III, Onöe (*wo no uhe*) is described as a pine-grove in Osada, where the shrines of two deities *Sumiyoshi Myojin* (Illustrious God), and *Ohara Dai myojin* (Great Illustrious God) exist. Finally there were three gods of Sumiyoshi, of the upper (or nearer?) middle (remote?) and bottom (furthest?) waters. When Jingu, the Queen-Regnant (AD 201﻿–﻿69), had completed her conquest of Korea, she built here the Sumiyoshi shrine and called the place Takasago (High Dune). Changes in the coastline occurred, and Takasago (which was a little port) disappeared, while Old Takasago became Onöe. [Possibly the twain trees originally grew near these shrines, and of their proximity the memory was preserved in the story, when Sumiyoshi in Tsu came into existence.]
There are two sayings about the pine-tree which are worth giving. One is *Matsu to ifu ji wo sakashima yomeba tsuma to naru no de ureshikaro*; if you read the syllabic characters of *ma* *tsu* (*matsu*, pine) backwards you have *tsu* *ma* (*tsuma*) “spouse,” which is, more japonico, a pleasant conceit. The other turns upon an analysis of the character 松 (pine-tree); *matsu to ifu ji wo wakachite yomeba kimi to boku to no futari-zure*, if you dissect the character for pine-tree you have 木 *boku*, “tree,” and 公 *kimi*, “you.” *Boku* is also the pronunciation of 僕 “I myself,” so that the saying means that the analysis of the character gives the pair of ego and tu. The one saying involves the notion of spousal love, the other that of friendship.
In Titsingh’s *Japan* will be found an illustration of Takasago no ura.

===Chinese===
3. 在村上氏的《播磨名所图会》（*Harima Meisho-zuwe*，《播磨国名所图说》，1863）卷三中，尾上（Onöe，*wo no uhe*）被描述为 Osada 的一处松原，那里座落着两座神社——*Sumiyoshi Myojin*（住吉明神）与 *Ohara Dai myojin*（小原大明神）。最终住吉有了三神，分镇上（较近？）中（较远？）下（最远？）三处水域。神功皇后（Jingu，公元 201–69）征服高丽之后，在此创建住吉神社，并命名此地为高砂（Takasago，高丘）。其后海岸线变迁，高砂（原是一个小港）湮没，旧高砂便成了尾上。〔或许这两株树原本就生长在这些神社近旁，及至津国之住吉出现，二者相邻的记忆便保存在了物语之中。〕
关于这株松树，有两句俗谚值得一提。其一是 *Matsu to ifu ji wo sakashima yomeba tsuma to naru no de ureshikaro*（松字倒读便成妻，故可喜）；把 *ma*、*tsu*（*matsu*，松）两个音节倒过来读，便得 *tsu*、*ma*（*tsuma*，「配偶」），以日本式的巧思而言，这是一则妙语。其二则建基于对「松」字的拆解：*Matsu to ifu ji wo wakachite yomeba kimi to boku to no futari-zure*（松字拆开读，便是君与我二人）；把松字拆开，得「木」（*boku*，「树」）与「公」（*kimi*，「你」）。*Boku* 又恰是「僕」（「我自己」）的读音，于是这句谚语的意思便是：拆字拆出了「我」与「你」一对。前一谚关涉夫妻之爱，后一谚关涉朋友之谊。
在蒂津（Titsingh）的《日本》一书中，可以找到高砂之浦（Takasago no ura）的插图。

===Original===
35. The priest has asked the villager why the woman speaks so sensibly, and suggested that he should say something that will set her off raving, so that he may be amused! The villager thereupon tells her that the blossoms are being scattered by the breeze.
A similar situation in the *Kiōjomono* is not infrequent. A mad woman is produced as an entertainment for visitors, and her ravings, serve for their diversion.
In *Minazukibarai*, for instance, a villager says to one who enquires what sights his district offers, “There is a young madwoman here﻿ ﻿… the way she dances about is most amusing﻿ ﻿… I’ll show her to you.”
In Hanjo, a servant, on his master’s behalf, addresses Hanako, “Why dost thou not rave to day, Mad Woman? Rave now to amuse us.” Strange idea of fun﻿—yet not so strange when one remembers that, a century ago, people of fashion used to make up parties to view the antics of lunatics confined in Bedlam.

===Chinese===
35. 法师问村人，那女子为何言语这般清明，并撺掇他说些能惹她狂乱的话，好让他取乐！村人于是告诉她，花儿正被风吹散。
狂女物（*Kiōjomono*）中类似的情境并不罕见。狂女被唤出来作客人的余兴，她的呓语狂言，便供人消遣。
例如在《六月祓》（*Minazukibarai*）中，村人回答询访本地有何可观的人说：「这里有个年轻的狂女……她手舞足蹈的模样最是有趣……我领你去见她。」
在《班女》（Hanjo）中，仆人奉主人之命向花子（Hanako）说：「狂女，你今日为何不发狂？快发狂来让我们高兴。」好奇怪的取乐之法——不过想到一个世纪前，时麾人物还常结伴去围观贝德拉姆（Bedlam）疯人院里疯人的丑态，也就不足为怪了。
