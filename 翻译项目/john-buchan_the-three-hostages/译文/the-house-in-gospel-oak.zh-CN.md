## VI The House in Gospel Oak / 六、福音橡的宅子

===Original===
It was a dry March afternoon, with one of those fantastic winds which seem to change their direction hourly, and contrive to be in a man’s face at every street corner. The dust was swirling in the gutters, and the scent of hyacinth and narcissus from the flower-shops was mingled with that bleak sandy smell which is London’s foretaste of spring. As I crossed Oxford Street I remember thinking what an odd pointless business I had drifted into. I saw nothing for it but to continue drifting and see what happened. I was on my way to visit a doctor of whom I knew nothing, about some ailment which I was not conscious of possessing. I didn’t even trouble to make a plan, being content to let chance have the guiding of me.

The house was one of those solid dreary erections which have usually the names of half a dozen doctors on their front doors. But in this case there was only one﻿—Dr. M. Newhover. The parlourmaid took me into the usual drab waiting-room furnished with Royal Academy engravings, fumed oak, and an assortment of belated picture-papers, and almost at once she returned and ushered me into the consulting-room. This again was of the most ordinary kind﻿—glazed bookcases, wash-hand basin in a corner, roll-top desk, a table with a medical journal or two and some leather cases. And Dr. Newhover at first sight seemed nothing out of the common. He was a youngish man, with high cheekbones, a high forehead, and a quantity of blond hair brushed straight back from it. He wore a pince-nez, and when he removed it showed pale prominent blue eyes. From his look I should have said that his father had called himself Neuhofer.

===Chinese===
那是个干燥的三月午后，刮着那种变幻莫测的怪风——风向仿佛一个钟头一变，偏偏有本事在每一个街角迎面撞上人的脸。尘土在阴沟里打着旋，花店里风信子和水仙的香气，同那种冷飕飕的沙土味搅在一起——那是伦敦对春天的预告。穿过牛津街（Oxford Street）时，我记得自己心里正想：我漂漂流流卷进的这桩事，多么古怪而无谓。我看不出别的法子，只好继续漂流，且看下文。我正要去拜访一位我对其一无所知的医生，看一种我并不觉得自己患上的病症。我甚至懒得定个计划，甘愿听凭机缘替我领路。

那宅子是常见的那种敦实而阴沉的房屋，门脸上通常钉着半打医生的名牌。但这一家只有一块——M·纽霍弗医生。客厅女仆把我领进一间惯常那种灰扑扑的候诊室，屋里陈设着皇家美术学院（Royal Academy）的印画、烟熏色的橡木家具，还有一堆过了期的画报；她几乎立刻就回来了，引我进了诊室。这间屋子也是再普通不过的——玻璃门书柜，墙角一只洗脸盆，一张卷盖式书桌，一张桌上搁着一两本医学杂志和几只皮匣。纽霍弗医生乍看也毫不出奇。他是个还年轻模样的男子，颧骨高，额头高，一头浓密的金发从额前直直往后梳。他戴一副夹鼻眼镜，摘下来时，露出一双苍白外凸的蓝眼睛。看他的相貌，我敢说他的父亲当年自称的，想必还是德文原拼的“纽霍弗”（Neuhofer）。

===Original===
He greeted me with a manner which seemed to me to be at once patronising and dictatorial. I wondered if he was some tremendous swell in his profession, of whom I ought to have heard. “Well, Mr. Hannay, what can I do for you?” he said. I noticed that he called me “Mr.,” though I had given “Sir Richard” both on the telephone and to the parlourmaid. It occurred to me that someone had already been speaking of me to him, and that he had got the name wrong in his memory.

I thought I had better expound the alarming symptoms with which I had awakened that morning.

“I don’t know what’s gone wrong with me,” I said. “I’ve a pain behind my eyeballs, and my whole head seems muddled up. I feel drowsy and slack, and I’ve got a weakness in my legs and back like a man who has just had flu.”

===Chinese===
他向我致意的那副做派，在我看来既居高临下，又专断独行。我纳闷，他是不是医界什么了不得的大人物，是我本该有所耳闻的。“唔，汉内先生，我能为你做点什么？”他说。我留意到他称我“先生”，尽管我在电话里和向客厅女仆通报时，给的都是“理查德爵士”。我随即想到，想必已经有人在他面前说起过我，只是他把名字记岔了。

我想，还是把那天早晨醒来时那套吓人的症状陈述一番为好。

“我不知道自己哪儿出了毛病，”我说。“我眼球后面疼，整个脑袋像是搅成一团浆糊。我又困又乏，腿上背上发虚，活像刚害过一场流感的人。”

===Original===
He made me sit down and proceeded to catechise me about my health. I said it had been good enough, but I mentioned my old malaria and several concussions, and I pretended to be pretty nervous about my condition. Then he went through the whole bag of tricks﻿—sounding me with a stethoscope, testing my blood pressure, and hitting me hard below the knee to see if I reacted. I had to play up to my part, but upon my soul I came near reacting too vigorously to some of his questions and boxing his ears. Always he kept up that odd, intimate, domineering, rather offensive manner.

He made me lie down on a couch while he fingered the muscles of my neck and shoulder and seemed to be shampooing my head with his long chilly hands. I was by this time feeling rather extra well, but I managed to invent little tendernesses here and there and a lot of alarming mental aberrations. I wondered if he were not getting suspicious, for he asked abruptly: “Have you had these symptoms long?” so I thought it better to return to the truth, and told him “only since this morning.”

===Chinese===
他叫我坐下，接着便盘问起我的健康史来。我说一向够好，但提了我那老疟疾和几次脑震荡，还装出对自己的病症颇为心慌的样子。随后他把全套家什演习了一遍——用听诊器给我听诊，量我的血压，又使劲敲我膝盖下方，看我的反应如何。我不得不把戏演足，可我发誓，他问某些问题时，我险些反应过猛，想扇他耳光。他自始至终端着那副古怪的、亲昵的、盛气凌人而相当惹人厌的做派。

他又叫我躺上一张诊床，一面用手指捏揉我脖颈和肩头的肌肉，一面用那双冰凉的长手像洗头似的揉我的脑袋。这会儿我自觉好得不能再好，却还是编出了东一处西一处的小压痛，外加一大堆吓人的精神失调。我拿不准他是不是起了疑心，因为他冷不丁问了一句：“这些症状有多久了？”我想还是回到实话上稳妥，便告诉他“今天早上才开始的”。

===Original===
At last he bade me get up, took off the tortoiseshell spectacles he had been wearing and resumed his pince-nez, and while I was buttoning my collar seemed to be sunk in reflection. He made me sit in the patient’s chair, and stood up and looked down on me with a magisterial air that made me want to laugh.

“You are suffering,” he said, “from a somewhat abnormal form of a common enough complaint. Just as the effects of a concussion are often manifest only some days after the blow, so the results of nervous strain may take a long time to develop. I have no doubt that in spite of your good health you have during recent years been working your mind and body at an undue pressure, and now this morning quite suddenly you reap the fruits. I don’t want to frighten you, Mr. Hannay, but neurosis is so mysterious a disease in its working that we must take it seriously, especially at its first manifestations. There are one or two points in your case which I am not happy about. There is, for example, a certain congestion﻿—or what seems to me a congestion﻿—in the nerve centres of the neck and head. That may be induced by the accidents﻿—concussion and the like﻿—which you have told me of, or it may not. The true cure must, of course, take time, and rest and change of scene are obligatory. You are fond of sport? A fisherman?”

===Chinese===
末了他叫我起身，摘下一直戴着的龟甲框眼镜，重新架上夹鼻眼镜；我扣衣领的当口，他仿佛沉入了深思。他让我坐进病人坐的那把椅子，自己站着，居高临下地俯视我，一副法官老爷的气派，叫我直想发笑。

“你患的，”他说，“是一种相当常见的毛病，只是形态略微反常。正如脑震荡的后果往往在受击之后好几天才显露，神经劳损的结果也可能要很久才发作。尽管你体格健壮，我毫不怀疑，近年来你的身心一直在超负荷运转，而今早，果子突然就熟了。我不想吓唬你，汉内先生，可是神经官能症这种病，机理神秘，我们必须认真对待，尤其在初发阶段。你的病症里有一两处我放心不下。譬如说，脖颈和头部的神经中枢有一定程度的充血——或者在我看来像是充血。这也许是你对我说起的那些意外——脑震荡之类——引起的，也许不是。除根治本自然需要时日，休息与换换环境则是必不可少的。你爱好运动吧？钓鱼？”

===Original===
I told him I was.

“Well, a little later I may prescribe a salmon river in Norway. The remoteness of the life from ordinary existence and the contemplation of swift running water have had wonderful results with some of my patients. But Norway is not possible till May, and in the meantime I am going to order you specific treatment. Yes. I mean massage, but by no means ordinary massage. That science is still in its infancy, and its practitioners are only fumbling at the doorway. But now and then we find a person, man or woman, with a kind of extra sense for disentangling and smoothing out muscular and nervous abnormalities. I am going to send you to such an one. The address may surprise you, but you are man of the world enough to know that medical skill is not confined to the area between Oxford Street and the Marylebone Road.” He took off his glasses, and smiled.

Then he wrote something on a slip of paper and handed it to me. I read “Madame Breda, 4 Palmyra Square, N.W.”

===Chinese===
我告诉他，钓。

“唔，再晚些时候，我或许会给你开一条挪威的鲑鱼河。那种生活远离日常尘世，再加上凝望奔流的活水，对我的一些病人出过奇效。不过挪威要等到五月才成，眼下我先给你安排一种专门治疗。是的，我说的是按摩，但绝非寻常的按摩。那门学问还在襁褓之中，从业者不过在门口瞎摸索。可我们偶尔会碰到一种人，男的或女的，天生多出一种官能，善于解开、抚平肌肉和神经上的种种反常。我打算把你送到这样一个人那里去。那个地址也许会让你诧异，不过你是见过世面的人，该知道医术并非只长在牛津街到马里波恩路（Marylebone Road）之间那一块。”他摘下眼镜，笑了笑。

然后他在一张纸条上写了几个字，递给我。我读到：“布雷达夫人（Madame Breda），帕尔米拉广场4号，西北邮区。”

===Original===
“Right!” I said. “Much obliged to you. I hope Madame Breda will cure this infernal headache. When can I see her?”

“I can promise you she will cure the headache. She is a Swedish lady who has lived in London since the War, and is so much an enthusiast in her art that she will only now and then take a private patient. For the most part she gives her skill free to the children’s hospitals. But she will not refuse me. As for beginning, I should lose no time for the sake of your own comfort. What about tomorrow morning?”

===Chinese===
“好！”我说。“多谢您了。但愿布雷达夫人能治好这要命的头痛。我什么时候能见她？”

“头痛她手到病除，这我可以向你担保。她是一位瑞典太太，大战以来一直住在伦敦，对自己的手艺热忱得很，如今只是偶尔才接一位私人病人。她的大半手艺，都免费施给儿童医院。不过她不会驳我的面子。至于开始嘛，就你自己舒坦着想，也不宜耽搁。明天上午如何？”

===Original===
“Why not tonight? I have nothing to do, and I want to be quit of my headache before bedtime. Why shouldn’t I go on there now?”

“No reason in the world. But I must make an appointment. Madame is on the telephone. Excuse me a moment.”

He left the room and returned in a few minutes to say that he had made an appointment for seven o’clock. “It is an outlandish place to get to, but most taxi-drivers know it. If your man doesn’t, tell him to drive to Gospel Oak, and then any policeman will direct you.”

===Chinese===
“干吗不今晚？我闲着没事，还想赶在睡觉前甩掉这头痛呢。我现在就上那儿去，有什么不成的？”

“一点儿问题都没有。不过我得先约好。夫人装着电话。失陪片刻。”

他出了诊室，几分钟后回来说，已经替我约好了七点钟。“那地方偏僻得很，不过开车的多半认得。要是你的司机不认得，就叫他往福音橡（Gospel Oak）开，到了那儿随便哪个警察都能给你指路。”

===Original===
I had my chequebook with me, but he didn’t want his fee, saying that he was not done with me. I was to come back in a week and report progress. As I left I had a strong impression of a hand as cold as a snake, pale bulging eyes, and cheekbones like a caricature of a Scotchman. An odd but rather impressive figure was Dr. Newhover. He didn’t look a fool, and if I hadn’t known the uncommon toughness of my constitution I might have been unsettled by his forebodings.

===Chinese===
我随身带着支票簿，可他不收诊金，说我的事还没了结，要我一周后回来报告进展。告辞出来，我脑中留着鲜明的印象：一只蛇一般冰凉的手，一双苍白外凸的眼睛，还有一副活像苏格兰人漫画像的颧骨。纽霍弗医生这人古怪，却颇有威势。他看着不像蠢人，若不是深知自己这副筋骨异乎寻常地结实，他那番不祥的预言，没准真会叫我心里发毛。

===Original===
I walked down to Oxford Street and had tea in a teashop. As I sat among the chattering typists and shopboys I kept wondering whether I was not wasting my time and behaving like a jackass. Here was I, as fit as a hunter, consulting specialists and visiting unknown masseuses in North London, and all with no clear purpose. In less than twenty-four hours I had tumbled into a perfectly crazy world, and for a second I had a horrid doubt whether the craziness was not inside my mind. Had something given in my brain last night in Medina’s room, so that now I was what people call “wanting”? I went over the sequence of events again, and was reassured by remembering that in it all I had kept my head. I had not got to the stage of making theories; I was still only waiting on developments, and I couldn’t see any other way before me. I must, of course, get hold of Sandy, but first let me see what this massage business meant. It might all be perfectly square; I might have remembered Dr. Newhover’s name by a queer trick of memory﻿—heard it, perhaps, from some friend﻿—and that remarkable practitioner might be quite honest. But then I remembered the man’s manner﻿—I was quite clear that he knew something of me, that someone had told him to expect me. Then it occurred to me that I might be doing a rash thing in going off to an unknown house in a seedy suburb. So I went into a public telephone-booth, rang up the Club, and told the porter that if Colonel Arbuthnot called, I was at 4 Palmyra Square, N.W.﻿—I made him write down the address﻿—and would be back before ten o’clock.

===Chinese===
我步行到牛津街，进了一家茶馆用茶。坐在叽叽喳喳的打字员和店伙中间，我不住地犯嘀咕：我这算不算虚耗光阴、像蠢驴一样瞎折腾？瞧瞧我，精神抖擞得赛过一匹猎马，却跑来咨询专科医生，跑到北伦敦去拜访素不相识的女按摩师，而这一切全无明确目的。不到二十四小时，我跌进了一个彻头彻尾疯狂的世界；有一瞬间，我起了一阵可怕的疑心：这疯狂莫不是长在我自己脑子里？昨夜在梅迪纳的宅子里，我脑子里可有什么东西断了弦，以至于如今成了人们所说的“缺心眼”？我把事件的先后又捋了一遍，想起这一路我始终没乱方寸，心才放了下来。我还没到创立理论的阶段；我仍只是在静候事态发展，而且眼前也看不出别的路。当然，我得找到桑迪，不过先得弄清这按摩的勾当是什么名堂。这一切也许全然清白；纽霍弗医生的名字，也许是我记忆里耍的一个古怪把戏——或许是打哪位朋友那儿听来的——而那位了不得的医师也许十足老实。可接着我又想起那人的做派——我清清楚楚地知道，他掌握着我的某些底细，有人告诉过他我会来。随后我又想到，就这么跑到破落郊区一所陌生宅子里去，兴许是鲁莽之举。于是我走进一个公共电话亭，给俱乐部挂了电话，告诉门房：若是阿巴思诺特上校来找我，我在帕尔米拉广场4号，西北邮区——我让他把地址记了下来——十点前准回。

I was rather short of exercise, so I decided to walk, since I had plenty of time. Strangely enough, the road was pretty much that which I had taken on that June day of 1914 when I had been waiting on Bullivant and the Black Stone gentry, and had walked clean out of London to pass the time.1 Then, I remembered, I had been thrilling with wild anticipation, but now I was an older and much wiser man, and though I was sufficiently puzzled I could curb my restlessness with philosophy. I went up Portland Place, past the Regent’s Park, till I left the houses of the well-to-do behind me, and got into that belt of mean streets which is the glacis of the northern heights. Various policemen directed me, and I enjoyed the walk as if I had been exploring, for London is always to me an undiscovered country. I passed yards which not so long ago had been patches of market-garden, and terraces, sometime pretentious, and now sinking into slums; for London is like the tropical bush﻿—if you don’t exercise constant care the jungle, in the shape of the slums, will break in. The streets were full of clerks and shopgirls waiting for buses, and workmen from the St. Pancras and Clerkenwell factories going home. The wind was rising, and in the untidy alleys was stirring up a noisome dust; but as the ground rose it blew cleaner and seemed to bring from Kentish fields and the Channel the tonic freshness of spring. I stopped for a little and watched behind me the plain of lights, which was London, quivering in the dark-blue windy dusk.

我正缺运动，时间又充裕，便决定步行前往。说来也怪，这条路竟同1914年6月的那一天我走的那条大同小异——那时我为布利文特和黑石那帮家伙奔走，为了消磨时光，一口气徒步走出了伦敦城。1我记得，当年我浑身沸腾着狂野的期盼，如今我年纪长了，人也世故得多，虽说满腹狐疑，却还能用哲理按捺住性子里的躁动。我走上波特兰街（Portland Place），经过摄政公园（Regent's Park），把殷实人家的宅第一一甩在身后，走进那一带穷街陋巷——它们是北面高地脚下的一道斜堤。好几位警察为我指路，这一路我走得津津有味，仿佛在探险，因为伦敦于我永远是一片未知的国土。我经过一座座院场，不久前它们还是一方方菜园；经过一排排联排住宅，当年想必也曾气派，如今正沦入贫民窟；伦敦就像热带丛林——若不时时经心，丛林便会化作贫民窟破门而入。街上满是等公共汽车的职员和女店员，还有圣潘克拉斯（St. Pancras）和克勒肯维尔（Clerkenwell）各厂坊下工回家的工人。风越刮越紧，在凌乱的小巷里卷起一股呛人的尘土；可随着地势渐高，风也变得干净起来，仿佛把肯特郡（Kent）田野和英吉利海峡上那股提振精神的春日清新都吹了过来。我停步回望，身后那一片灯的平原便是伦敦，在深蓝多风的暮色里微微颤动。

It was almost dark when at last, after several false casts, I came into Palmyra Square. It was a square only in name, for one side was filled with a warehouse of sorts, and another straggled away in nests of small brick houses. One side was a terrace of artisans’ dwellings, quite new, each with a tiny bow-window and names like “Chatsworth” and “Kitchener Villa.” The fourth side, facing south, had once had a certain dignity, and the builder who had designed the place seventy years ago had thought, no doubt, that he was creating a desirable residential quarter. There the houses stood apart, each in a patch of garden, which may at one time have had lawns and flowers. Now these gardens were mere dusty yards, the refuge of tin cans and bits of paper, and only a blackened elm, an ill-grown privet hedge, and some stunted lilacs told of the more cheerful past.
