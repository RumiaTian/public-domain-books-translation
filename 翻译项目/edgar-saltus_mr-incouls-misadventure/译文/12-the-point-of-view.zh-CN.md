## X The Point of View / 第十章 视角

===Original===
On leaving the villa Lenox Leigh experienced a number of conflicting emotions, and at last found relief in sleep. The day that followed he passed in chambered solitude; it was possible that some delegate from Mr. Incoul might wish to exchange a word with him, and in accordance with the unwritten statutes of what is seemly, it behooved him to be in readiness for the exchange of that word. Moreover, he was expectant of a line from Maida, some word indicative of the course of conduct which he should pursue, some message, in fact, which would aid him to rise from the uncertainty in which he groped. As a consequence he remained in his room. He was not one to whom solitude is irksome, indeed he had often found it grateful in its refreshment, but to be enjoyable solitude should not be coupled with suspense; in that case it is uneasiness magnified by the infinite. And if fear be analyzed, what is it save the dread of the unknown? When the nerves are unstrung a calamity is often a tonic. The worst that can be has been done, the blow has fallen, and with the falling fear vanishes, hope returns, the healing process begins at once.
The uneasiness which visited Lenox Leigh came precisely from his inability to determine whether or not a blow was impending. As to the blow, he cared, in the abstract, very little. If it were to be given, let it be dealt and be done with; that which alone troubled him was his ignorance of what had ensued after his meeting with Mr. Incoul, and his incapacity to foresee in what manner the consequences of that meeting would affect his relations with Mr. Incoul’s wife.
In this uncertainty he looked at the matter from every side, and, that he might get the broadest view, he recalled the incidents connected with the meeting. The facts of the case seemed then to resolve themselves into this: Mr. Incoul had unexpectedly returned to his home after midnight, and had met a friend of his wife’s descending the stair. Their greeting, if formal, had been perfectly courteous. The departing guest had informed the returning husband at what hotel he was stopping, and that gentleman had expressed the hope that he was comfortable. Certainly there was nothing extraordinary in that. People who dwelled in recondite regions might see impropriety in a call that extended up to and beyond midnight, whereas others who lived in more liberal centres might consider it the most natural thing in the world. It was, then, merely the point of view, and what was the point of view which Mr. Incoul had adopted? If he considered it an impropriety why had he seemed so indifferent? And, if he considered it natural and proper, why should he have been so damned civil? Why should he have expressed the hope that his wife’s guest was comfortable at a hotel? Was the expression of that hope merely a commonplace rejoinder, or was it an intentional slur? Surely, everyone possessed of the brain of a medium-sized rabbit feels that it is as absurd to expect an intelligent being to be comfortable in a hotel as it is to suppose that he can find enjoyment in an evening party or amusement in a comic paper. Then again, and this, after all, was the great question: was the return of Mr. Incoul intentional or accidental? If it was intentional, if he had gone away intending that he would be absent all night merely that by an unexpected return he might verify any suspicions which he may have harbored, then in driving to his door in a rumbling coach he had shown himself a very poor plotter. On the other hand, if the return were accidental had it served to turn a suspicionless husband into a suspicious one, and if it had so served, how far did those suspicions extend? Did he think that his wife and her guest had been occupied with aimless chitchat, or did he believe that their conversation had been of a personal and intimate nature?
As Lenox pondered over these things it seemed to him that, let Mr. Incoul suspect what he might, the one and unique cause for apprehension lay in the attitude which Maida had assumed when her husband, after closing the door, had gone to her in search of an explanation. That he had so gone there was to him no possible doubt. And it was in the expectancy of tidings as to the result of that explanation that he waited the entire day in his room.

离开别墅之后，伦诺克斯·利心里掠过种种相互抵触的情绪，末了是在睡眠里寻得解脱的。第二天他在与外界隔开的独处中度过：因库尔先生没准会派个什么代表来，想同他交换一句话，而依照体面的不成文法，他理应待命，好完成那句话的交换。再说，他还指望玛伊达来一行字，某个示意他此后行止的字眼，某条实际能帮他从未知的迷雾里站起来的消息。于是他待在房间里。他不是那种觉得独处难熬的人，独处的滋养他常觉受用；只是独处要滋味好，便不该掺着悬心；一掺，不安就被无限放大了。再说恐惧若加以剖析，除了对未知的惧怕还能是什么？神经一垮，灾祸倒往往是补剂。最坏的既已发生，打击既已落下，随着那一落，恐惧烟消云散，希望回头，疗愈立刻开始。
造访伦诺克斯·利的不安，恰恰来自他断不定有没有一场打击正在逼近。至于打击本身，抽象地说，他并不大在乎。要来，就让它来，来过拉倒；唯一搅扰他的，是他不知道同因库尔先生碰面之后接着发生了什么，是他无从预见那次见面的后果会以何种方式影响他与因库尔先生之妻的关系。
在这份悬而未决里，他把事情翻来覆去看了个遍；为了看得最全，他把与那次见面相关的种种细节回想了一遍。案情的实情似乎归结如下：因库尔先生午夜之后出乎意料地回了家，在楼梯上遇见一位正下楼的他太太的朋友。两人的寒暄纵然拘礼，却客气到家。离去的客人告知归来的丈夫自己下榻在哪座旅馆，那位先生则表示希望他住得舒服。这当然毫无出奇之处。住在幽僻地方的人，兴许会觉得一场拖到午夜之后还在继续的拜访有失体统；而住在风气更开通的地方的人，则会认为这是天下再自然不过的事。可见这不过是视角问题，而因库尔先生采用的是哪个视角？他若认作失体统，何以显得那样淡漠？他若认作自然得体，又何以那样见鬼的客气？他何以要表示希望太太的客人在旅馆里住得舒服？那希望是一句随口的客套呢，还是一记存心的挖苦？当然，凡是脑子不小于中等兔子的人都知道：指望一个有灵性的人在旅馆里舒服，同指望他从晚会里得乐趣、从滑稽小报里得消遣一样荒唐。再者——这才是要害——因库尔先生的回返，是蓄意还是偶然？若是蓄意，若他出门时便存心整夜不归，只为杀个回马枪好坐实他或许早怀着的疑心，那么他坐着隆隆作响的马车赶回自家门前，就把自己证明成一个很不入流的设局者。反过来，若是偶然，这次回返有没有把一个无疑心的丈夫变成一个起疑的丈夫？若变了，那疑心又到了几分？他是以为太太和她的客人尽在闲扯些没要紧的，还是认定他们的谈话已经私人而亲密？
伦诺克斯盘算着这些，觉得不论因库尔先生疑什么，唯一堪忧的，是玛伊达在丈夫关门之后到她那里讨说法时所摆的态度。他确曾去了她那里，这一点毫无疑问。而伦诺克斯一整天守在房里等的，正是那场说法的结果的消息。

But the afternoon waned into dusk and still no tidings came. As the hours wore on his uneasiness decreased. “Bah!” he muttered to himself at last, “in the winter I gave all my mornings to Pyrrho and Aenesidemus, and here six months later during an entire day I bother myself about eventualities.”
He sighed wearily with an air of self-disgust, and rising from the sofa on which his meditations had been passed he went to the window. The Casino opposite was already illuminated. “They will be there tonight,” he thought. “I have been a fool for my pains. If Maida hasn’t written it is because there has been nothing to write. I will look them up after dinner and everything will be as before.” He took off his morning suit and got himself into evening dress. He tied his white cravat without emotion, with a precision that was geometric in its accuracy, and to hold the tie in place he ran a silver pin through the collar without so much as pricking his neck. He was thoroughly at ease. The fear of the blow had passed. Pyrrho, Aenesidemus, the whole corps of ataraxists had surged suddenly and rescued him from the toils of the inscrutable.
At a florist’s in the street below he found an orchid with which he decked his buttonhole, and then in search of dinner he sauntered into Helder’s, a restaurant on the main street, a trifle above the Grand Hôtel. It was crowded; there did not seem to be a single table unoccupied. He hesitated for a moment, and was about to go elsewhere when he noticed someone signaling to him from the remoter end of the garden. He could not at first make out who it was and it was not until he had made use of a monocle that he recognized a fellow Baltimorean, Mr. Clarence May, with whom in days gone by he had been on terms approaching those of intimacy.
Mr. Clarence May, more familiarly known as Clara, was a pigeon-shooter who for some years past had been promenading the side scenes of continental life. He was well known in the penal colonies of the Riviera, and hand-in-glove with some of the most distinguished *rastaquouères*, yet did he happen in a proscenium it was by accident. In appearance he was not beautiful: he was a meagre little man, possessed of vague features and an allowance of sandy hair so undetermined that few were able to remember whether or not he wore any on his face. When he spoke it was with a slight stutter, a trick of speech which he declared he had inherited from his wet-nurse.
