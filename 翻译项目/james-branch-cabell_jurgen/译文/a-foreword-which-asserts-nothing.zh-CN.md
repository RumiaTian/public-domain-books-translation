## A Foreword: Which Asserts Nothing / 无所断言的前言
===Original===

===Chinese===

===Original===

In Continental periodicals not more than a dozen articles in all would seem to have given accounts or partial translations of the Jurgen legends. No thorough investigation of this epos can be said to have appeared in print, anywhere, prior to the publication, in 1913, of the monumental *Synopses of Aryan Mythology* by Angelo de Ruiz. It is unnecessary to observe that in this exhaustive digest Professor de Ruiz has given (VII, p. 415 *et sequentia*) a summary of the greater part of these legends as contained in the collections of Verville and Bülg; and has discussed at length and with much learning the esoteric meaning of these folk-stories and their bearing upon questions to which the “solar theory” of myth explanation has given rise. To his volumes, and to the pages of Mr. Lewistam’s *Key to the Popular Tales of Poictesme*, must be referred all those who may elect to think of Jurgen as the resplendent, journeying and procreative sun.
Equally in reading hereinafter will the judicious waive all allegorical interpretation, if merely because the suggestions hitherto advanced are inconveniently various. Thus Verville finds the Nessus shirt a symbol of retribution, where Bülg, with rather wide divergence, would have it represent the dangerous gift of genius. Then it may be remembered that Dr. Codman says, without any hesitancy, of Mother Sereda: “This Mother Middle is the world generally (an obvious anagram of *Erda es*), and this Sereda rules not merely the middle of the working-days but the midst of everything. She is the factor of *middleness*, of mediocrity, of an avoidance of extremes, of the eternal compromise begotten by use and wont. She is the Mrs. Grundy of the Léshy; she is Comstockery: and her shadow is common sense.” Yet Codman speaks with certainly no more authority than Prote, when the latter, in his *Origins of Fable*, declares this epos is “a parable of﻿ ﻿… man’s vain journeying in search of that rationality and justice which his nature craves, and discovers nowhere in the universe: and the shirt is an emblem of this instinctive craving, as﻿ ﻿… the shadow symbolizes conscience. Sereda typifies a surrender to life as it is, a giving up of man’s rebellious self-centredness and selfishness: the anagram being *se dare*.”
Thus do interpretations throng and clash, and neatly equal the commentators in number.

在欧洲大陆的期刊上，叙及或部分翻译朱尔根（Jurgen）传说的文章，统共恐怕也不过十来篇。在这部史诗付梓印刷之前，世间任何角落都未曾出现过哪怕一篇详尽探究——直至1913年，安杰洛·德·鲁伊斯那部皇皇巨著《雅利安神话纲要》面世。无须多言，在这部包罗万象的辑要中，德·鲁伊斯教授（第七卷，第415页及后文）将韦尔维尔与比尔格所搜集的这些传说作了大体概述，并以渊博学识详加探讨了这些民间故事的秘传寓意，以及它们与神话“太阳论”所引发的诸多争议之间的关联。凡是倾向于将朱尔根视作光芒万丈、巡行周天且滋生万物的太阳之人，皆当去参阅教授的宏卷，以及刘易斯塔姆先生所著《波伊捷斯民间故事要略》的篇章。

同样，明达的读者在阅读后文时，也会明智地摈除一切寓言式附会，单单是因为迄今为止提出的种种解释未免过于五花八门。例如，韦尔维尔将涅索斯之衫（Nessus shirt）视为天道报应的象征；而比尔格则大相径庭，认为它代表天才那份凶险的禀赋。此外想必有人记得，科德曼博士谈及谢列达母亲（Mother Sereda）时曾毫不犹豫地宣称：“这位‘中庸之母’大体便是这尘世（显然是Erda es的易位变位），而这位谢列达所掌管的不仅是劳作日的正中，更是世间万物的正中。她是‘折中性’与庸碌凡常的化身，是规避极端的代表，是习惯与风俗所孕育的永恒妥协。她是森林精怪中的格伦迪太太；她是康斯托克主义道德审查的化身；而她的阴影便是常识。”然而，科德曼的权威性断然不会高于普罗特——后者在《寓言之起源》中断言，这部史诗实乃“一则寓言……喻示人类徒劳奔波，苦苦探寻其天性所渴望、却在宇宙间无处觅得的理性与公正；而那件衬衣便是这种本能渴求的象征，正如……阴影象征着良知。谢列达则体现了对现实生活的屈从，是对人类叛逆的自我中心与自私自利的放弃：其异序变位词正是se dare（自我献出）。”

种种释义便如此蜂拥相撞、互不相让，其数目恰好与阐释者的人数相当。

Yet possibly each one of these unriddlings, with no doubt a host of others, is conceivable: so that wisdom will dwell upon none of them very seriously.
With the origin and the occult meaning of the folklore of Poictesme this book at least is in no wise concerned: its unambitious aim has been merely to familiarize English readers with the Jurgen epos for the tale’s sake. And this tale of old years is one which, by rare fortune, can be given to English readers almost unabridged, in view of the singular delicacy and pure-mindedness of the Jurgen mythos: in all, not more than a half-dozen deletions have seemed expedient (and have been duly indicated) in order to remove such sparse and unimportant outcroppings of medieval frankness as might conceivably offend the squeamish.
Since this volume is presented simply as a story to be read for pastime, neither morality nor symbolism is hereinafter educed, and no “parallels” and “authorities” are quoted. Even the gaps are left unbridged by guesswork: whereas the historic and mythological problems perhaps involved are relinquished to those really thoroughgoing scholars whom erudition qualifies to deal with such topics, and tedium does not deter.﻿ ﻿…
In such terms, and thus far, ran the Foreword to the first issues of this book, whose later fortunes have made necessary the lengthening of the Foreword with a postscript. The needed addition﻿—this much at least chiming with good luck﻿—is brief. It is just that fragment which some scholars, since the first appearance of this volume, have asserted﻿—upon what perfect frankness must describe as not indisputable grounds﻿—to be a portion of the thirty-second chapter of the complete form of *La Haulte Histoire de Jurgen*.
And in reply to what these scholars assert, discretion says nothing. For this fragment was, of course, unknown when the High History was first put into English, and there in consequence appears, here, little to be won either by endorsing or denying its claims to authenticity. Rather, does discretion prompt the appending, without any gloss or scholia, of this fragment, which deals with
*The Judging of Jurgen.*
Now a court was held by the Philistines to decide whether or no King Jurgen should be relegated to limbo.

然而，上述每一种解读，连同其余无数未及备载的阐释，或许皆自成一理；因此智者断不会将其中任何一种太当回事。

至于波伊捷斯（Poictesme）民俗的源起与幽微隐义，本书至少是全然不予挂怀的：它并无宏愿，不过是想为了故事本身，让英文读者领略朱尔根的传奇史诗。鉴于朱尔根神话格外典雅纯洁，此一古老故事极尽幸运，几乎可以不作删节便呈献给英文读者：为了剔除几处可能冒犯过分拘谨之人的零星、无关紧要的中世纪直白流露，全书通共仅作了不过半打权宜性的删节（且均已妥善标明）。

本书纯粹是作为供人消遣的故事而呈现，故后文既不推演道德寓意，亦不牵扯象征符号，更不援引任何“参照文献”与“权威定论”。纵使留有缺漏，亦不作主观臆断的补缀；至于其中可能牵涉的历史与神话难题，则尽数留给那些真正博古通今的学者去料理——唯有他们的渊博学识堪配探究此类议题，且耐得住枯燥繁琐。……

本书初版之前言，辞句止于此，概貌亦如是。嗣后的际遇，却使得此前言不得不添上一篇补记。此项补遗——至少在幸事一端尚称相符——极为简短。自本书首度面世以来，便有若干学者据理力争——若秉持全然之坦诚，其依据实难称无可辩驳——断言此乃《朱尔根正史》全本第三十二章之一节遗篇。

对于这些学者的断言，审慎之态唯有保持缄默。盖因昔日将《正史》译为英文之际，此一残卷尚无人知晓；故而无论在此认可抑或否认其真伪，皆无甚裨益。倒不如出于谨慎，将这一残卷不加按语、不置评注地附于文末。残卷所叙如下：

《朱尔根受审》

话说非利士人（Philistines）设下一处法庭，以裁决是否应当将朱尔根国王贬入幽冥虚空。

And when the judges were prepared for judging, there came into the court a great tumblebug, rolling in front of him his loved and properly housed young ones. With the creature came pages, in black and white, bearing a sword, a staff and a lance.
This insect looked at Jurgen, and its pincers rose erect in horror. The bug cried to the three judges, “Now, by St. Anthony! this Jurgen must forthwith be relegated to limbo, for he is offensive and lewd and lascivious and indecent.”
“And how can that be?” says Jurgen.
“You are offensive,” the bug replied, “because this page has a sword which I choose to say is not a sword. You are lewd because that page has a lance which I prefer to think is not a lance. You are lascivious because yonder page has a staff which I elect to declare is not a staff. And finally, you are indecent for reasons of which a description would be objectionable to me, and which therefore I must decline to reveal to anybody.”
“Well, that sounds logical,” says Jurgen, “but still, at the same time, it would be no worse for an admixture of commonsense. For you gentlemen can see for yourselves, by considering these pages fairly and as a whole, that these pages bear a sword and a lance and a staff, and nothing else whatever; and you will deduce, I hope, that all the lewdness is in the insectival mind of him who itches to be calling these things by other names.”
The judges said nothing as yet. But they that guarded Jurgen, and all the other Philistines, stood to this side and to that side with their eyes shut tight, and all these said: “We decline to look at the pages fairly and as a whole, because to look might seem to imply a doubt of what the tumblebug has decreed. Besides, as long as the tumblebug has reasons which he declines to reveal, his reasons stay unanswerable, and you are plainly a prurient rascal who are making trouble for yourself.”
“To the contrary,” says Jurgen, “I am a poet, and I make literature.”
“But in Philistia to make literature and to make trouble for yourself are synonyms,” the tumblebug explained. “I know, for already we of Philistia have been pestered by three of these makers of literature. Yes, there was Edgar, whom I starved and hunted until I was tired of it: then I chased him up a back alley one night, and knocked out those annoying brains of his.

待到法官们升堂齐备，准备断案之时，堂前走入一只巨大的屎壳郎（tumblebug），当先推着它那宝贝备至、妥为安置的一窝幼虫。伴随着这造物而来的是黑白相间的侍从（亦或是黑白印就的篇页），携着一柄剑、一根权杖与一杆长矛。

这甲虫看向朱尔根，一对螯钳因惊骇而直直竖立起来。甲虫冲着三位法官高声叫道：“奉圣安东尼之名！必须立时将这朱尔根贬入幽冥，因他冒犯失礼、淫邪下流、放荡不羁、伤风败俗！”

“这从何谈起？”朱尔根问道。

“你冒犯失礼，”甲虫答道，“因为这书页侍从佩着一柄剑，而我偏要说它不是剑。你淫邪下流，因为那书页侍从握着一杆长矛，而我宁可认为它不是长矛。你放荡不羁，因为那边的书页侍从执着一根权杖，而我执意断言它不是权杖。最后，你伤风败俗，其缘由令我作呕而不屑形诸笔墨，因而我拒绝向任何人透露。”

“唔，听来倒颇有逻辑，”朱尔根说，“不过若是能掺上几分常识，断不至于这般糟糕。诸位大人只需秉公通观全书全篇，便可亲眼得见，这些篇页侍从所携者，不过一剑一矛一杖，别无他物；我更指望诸位能推论得出，一切下流淫邪，全在乎那急不可耐要把这些物事唤作别样名目的虫豸心思里。”

法官们尚且不发一言。然而押解朱尔根的守卫，以及所有其余的非利士人，却分立左右两侧，紧紧闭着双眼，异口同声叫道：“我们拒绝秉公通观这些篇页，因为看上一眼，便仿佛是对屎壳郎大人的裁定心存疑窦。况且，既然屎壳郎大人拒绝吐露其缘由，他的缘由便不可辩驳；显而易见，你是个心术不正的无赖，纯粹自讨苦吃。”

“恰恰相反，”朱尔根说，“我是个诗人，我是在创作文学。”

“可在非利士地，创作文学与自讨苦吃乃是同义词，”屎壳郎解释道，“我再清楚不过，因为我们非利士人此前已被三个文学创作者搅得鸡犬不宁。不错，先是有个埃德加（Edgar），我把他逼得饥寒交迫、四处碰壁，直到我自个儿都厌烦了：终于有一夜，我把他逼进一条暗巷，彻底敲碎了他那令人厌烦的脑瓜。

And there was Walt, whom I chivvied and battered from place to place, and made a paralytic of him: and him, too, I labelled offensive and lewd and lascivious and indecent. Then later there was Mark, whom I frightened into disguising himself in a clown’s suit, so that nobody might suspect him to be a maker of literature: indeed, I frightened him so that he hid away the greater part of what he had made until after he was dead, and I could not get at him. Still, these are the only three detected makers of literature that have ever infested Philistia, thanks be to goodness and my vigilance, but for both of which we might have been no more free from makers of literature than are the other countries.”
“Now, but these three,” cried Jurgen, “are the glory of Philistia: and of all that Philistia has produced, it is these three alone, whom living ye made least of, that today are honored wherever art is honored, and where nobody bothers one way or the other about Philistia.”
“What is art to me and my way of living?” replied the tumblebug, wearily. “I have no concern with art and letters and the other lewd idols of foreign nations. I have in charge the moral welfare of my young, whom I roll here before me, and trust with St. Anthony’s aid to raise in time to be God-fearing tumblebugs like me, delighting in what is proper to their nature. For the rest, I have never minded dead men being well-spoken-of. No, no, my lad: once whatever I may do means nothing to you, and once you are really rotten, you will find the tumblebug friendly enough. Meanwhile I am paid to protest that living persons are offensive and lewd and lascivious and indecent, and one must live.”
Then the Philistines who stood to this side and to that side said in indignant unison: “And we, the reputable citizenry of Philistia, are not at all in sympathy with those who would take any protest against the tumblebug as a justification of what they are pleased to call art. The harm done by the tumblebug seems to us very slight, whereas the harm done by the self-styled artist may be very great.”
Jurgen now looked more attentively at this queer creature: and he saw that the tumblebug was malodorous, certainly, but at bottom honest and well-meaning; and this seemed to Jurgen the saddest thing he had found among the Philistines.

“随后又来了个沃尔特（Walt），我把他从一处追逐毒打到另一处，直打得他半身不遂：对他也一样，我给他扣上了冒犯失礼、淫邪下流、放荡不羁和伤风败俗的帽子。再往后又来了个马克（Mark），我吓得他换上小丑戏装掩人耳目，免得教人看出他是个文学创作者：说真的，我把他吓得将大半心血之作深藏起来，直到他死后、我都奈何他不得之时才肯公之于世。依我看，跟我耍这等花招实属卑劣下作。不过，托老天有眼和我老人家警惕之福，在非利士地被揪出来的文学创作者也就只有这三个；若非这两条，我们这儿恐怕也要像别的国度一样，让搞文学创作的人泛滥成灾了。”

“哎，可正是这三个人，”朱尔根大喊道，“才是非利士地的无上荣光！非利士地产出的万千物事之中，唯独这三位在世时被你们视若草芥的人，如今在一切尊崇艺术之地皆受敬仰，而在那些地方，根本无人会在意非利士地如何。”

“艺术于我和我的生活之道算得什么？”屎壳郎厌倦地答道，“我与艺术文章，乃至外国蛮邦那些下流的偶像毫无瓜葛。我肩负着身前滚着的一窝幼虫的道德福祉，且仰仗圣安东尼的保佑，假以时日把它们抚育成跟我一样敬畏上帝的屎壳郎，各安其分、怡然自得。至于死人嘛，我向来不在意别人夸他们好话。不，不，我的好小子：只要凡我所为之事再不能动你分毫，只要你当真烂透了，你便会发现屎壳郎相当友善。眼下，有人付工钱让我抗议活着的人冒犯失礼、淫邪下流、放荡不羁、伤风败俗，人总得糊口嘛。”

于是分立两侧的非利士人义愤填膺地齐声附和：“而我们，身为非利士地声誉卓著的良好市民，绝不同情任何胆敢借攻击屎壳郎大人来为他们所谓的艺术开脱之人。屎壳郎大人造成的损害在我们看来微乎其微，而自命为艺术家之徒造成的祸害却可能大如倾天。”

朱尔根此刻更为审慎地打量起这奇异的造物：他看清了，这屎壳郎周身恶臭固然不假，但骨子里却实诚得紧，且满怀善意；在朱尔根看来，这实乃他在非利士人当中所撞见的最为可悲之事。
