### On a Landscape of Nicolas Poussin / 论尼古拉·普桑的一幅风景画
===Original===

> “And blind Orion hungry for the morn.”

Orion, the subject of this landscape, was the classical Nimrod; and is called by Homer, “a hunter of shadows, himself a shade.” He was the son of Neptune; and having lost an eve in some affray between the Gods and men, was told that if he would go to meet the rising sun he would recover his sight. He is represented setting out on his journey, with men on his shoulders to guide him, a bow in his hand, and Diana in the clouds greeting him. He stalks along, a giant upon earth, and reels and falters in his gait, as if just awakened out of sleep, or uncertain of his way;﻿—you see his blindness, though his back is turned. Mists rise around him, and veil the sides of the green forests; earth is dank and fresh with dews, the “gray dawn and the Pleiades before him dance,” and in the distance are seen the blue hills and sullen ocean. Nothing was ever more finely conceived or done. It breathes the spirit of the morning; its moisture, its repose, its obscurity, waiting the miracle of light to kindle it into smiles; the whole is, like the principal figure in it, “a forerunner of the dawn.” The same atmosphere tinges and imbues every object, the same dull light “shadowy sets off” the face of nature: one feeling of vastness, of strangeness, and of primeval forms pervades the painter’s canvas, and we are thrown back upon the first integrity of things. This great and learned man might be said to see nature through the glass of time; he alone has a right to be considered as the painter of classical antiquity. Sir Joshua has done him justice in this respect. He could give to the scenery of his heroic fables that unimpaired look of original nature, full, solid, large, luxuriant, teeming with life and power; or deck it with all the pomp of art, with temples and towers, and mythologic groves. His pictures “denote a foregone conclusion.” He applies Nature to his purposes, works out her images according to the standard of his thoughts, embodies high fictions; and the first conception being given, all the rest seems to grow out of and be assimilated to it, by the unfailing process of a studious imagination. Like his own Orion, he overlooks the surrounding scene, appears to “take up the isles as a very little thing, and to lay the earth in a balance.”

> “失明的俄里翁渴慕着黎明。”

这幅风景画的题材俄里翁，就是古典传说中的宁录；荷马称他为“影子的猎手，自己也是个影子”。他是尼普顿之子；在神与人的一场混战中瞎了一只眼睛，有人告诉他，若去迎接东升的太阳，便可复明。画中他正启程上路，肩上坐着引路的人，手中持弓，云中的狄安娜向他致意。他迈着大步前行，是大地上的巨人，步态却踉跄趔趄，仿佛刚从睡梦中醒来，或辨认不清路径；——虽然画的是他的背影，你却看得出他的失明。雾霭在他四周升起，罩住翠绿森林的坡面；大地带着露水，湿冷而清新；“灰白的黎明和昴星团在他前方起舞”，远处可见青蓝的山峦与沉沉的海洋。再没有比这构思更精、完成更妙的了。画面吐纳着清晨的气息；它的湿润、它的静谧、它的朦胧，正等着光的奇迹把它点燃成微笑；整体同画中主角一样，是“黎明的先行者”。同一种气氛浸染、灌注着每一件物象，同一种晦暗的光“影影绰绰地”映衬自然的容颜：一种浩大、陌生、太古形态之感弥漫于画家的画布，我们被送回到万物初创的完整之中。这位伟大而博学的人，可以说是在透过时间的透镜看自然；唯有他，有资格被视为古典古代的画家。在这方面，雷诺兹爵士对他评价公允。他能给他英雄寓言中的景色披上未受损毁的原始自然之貌——饱满、坚实、宏阔、繁茂，洋溢着生命与力量；也能给它装点上艺术的一切华彩，庙宇、塔楼，与神话的圣林。他的画“显示的是既定的结局”。他把自然为我所用，按自己思想的标准锤炼自然的意象，赋高远的虚构以形体；既有了最初的概念，其余一切便经由勤勉的想象力那从不失误的过程，从中生长出来、与之同化。像他自己笔下的俄里翁，他俯瞰周遭景物，仿佛“把众岛当作极小之物拿起，把大地放在天平上”。

With a laborious and mighty grasp, he puts nature into the mould of the ideal and antique; and was among painters (more than anyone else) what Milton was among poets. There is in both something of the same pedantry, the same stiffness, the same elevation, the same grandeur, the same mixture of art and nature, the same richness of borrowed materials, the same unity of character. Neither the poet nor the painter lowered the subjects they treated, but filled up the outline in the fancy, and added strength and reality to it; and thus not only satisfied, but surpassed the expectations of the spectator and the reader. This is held for the triumph and the perfection of works of art. To give us nature, such as we see it, is well and deserving of praise; to give us nature, such as we have never seen, but have often wished to see it, is better, and deserving of higher praise. He who can show the world in its first naked glory, with the hues of fancy spread over it, or in its high and palmy state, with the gravity of history stamped on the proud monuments of vanished empire﻿—who, by his “so potent art,” can recall time past, transport us to distant places, and join the regions of imagination (a new conquest) to those of reality﻿—who shows us not only what Nature is, but what she has been, and is capable of﻿—he who does this, and does it with simplicity, with truth, and grandeur, is lord of Nature and her powers; and his mind is universal, and his art the master-art!
There is nothing in this “more than natural,” if criticism could be persuaded to think so. The historic painter does not neglect or contravene Nature, but follows her more closely up into her fantastic heights or hidden recesses. He demonstrates what she would be in conceivable circumstances and under implied conditions. He “gives to airy nothing a local habitation,” not “a name.” At his touch, words start up into images, thoughts become things. He clothes a dream, a phantom, with form and colour, and the wholesome attributes of reality. *His* art is a second nature; not a different one. There are those, indeed, who think that not to copy nature is the rule for attaining perfection. Because they cannot paint the objects which they have they have, they fancy themselves qualified to paint the ideas which they have not seen.

他以勤勉而伟力的把握，把自然纳入理想与古典的范型；他在画家中（比谁都更甚），正如弥尔顿在诗人中。两人身上都有几分同样的书卷气，同样的生硬，同样的高卓，同样的雄伟，同样的艺术与自然的交融，同样丰富的借用材料，同样统一的性格。诗人也好画家也好，都不曾贬低所处理的题材，而是在想象中填足那轮廓，为其添上力量与实在；这样不但满足了、而且超出了观者与读者的期待。这被视为艺术作品的凯旋与极致。给我们所见的那样的自然，是好的，值得称许；给我们从未见过、却常常愿意见到的那样的自然，则更好，值得更高的称许。谁能把世界呈现在它最初赤裸的荣光里，覆以幻想的色泽；或呈现在它鼎盛的全盛状态里，把历史的庄重钤印在消逝帝国的巍峨纪念碑上——谁能以他“如此神通的艺术”召回逝去的时光，把我们载往遥远的地方，把想象的疆域（一片新的征服）同现实的疆域连成一片——谁让我们看到的不仅是自然的现状，还有她的往昔与她的潜能——谁能做到这些，又做得单纯、真实而雄伟，谁就是自然及其伟力的主宰；他的心智便无所不包，他的艺术便是众艺之主！
这当中没有任何“超乎自然”的东西——倘若批评界肯这样想的话。历史画家并不忽视或违逆自然，而是更紧地追随她，登临她奇幻的高处，深入她隐秘的幽处。他演示出，在可设想的情境与暗含的条件之下，她将会是什么样子。他“给飘渺的虚无一个具体的居所”，而不止是“一个名目”。经他一点，词语跃起为形象，思想化作了实物。他给一场梦、一个幽灵，穿上形式与色彩，穿上现实那健全的属性。*他的*艺术是第二自然；不是另一种自然。诚然，也有人以为，不摹仿自然才是臻于完善的法则。因为他们画不出眼前的对象，便幻想自己有资格去画他们不曾见过的观念。

But it is possible to fail in this latter and more difficult style of imitation, as well as in the former humbler one. The detection, it is true, is not so easy, because the objects are not so nigh at hand to compare, and therefore there is more room both for false pretension and for self-deceit. They take an epic motto or subject, and conclude that the spirit is implied as a thing of course. They paint inferior portraits, maudlin lifeless faces, without ordinary expression, or one look, feature, or particle of nature in them, and think that this is to rise to the truth of history. They vulgarise and degrade whatever is interesting or sacred to the mind, and suppose that they thus add to the dignity of their profession. They represent a face that seems as if no thought or feeling of any kind had ever passed through it, and would have you believe that this is the very sublime of expression, such as it would appear in heroes, or demigods of old, when rapture or agony was raised to its height. They show you a landscape that looks as if the sun never shone upon it, and tell you that it is not modern﻿—that so earth looked when Titan first kissed it with his rays. This is not the true ideal. It is not to fill the moulds of the imagination, but to deface and injure them; it is not to come up to, but to fall short of the poorest conception in the public mind. Such pictures should not be hung in the same room with that of Orion.56
Poussin was, of all painters, the most poetical. He was the painter of ideas. No one ever told a story half so well, nor so well knew what was capable of being told by the pencil. He seized on, and struck off with grace and precision, just that point of view which would be likely to catch the reader’s fancy. There is a significance, a consciousness in whatever he does (sometimes a vice, but oftener a virtue) beyond any other painter. His Giants sitting on the tops of craggy mountains, as huge themselves, and playing idly on their Pan’s-pipes, seem to have been seated there these three thousand years, and to know the beginning and the end of their own story. An infant Bacchus or Jupiter is big with his future destiny. Even inanimate and dumb things speak a language of their own. His snakes, the messengers of fate, are inspired with human intellect.

然而，在后一种更难的摹仿里失手，同在前一种较低难的摹仿里失手，同样是可能的。诚然，察觉这失手不那么容易，因为可供比较的对象不在眼前，于是虚假的托辞与自我欺瞒便都有了更多的余地。他们取一个史诗式的题铭或题材，便断定精神自然不言而喻地包含在内。他们画些劣等的肖像、感伤而僵死的脸，连寻常的表情都没有，没有一丝神采、一点特征、一粒自然的分子，却以为这就上升到了历史的真实。他们把一切心灵的兴味之所寄、神圣之所系庸俗化、贬低，还以为这样是为自己行当的尊严添彩。他们画一张仿佛从未有任何思想或情感从上经过的脸，却要你相信这正是表情的极致崇高——古时英雄或半神在狂喜或苦痛升至顶点时所应有的样子。他们给你看一幅仿佛太阳从未照过的风景，并告诉你这不是现代的——说大地在泰坦初次以光线吻它时便是这般模样。这不是真的理想。这不是去充实想象的范型，而是玷污、损伤它们；不是够到、而是连公众心中最贫弱的构想都够不到。这样的画，不配同《俄里翁》挂在同一个房间里。56
在所有画家中，普桑最具诗意。他是观念的画家。谁讲故事也不及他的一半好，谁也不如他深知画笔所能讲述的极限。他恰好抓住并优雅而精确地敲定那个最有可能抓住读者想象的视角。他笔下的一切都有一种意味、一种自觉（有时是缺点，更多时候是美德），为其他任何画家所不及。他的巨人们坐在嶙峋的山巅，本身一样巨大，闲闲地吹着潘神之笛，仿佛在那儿坐了三千年，知道自己故事的开头和结尾。一个襁褓中的巴克斯或朱庇特，已怀着未来的命运。连无生命的哑物也说着自己的语言。他的群蛇，命运的信使，被赋予了人的心智。

His trees grow and expand their leaves in the air, glad of the rain, proud of the sun, awake to the winds of heaven. In his *Plague of Athens*, the very buildings seem stiff with horror. His picture of the Deluge is, perhaps, the finest historical landscape in the world. You see a waste of waters, wide, interminable: the sun is labouring, wan and weary, up the sky the clouds, dull and leaden, lie like a load upon the eye, and heaven and earth seem commingling into one confused mass! His human figures are sometimes “o’erinformed” with this kind of feeling. Their actions have too much gesticulation, and the set expression of the features borders too much on the mechanical and caricatured style. In this respect they form a contrast to Raphael’s, whose figures never appear to be sitting for their pictures, or to be conscious of a spectator, or to have come from the painter’s hand. In Nicolas Poussin, on the contrary, everything seems to have a distinct understanding with the artist; “the very stones prate of their whereabout”; each object has its part and place assigned, and is in a sort of compact with the rest of the picture. It is this conscious keeping, and, as it were, *internal* design, that gives their peculiar character to the works of this artist. There was a picture of Aurora in the British Gallery a year or two ago. It was a suffusion of golden light. The Goddess wore her saffron-coloured robes, and appeared just risen from the gloomy bed of old Tithonus. Her very steeds, milk-white, were tinged with the yellow dawn. It was a personification of the morning. Poussin succeeded better in classic than in sacred subjects. The latter are comparatively heavy, forced, full of violent contrasts of colour, of red, blue, and black, and without the true prophetic inspiration of the characters. But in his pagan allegories and fables he was quite at home. The native gravity and native levity of the Frenchman were combined with Italian scenery and an antique gusto, and gave even to his colouring an air of learned indifference. He wants, in one respect, grace, form, expression; but he has everywhere sense and meaning, perfect costume and propriety. His personages always belong to the class and time represented, and are strictly versed in the business in hand.

他的树在空气中生长舒展，欣然于雨，傲然于日，醒然于天风。在他的《雅典瘟疫》（*Plague of Athens*）里，连房屋都仿佛吓僵了。他那幅《洪水》，也许是世上最好的历史风景画。你看到一片汪洋，浩渺无际：太阳惨白倦怠，吃力地爬上天穹；云沉滞如铅，像重负压在眼上；天与地仿佛正汇作混沌一团！
他的人物有时被这种情感“灌得太满”。动作手势太多，五官那种摆定的表情又太近乎机械与漫画的格调。在这方面他们同拉斐尔的人物恰成对照：拉斐尔的人物从不像是坐着供人写生，从不意识到有观者，也从不像是出自画家之手。而在尼古拉·普桑那里，一切仿佛都同艺术家有明确的默契；“连石头也唠叨着自己的来历”；每个对象都分派有自己的角色与位置，同画面的其余部分立有一种契约。正是这种自觉的呼应、这种仿佛发自*内里*的设计，赋予了这位艺术家作品独特的性格。
一两年前，英国美术馆有一幅《奥罗拉》。那是一片金色光的弥漫。女神身披藏红花色的长袍，仿佛刚从老提托诺斯幽暗的床榻上起身。连她乳白的马匹，也染上了金黄的曙色。那是清晨的化身。
普桑画古典题材胜过圣教题材。后者相比之下滞重、牵强，满是红、蓝、黑的激烈色彩对照，人物也缺乏真正的先知灵感。但在异教的寓言与传说里，他如鱼得水。法国人与生俱来的庄重与天生的轻快，同意大利的景色、古典的趣味结合起来，甚至给他的着色也带上一种博学的淡然。在某一方面，他缺少优雅、形式、表情；但他处处有理致与意涵，服饰与分寸无可挑剔。他的人物总属于所表现的阶层与时代，对眼下的事务谙熟于心。

but those of Poussin have more of the intellectual part of the character, and seem vicious on reflection, and of set purpose. Rubens’ are noble specimens of a class; Poussin’s are allegorical abstractions of the same class, with bodies less pampered, but with minds more secretly depraved. Pictures are a set of chosen images, a stream of pleasant thoughts passing through the mind. 而普桑笔下的人物则更多性格中的心智一面，其邪恶仿佛出于深思，出于有意为之。鲁本斯的是一类的精美标本；普桑的则是同类的寓意抽象，肉体不那么养尊处优，心智却更幽暗地堕落。不过，那位佛兰德画家的酒神群像，终究是他构图上的绝品。布伦海姆那几件色彩、性格与表情的奇观可以为证。而在更纯净、更精雅地描绘古典传说上，普桑无与伦比。鲁本斯在粗犷与画意上足与他匹敌，却不敢妄称比得上他《阿波罗赐诗人饮水》一画中构思的典雅与纯净，也比不上那幅仙女造像的设计之美：她从指尖挤出满满一串葡萄的汁液（一座玫瑰色的酒榨），落进下方一个胖乎乎婴孩的口中。而最重要的，谁又能用相称的赞词来歌颂他那幅画：滕比谷的牧人们在一个晴好的春日清晨出游，来到一座墓前，墓上刻着——**et ego in arcadia vixi**（我也曾在阿卡迪亚生活）！有人一脸急切的好奇，有人惶然惊退、满面骇怪，清风吹动着浓荫的枝柯，“低谷之中，和风常至”，远处阳光朗照的原野一望无际——这一切向后世的人们诉说着（并将永远诉说着）往古的时代！57
绘画是一组精选的意象，是一股流过心田的愉快思绪。把房间的四壁挂满绘画，是一种享受；在心里保有这样一座画廊，把古代艺术的遗珍“装订在大脑的书册与卷帙里，（但愿）不掺半点卑杂质料”，反复披览，也同样是享受！在绘画中、在对艺术的研习与热爱中度过的一生，是一场幸福而无喧的梦：或者不如说，是同时做梦并同时醒着；因为它既有“清醒之福的那份沉稳的确凿”，又有幻想的、出神的存在者那份浪漫的陶醉。它们是万物光明而圆满的精华，“懂得品味这些乐趣、并常常以之点缀人生的人，并非不明智！”

﻿—The *Orion*, which I have here taken occasion to descant upon, is one of a collection of excellent pictures, as this collection is itself one of a series from the old masters, which have for some years back embrowned the walls of the British Gallery, and enriched the public eye. What hues (those of nature mellowed by time) breathe around as we enter! What forms are there, woven into the memory! What looks, which only the answering looks of the spectator can express! What intellectual stores have been yearly poured forth from the shrine of ancient art! The works are various, but the names the same﻿—heaps of Rembrandts frowning from the darkened walls, Rubens’ glad gorgeous groups, Titians more rich and rare, Claudes always exquisite, sometimes beyond compare, Guido’s endless cloying sweetness, the learning of Poussin and the Caracci, and Raphael’s princely magnificence crowning all. We read certain letters and syllables in the Catalogue, and at the well-known magic sound a miracle of skill and beauty starts to view. One might think that one year’s prodigal display of such perfection would exhaust the labours of one man’s life; but the next year, and the next to that, we find another harvest reaped and gathered in to the great garner of art, by the same immortal hands﻿—

——我在此借题发挥的这幅《俄里翁》（*Orion*），是一批精彩藏品中的一件；而这批藏品，又是历年陈列在英国美术馆墙上、饱饫公众眼福的古代大师系列藏品中的一部分。一走进去，什么样的色调（那是被时间酿熟了的自然之色）在四周吐纳！什么样的形式，织进了记忆！什么样的神情，唯有观者应答的目光才能表达！什么样的心智珍藏，年年从古代艺术的圣殿倾泻而出！作品各异，名号不改——成堆的伦勃朗在暗沉沉的墙上蹙眉，鲁本斯欢快绚烂的群像，更浓郁更珍罕的提香，克劳德永远精美、有时无与伦比，圭多那没完没了的甜腻，普桑与卡拉齐的学养，而拉斐尔的王者气派冠冕一切。我们在《目录》里读到某几个字母与音节，在这熟稔的魔法之声里，一桩技艺与美的奇迹跃入眼帘。人们也许以为，如此完美之作如此铺张地展出一年，便足以穷尽一个人毕生的劳作；可到了来年，再到来年，我们发现同一双不朽之手又收获了另一茬庄稼，归入艺术的大仓廪——

他放它们进来，又放它们离去。——”

===Original===

Their works seem endless as their reputation﻿—to be many as they are complete﻿—to multiply with the desire of the mind to see more and more of them; as if there were a living power in the breath of Fame, and in the very names of the great heirs of glory “there were propagation to!” It is something to have a collection of this sort to count upon once a year; to have one last, lingering look yet to come. Pictures are scattered like stray gifts through the world; and while they remain, earth has yet a little gilding left, not quite rubbed off, dishonoured, and defaced. There are plenty of standard works still to be found in this country, in the collections at Blenheim, at Burleigh, and in those belonging to Mr. Angerstein, Lord Grosvenor, the Marquis of Stafford, and others, to keep up this treat to the lovers of art for many years; and it is the more desirable to reserve a privileged sanctuary of this sort, where the eye may dote, and the heart take its fill of such pictures as Poussin’s *Orion*, since the Louvre is stripped of its triumphant spoils, and since he who collected it, and wore it as a rich jewel in his Iron Crown, the hunter of greatness and of glory, is himself a shade!

他们的作品同他们的名声一样仿佛无穷无尽——既完整又繁夥——随着心灵想多看、再多看的欲望而成倍增长；仿佛名望的吐纳中自有一股生命之力，仿佛荣耀的伟大承继者们，单是名号本身“便自能繁衍生息”！
每年能指望看到这样一批藏品，是件快事；还剩最后一眼流连的机会，也是件快事。绘画像零散的赠礼撒遍世界；只要它们还在，大地上就还留着一层薄薄的金色，尚未磨尽、尚未蒙羞、尚未毁损。本国还有大量典范之作可寻，在布伦海姆、在伯利的收藏里，在安格斯坦先生、格罗夫纳勋爵、斯塔福德侯爵等人的收藏里，足以把这份眼福维持许多年；正因卢浮宫已褪尽它胜利的战利品，正因那位把它汇集起来、把它当一颗富丽的宝石镶在自己铁王冠上的人——那位追逐伟大与荣耀的猎手——自己已然是一个影子，保留这样一座特权的圣所，让眼睛得以痴赏、让心得以饱饫普桑《俄里翁》这样的画，就越发可欲了！
