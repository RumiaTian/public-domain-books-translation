## On the Picturesque and Ideal (A Fragment) / 论画意与理想（残篇）
===Original===

The natural in visible objects is whatever is ordinarily presented to the senses: the picturesque is that which stands out and catches the attention by some striking peculiarity: the *ideal* is that which answers to the preconceived imagination and appetite in the mind for love and beauty. The picturesque depends chiefly on the principle of discrimination or contrast; the *ideal* on harmony and continuity of effect: the one surprises, the other satisfies the mind; the one starts off from a given point, the other reposes on itself; the one is determined by an excess of form, the other by a concentration of feeling.
The picturesque may be considered as something like an excrescence on the face of nature. It runs imperceptibly into the fantastical and grotesque. Fairies and satyrs are picturesque; but they are scarcely *ideal*. They are an extreme and unique conception of a certain thing, but not of what the mind delights in or broods fondly over. The image created by the artist’s hand is not moulded and fashioned by the love of good and yearning after grace and beauty, but rather the contrary: that is they are ideal deformity, not ideal beauty. Rubens was perhaps the most picturesque of painters; but he was almost the least *ideal*. So Rembrandt was (out of sight) the most picturesque of colourists; as Correggio was the most *ideal*. In other words, his composition of light and shade is more a whole, more in unison, more blended into the same harmonious feeling than Rembrandt’s, who staggers by contrast, but does not soothe by gradation. Correggio’s forms, indeed, had a picturesque air; for they often incline (even when most beautiful) to the quaintness of caricature. Van Dyke, I think, was at once the least picturesque and least *ideal* of all the great painters. He was purely natural, and neither selected from outward forms nor added anything from his own mind. He owes everything to perfect truth, clearness, and transparency; and though his productions certainly arrest the eye, and strike in a room full of pictures, it is from the contrast they present to other pictures, and from being stripped quite naked of all artificial advantages.

===Chinese===

可见事物中的自然物，是感官寻常所见的一切：入画之物（画意）是以某种触目的特异之点凸现出来、抓住注意的东西：*理想*之物则与心灵中对爱与美的先在想象与渴望相应。画意主要系于区分或对照的原理；理想之物则系于效果的和谐与连绵：一个使人惊讶，一个使人心满；一个从给定的起点出发，一个安歇于自身；一个由形式之过度决定，一个由情感之凝聚决定。
画意不妨看作自然面容上某种近乎赘生物的东西。它不知不觉便滑向奇幻与怪诞。仙女与山神是入画的；却算不上*理想*。它们是对某一事物的极端而独特的构想，却不是对心灵所喜、所眷恋冥想者的构想。艺术家手造的形象，不是被对善的爱、对优雅与美的渴慕塑造成形的，倒是恰恰相反：也就是说，它们是理想的变形，不是理想的美。鲁本斯（Rubens）也许是画家中最富画意的；却几乎是最不*理想*的。同样，伦勃朗是（远超同侪地）最富画意的色彩家；正如柯勒乔（Correggio）是最*理想*的。换句话说，柯勒乔的光影构成比伦勃朗的更完整、更浑然、更融汇于同一种和谐之感；伦勃朗以对照惊人，却不以渐层抚慰人。柯勒乔的形式固然带几分画意；因为它们（即便最美的时候）也常倾向于漫画式的奇巧。而凡·戴克（Van Dyke），我想，是大画家中既最乏画意、又最乏*理想*的一个。他纯粹是自然的，既不对外在形式有所拣选，也不从自己的心灵里添加分毫。他的一切都归功于圆满的真实、清晰与透明；他的作品固然抓人眼目，在满室画作中脱颖而出，但那靠的是它们同其他画作形成的对照，靠的是剥尽了一切人工的装点、赤裸裸的呈现。

===Original===

They strike almost as a piece of white paper would, hung up in the same situation﻿—I began with saying that whatever stands out from a given line, and as it were projects upon the eye, is picturesque; and this holds true (comparatively) in form and colour. A rough terrier dog, with the hair bristled and matted together, is picturesque. As we say, there is a decided character in it, a marked determination to an extreme point. A shock-dog is odd and disagreeable, but there is nothing picturesque in its appearance; it is a mere mass of flimsy confusion. A goat with projecting horns and pendent beard is a picturesque animal; a sheep is not. A horse is only picturesque from opposition of colour; as in Mr. Northcote’s study of Gadshill, where the white horse’s head coming against the dark, scowling face of the man makes as fine a contrast as can be imagined. An old stump of a tree with rugged bark, and one or two straggling branches, a little stunted hedgerow line, marking the boundary of the horizon, a stubble-field, a winding path, a rock seen against the sky, are picturesque, because they have all of them prominence and a distinctive character of their own. They are not objects (to borrow Shakespeare’s phrase) “of no mark or likelihood.” A country may be beautiful, romantic, or sublime, without being picturesque. The Lakes in the North of England are not picturesque, though certainly the most interesting sight in this country. To be a subject for painting, a prospect must present sharp, striking points of view or singular forms, or one object must relieve and set off another. There must be distinct stages and salient points for the eye to rest upon or start from in its progress over the expanse before it. The distance of a landscape will oftentimes look flat or heavy, that the trunk of a tree or a ruin in the foreground would immediately throw into perspective and turn to air. Rembrandt’s landscapes are the least picturesque in the world, except from the straight lines and sharp angles, the deep incision and dragging of his pencil, like a harrow over the ground, and the broad contrast of earth and sky. Earth, in his copies, is rough and hairy; and Pan has struck his hoof against it!﻿—A camel is a picturesque ornament in a landscape or history-piece.

它们的夺目，几乎就像在同一位置挂出的一张白纸——我开头说过：凡是从给定的线条中凸出出来、仿佛向眼睛投来的东西，便是入画的；这在形式与色彩上（相对地）都成立。一只毛色粗硬、鬃毛乍起纠结的㹴犬是入画的。用我们的话说，它身上有一种决然的性格，一种推向极致的鲜明倾向。一只卷毛犬古怪而不讨喜，外貌却毫无画意；它只是一团轻飘飘的杂乱。双角前耸、长须下垂的山羊是入画的动物；绵羊不是。马只有借色彩的对照才入画；就像诺思科特先生（Mr. Northcote）的盖兹山习作，白马的头衬着那张黝黑阴沉的人脸，构成想象得到的最美的对照。一株树皮粗皱、残枝一二的老树桩，一线标记地平线边界的矮瘦篱墙，一片收割后的茬地，一条蜿蜒小径，一块以天空为背景的岩石，都是入画的，因为它们个个突兀而自有其独特的性格。它们不是（借用莎士比亚（Shakespeare）的话）“全无标记、全无特征”的东西。一片乡野可以美、可以浪漫、可以崇高，而不入画。英格兰北部的湖区便不入画，尽管它确是本国最耐观的景致。要成为画材，一片景致必须呈现锐利触目的视点或奇特的形态，或一物必须烘托、映衬另一物。眼睛在扫过眼前的旷景时，必须有分明的层次与凸出的停靠点或出发点。风景的远景常常显得平板或沉闷，而前景里一段树干或一处废墟，立时便会使它有了纵深、化实为虚。伦勃朗的风景画是天底下最不讲究画意的——除了那些直线与锐角、画笔如耙地般深刻的切入与拖曳、以及大地与天空的大幅对照。在他的笔下，大地粗砺而多毛；潘神的蹄子正踏在上面！——骆驼在风景画或历史画里是一件入画的点缀。

This is not merely from its romantic and oriental character; for an elephant has not the same effect, and if introduced as a necessary appendage, is also an unwieldy incumbrance. A negro’s head in a group is picturesque from contrast; so are the spots on a panther’s hide. This was the principle that Paul Veronese went upon, who said the rule for composition was *black upon white, and white upon black*. He was a pretty good judge. His celebrated picture of the Marriage of Cana is in all likelihood the completest piece of workmanship extant in the art. When I saw it, it nearly covered one side of a large room in the Louvre (being itself forty feet by twenty)﻿—and it seemed as if that side of the apartment was thrown open, and you looked out at the open sky, at buildings, marble pillars, galleries with people in them, emperors, female slaves, Turks, negroes, musicians, all the famous painters of the time, the tables loaded with viands, goblets, and dogs under them﻿—a sparkling, overwhelming confusion, a bright, unexpected reality﻿—the only fault you could find was that no miracle was going on in the faces of the spectators: the only miracle there was the picture itself! A French gentleman, who showed me this “triumph of painting” (as it has been called), perceiving I was struck with it, observed, “My wife admires it exceedingly for the facility of the execution.” I took this proof of sympathy for a compliment. It is said that when Humboldt, the celebrated traveller and naturalist, was introduced to Bonaparte, the Emperor addressed him in these words﻿—“*Vous aimez la botanique, Monsieur*”; and on the other’s replying in the affirmative, added, “*Et ma femme aussi!*” This has been found fault with as a piece of brutality and insolence in the great man by bigoted critics, who do not know what a thing it is to get a Frenchwoman to agree with them in any point. For my part, I took the observation as it was meant, and it did not put me out of conceit with myself or the picture that Madame M﻿—﻿— liked it as well as *Monsieur l’Anglois*. Certainly, there could be no harm in that. By the side of it happened to be hung two allegorical pictures of Rubens (and in such matters he too was “no baby”91)﻿—I don’t remember what the figures were, but the texture seemed of wool or cotton.

这不只因为它浪漫的东方性格；大象便没有同样的效果，倘若作为必不可少的附件入画，反倒是一件笨重的累赘。群像中一颗黑人的头因对照而入画；豹皮上的斑点亦然。保罗·韦罗内塞奉行的正是这条原理，他说构图的法则就是*黑上施白，白上施黑*。他是很高明的裁判。他那幅名作《迦拿的婚宴》，很可能是这门艺术现存最完备的一件工艺。我见到它时，它几乎盖满卢浮宫一个大房间的一整面墙（画身长四十英尺、宽二十英尺）——仿佛那一面墙整个打开了，你望出去便是露天的天空、楼宇、大理石柱、廊中的人群、皇帝、女奴、土耳其人、黑人、乐师、当时所有著名的画家、堆满肴馔与酒盏的桌案、桌下穿行的狗——一片璀璨夺目、令人应接不暇的纷纭，一个明亮而猝不及防的真实——你唯一能挑剔的，是观画者脸上并未显出神迹正在发生的神情：在那里，唯一的神迹就是画本身！一位领我看这幅“绘画的凯旋”（有人这样称它）的法国绅士见我看得出神，便说：“我妻子极为欣赏它，欣赏它笔致的娴熟轻捷。”我把这番同情的表示当作了恭维。据说，著名的旅行家兼博物学家洪堡（Humboldt）被引见给波拿巴时，皇帝对他说的第一句话是——“*先生，您喜欢植物学*”；对方答了声是，他又补上一句：“*我夫人也喜欢*！”褊狭的批评家们曾就此指责这位大人物的粗鲁无礼——他们不知道，要让一位法国太太在任何一个问题上附和你，是何等难得的事。至于我，我把这话照它的本意领受了；M 夫人既同*英国先生*一样喜欢这幅画，并不曾叫我对这幅画、或对我自己少了兴味。这自然毫无坏处。画旁恰巧挂着鲁本斯的两幅寓意画（在这类事情上他也绝不是“雏儿”91）——画的是什么人物我已不记得，只觉得那肌理像是羊毛或棉絮。

The texture of the Paul Veronese was not wool or cotton, but stuff, jewels, flesh, marble, air, whatever composed the essence of the varied subjects, in endless relief and truth of handling. If the Fleming had seen his two allegories hanging where they did, he would, without a question, have wished them far enough.
I imagine that Rubens’s landscapes are picturesque: Claude’s are *ideal*. Rubens is always in extremes; Claude in the middle. Rubens carries some one peculiar quality or feature of nature to the utmost verge of probability: Claude balances and harmonises different forms and masses with laboured delicacy, so that nothing falls short, no one thing overpowers another. Rainbows, showers, partial gleams of sunshine, moonlight, are the means with which Rubens produces his most gorgeous and enchanting effects: there are neither rainbows, nor showers, nor sudden bursts of sunshine, nor glittering moonbeams in Claude. He is all softness and proportion: the other is all spirit and brilliant excess. The two sides (for example) of one of Claude’s landscapes balance one another, as in a scale of beauty: in Rubens the several objects are grouped and thrown together with capricious wantonness. Claude has more repose: Rubens more gaiety and extravagance. And here it might be asked, Is a rainbow a picturesque or an *ideal* object? It seems to me to be both. It is an accident in nature; but it is an inmate of the fancy. It startles and surprises the sense, but it soothes and tranquillises the spirit. It makes the eye glisten to behold it, but the mind turns to it long after it has faded from its place in the sky. It has both properties, then, of giving an extraordinary impulse to the mind by the singularity of its appearance, and of riveting the imagination by its intense beauty. I may just notice here in passing, that I think the effect of moonlight is treated in an *ideal* manner in the well-known line in Shakespeare﻿—

> “See how the moonlight *sleeps* upon yon bank.”

保罗·韦罗内塞那幅画的肌理可不是羊毛或棉絮，而是绸缎、珠宝、血肉、大理石、空气，是构成多样题材之本质的一切，在无穷的凹凸起伏与真实的手法之中。那位佛兰德人倘若见到自己那两幅寓意画挂在那里，毫无疑问会巴不得它们挂得越远越好。
我推想鲁本斯的风景画是画意的：克劳德（Claude）的是*理想*的。鲁本斯总在两极；克劳德居中。鲁本斯把自然的某一独特品性或面貌推到可能性的极边：克劳德则以勤勉的精微权衡、调和各种形式与块面，使一切恰如其分，无一物压倒另一物。彩虹、阵雨、局部的日光、月色，是鲁本斯借以造成最华丽醉人之效果的手段：克劳德的画里既没有彩虹，也没有阵雨，既没有骤泻的阳光，也没有闪亮的月华。他全是柔婉与匀称：另一位全是气性与辉煌的过度。比如，克劳德一幅风景的两侧彼此平衡，如在天平之上：在鲁本斯那里，诸物却任性而恣肆地聚拢、抛掷在一起。克劳德更多安憩：鲁本斯更多欢快与奔放。
这里或许要问：彩虹是画意的对象，还是*理想*的对象？在我看来，它兼而有之。它是自然中的一桩偶然；却是想象中的一位住客。它惊动、震诧感官，却抚慰、宁定性灵。望见它，眼睛会放光；而当它从天际消隐许久之后，心思仍转向它。可见它兼有两种性质：以其面貌之奇拔给心灵非同寻常的冲击，以其美之浓烈把想象力牢牢钉住。这里不妨顺便一提：莎士比亚有一名句，我以为月色的效果正是以*理想*之笔处理的——

> “看月光怎样*睡*在那边堤岸上。”

The image is heightened by the exquisiteness of the expression beyond its natural beauty, and it seems as if there could be no end to the delight taken in it.﻿—A number of sheep coming to a pool of water to drink, with shady trees in the background, the rest of the flock following them, and the shepherd and his dog left carelessly behind, is surely the *ideal* in landscape-composition, if the *ideal* has its source in the interest excited by a subject, in its power of drawing the affections after it linked in a golden chain, and in the desire of the mind to dwell on it forever. The *ideal*, in a word, is the height of the pleasing, that which satisfies and accords with the inmost longing of the soul: the picturesque is merely a sharper and bolder impression of reality. A morning mist drawing a slender veil over all objects is at once picturesque and *ideal*; for it in the first place excites immediate surprise and admiration, and in the next a wish for it to continue, and a fear lest it should be too soon dissipated. Is the Cupid riding on a lion in the ceiling at Whitehall, and urging him with a spear over a precipice, with only clouds and sky beyond, most picturesque or *ideal*? It has every effect of startling contrast and situation, and yet inspires breathless expectation and wonder for the event. Rembrandt’s *Jacob’s Dream*, again, is both fearful to the eye, but realising that loftiest vision of the soul. Take two faces in Leonardo da Vinci’s *Last Supper*, the Judas and the St. John: the one is all strength, repulsive character; the other is all divine grace and mild sensibility. The individual, the characteristic in painting, is that *which is* in a marked manner﻿—the *ideal* is that which we wish anything to be, and to contemplate without measure and without end. The first is truth, the last is good. The one appeals to the sense and understanding, the other to the will and the affections. The truly beautiful and grand attracts the mind to it by instinctive harmony, is absorbed in it, and nothing can ever part them afterwards. Look at a Madonna of Raphael’s: what gives the *ideal* character to the expression﻿—the insatiable purpose of the soul, or its measureless content in the object of its contemplation?

这一意象因措语之精妙而超出其自然的美，仿佛对它的赏玩永无止境。——一群羊来到水潭边饮水，背景是成荫的树，余下的羊群尾随而来，牧人与他的狗被漫不经心地落在后头：倘若*理想*源于题材所唤起的兴味、源于它把深情如金链般牵在身后、随之前行的力量、源于心灵愿永远栖止其上的渴望，那这无疑是风景构成中的*理想*。
一言以蔽之，*理想*是愉悦之极致，是满足并契合灵魂最深处渴望的东西：画意不过是现实更锐利、更大胆的印象。晨雾给万物罩上一层薄纱，便既是画意的又是*理想*的；因为它首先激起即时的惊异与赞叹，继而又唤起愿它留驻的心愿、以及唯恐它消散太快的忧惧。白厅天顶上那幅丘比特骑狮、以矛驱它越过悬崖、崖外唯有云天的画，究竟是画意居多，还是*理想*居多？它具备惊人的对照与位置的一切效果，却又令人屏息地期待着、惊叹着那结局。再如伦勃朗的《雅各之梦》（*Jacob's Dream*），既令观者心悸，又实现了灵魂那最高的异象。且取列奥纳多·达·芬奇（Leonardo da Vinci）《最后的晚餐》（*Last Supper*）中的两张脸，犹大与圣约翰：一张全是力量与可憎的性格；另一张全是神圣的恩慈与温婉的敏感。绘画中的个别的、特征的东西，是那种醒目地*实然*的东西——*理想*的东西则是我们愿万物皆然、愿无尽无休地静观的东西。前者是真实，后者是善。一个诉诸感官与理智，另一个诉诸意志与情感。真正美而伟大的东西，以本能的和谐把心灵引向自己，心灵沉浸其中，从此没有什么能把它们分开。看一看拉斐尔的一幅圣母像：是什么赋予了那表情以*理想*的性格——是灵魂不知餍足的企求，还是它在观照对象中那无量的安宁自足？

A portrait of Van Dyke’s is mere indifference and still-life in the comparison: it has not in it the principle of growing and still unsatisfied desire. In the *ideal* there is no fixed stint or limit but the limit of possibility: it is the infinite with respect to human capacities and wishes. Love is for this reason an *ideal* passion. We give to it our all of hope, of fear, of present enjoyment, and stake our last chance of happiness wilfully and desperately upon it. A good authority puts into the mouth of one of his heroines﻿—

> “My bounty is as boundless as the sea,

My love as deep!”

How many fair catechumens will there be found in all ages to repeat as much after Shakespeare’s Juliet!

相比之下，凡·戴克的一幅肖像便只是淡漠的静物了：它里面没有那不断生长、永不餍足的渴望的原理。在*理想*之中，除可能性的界限之外别无定限：就人的能力与愿望而言，它就是无限。正因如此，爱是一种*理想*的激情。我们把自己的全部希望、恐惧与眼前的欢愉都交付给它，任性地、孤注一掷地把幸福的最后机会押在它上面。一位高明的权威让他笔下的女主人公说——

> “我的慷慨像海一样无边，

我的爱像海一样深！”
